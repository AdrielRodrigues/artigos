Abstract—Fifth generation (5G) mobile networks will utilize network slicing to support a diverse range of services, each with distinct specifications for data rate, latency, and reliability. These services are broadly classified into 3 main categories: ultrareliable low-latency communication (URLLC), enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), and massive machine type communication (mMTC). Network slicing efficiently provisions these services by creating multiple virtual networks over the same physical infrastructure using network function virtualization (NFV). In this paper, we propose a traffic-aware network slicing scheme by levaraging a 3-layer cloud fog radio access network (RAN) (CF-RAN) over wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) architecture. This network architecture consists of remote radio heads (RRHs) at the first layer, fog nodes at the second layer, and baseband unit (BBU) hotels at the third layer. The fronthaul for incoming URLLC requests are served at the fog nodes because of their stringent latency requirements, while the fronthaul for eMBB and mMTC requests is handled at BBU hotels. The proposed architecture dynamically adapts to the random traffic generated by each RRH. Furthermore, we propose a heuristic algorithm to investigate how changes in the service request ratio effects the overall performance of the proposed architecture. The algorithm minimizes resource utilization while maintaining maximum network connectivity. The results demonstrate the impact of incoming slice request variation on the number of active nodes, number of served requests, and aggregated fronthaul

#### I. INTRODUCTION

THE goal of 5G and beyond networks is to provide unprecedented performance for a variety of services, including autonomous driving, remote surgery, 8K video streaming, etc. These services have been categorized as URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC [1], and they place heterogeneous data-rate, latency, and reliability demands on the networks [2]. URLLC includes applications with stringent end-to-end (E2E) latency ( $\leq 1$ ms) and reliability ( $\approx 99.99\%$ ) requirements, including intelligent transportation, industrial automation, and remote surgery. The eMBB services are more bandwidth-intensive,

such as 8K video streaming, and augmented/virtual reality services. The mMTC services permit massive connectivity of internet of things (IoT) devices at relatively low data-rates. Network slicing in 5G networks incorporates all these services with divergent requirements by establishing multiple virtual networks over the same physical network using NFV [3], [4]. Network slicing is a promising technique for handling rapidly expanding applications with specific requirements.

In recent years, many researchers have worked on implementing network slicing in cloud RAN (C-RAN) architecture [5]. In C-RAN the BBU hotel are separated from the RRH and are consolidated at a common location where they can serve multiple RRHs [6]. Even though the centralization of BBUs in C-RAN promises efficient resource allocation and cost savings [7], [8], it cannot effectively support services with diverse requirements due to scalability issues. Consequently, the most recent research has focused on network slicing in 3-layer radio unit (RU)-distributed unit (DU)-central unit (CU) architecture, in which baseband processing functions are further divided between DU and CU [9]. Since the DU and CU functions are handled by central offices (COs), adapting to variations in incoming traffic becomes challenging. This limits the benefits of this architecture over the traditional 2-layer C-RAN architecture [10].

To address the challenge due to random behavior of incoming slice requests, we propose a 3-layer CF-RAN over WDM architecture consisting of RRH at layer 1, fog nodes at layer 2, and BBU hotels at layer 3. Fog nodes are positioned closer to the RRHs at cell sites (CSs) and are dedicated to serving delaysensitive URLLC requests. Unlike RU-DU-CU architecture, entire URLLC requests are processed at the fog nodes to meet their strict latency and reliability requirements. Moreover, the eMBB and mMTC requests are processed at the BBU hotels. The traffic between RRHs and fog node/BBU hotels constitutes the fronthaul traffic carried by enhanced common public radio interface (CPRI) (eCPRI) using split option 7.2 as opposed to split option 8 in C-RAN that has huge data

# *A. Literature Review*

In [11], the authors discussed the motivation and challenges associated with 5G network slicing implementation. In [12], an algorithm is proposed to support a large number of IoT devices and rapidly increasing user demands in C-RAN. Domenico *et al.* [13] investigated the virtual network functions (VNF) placement and allocation of computational resources while deploying hybrid cloud C-RAN architecture. Furthermore, [14] formulates a mathematical model to minimize E2E energy consumption in virtualized C-RAN. The authors in [15] propose an optical-wireless integrated fronthaul concept to support eMBB and mMTC services while minimizing overall cost. The authors in [16] presented a heuristic algorithm to enable isolation among diverse network slices in the 3 layer RU-DU-CU architecture. Song *et al.* [17] provided an hierarchical edge cloud based architecture and analyzed the E2E delay and throughput for URLLC, eMBB and mMTC services. In [18], an integer linear programming based model is formulated that minimizes cost through optimal placement of RUs in optical RAN.

#### *B. Motivation & Contributions*

According to the literature review, the majority of research work focuses on efficient resource allocation and cost savings while deploying C-RAN and RU-DU-CU architectures. To the best of our knowledge, no published work has explored the impact of random traffic variations for different network slicing while deploying CF-RAN over WDM architecture. The main contributions of this article are as follows:

- We investigate the impact of random behavior of incoming URLLC, eMBB and mMTC traffic on the overall performance of the network.
- We present a heuristic algorithm for evaluating the impact of traffic variation on CF-RAN over WDM [19]. The objective of the algorithm is to serves maximum number of incoming requests by activating minimum number of fog nodes/BBU hotels while considering the constraints for fronthaul latency and link capacity. Furthermore, the algorithm performs grooming routing and wavelength assignment (GRWA) for all three types of incoming requests.

## II. NETWORK ARCHITECTURE

Our 3-layer CF-RAN over WDM architecture is shown in Fig. 1. The nodes are arranged in 3 layers with CSs at layer 1, fog nodes at layer 2, BBU hotels at layer 3. On top of it is the core CO. All nodes are interconnected through optical fiber links. Each fiber comprises multiple wavelengths, and each wavelength is assigned to a particular service type to ensure isolation. Moreover, In our proposed architecture, the computing tasks for URLLC requests are performed locally at the fog node, while for the eMBB and mMTC requests they are performed at the BBU hotel.

# *A. Traffic Types*

In this work, the URLLC, eMBB and mMTC are referred to as type 1, 2 and 3 requests, respectively. It is assumed that only the same traffic type can be groomed into shared lightpaths (optical channels spanning one or more physical links) to ensure isolation between the slices [20]. For type 1 requests, the fronthaul traffic is carried between the RRHs and fog nodes, while backhaul is transported between the fog nodes and the core CO. The backhaul request of URLLC traverses the BBU hotel node without being processed. The fronthaul traffic for type 2 and type 3 requests is processed at the BBU hotels as it traverses fog nodes without being processed, as fog nodes are equipped with reconfigurable optical add-drop multiplexer (ROADM) [21]. Moreover, the backhaul for type 2 and type 3 requests is carried between BBU hotels and core CO same as in traditional 2-layer architecture.

### B. Functional Splits

The functional split is the splitting of functionalities between the RRH and the BBU hotel. The employed split option at the RRH decides which functions are performed at the RRHs and which are centralized. 3GPP defines 8 split options, each of which has distinct data-rate and latency requirements [22]. Traditionally, C-RAN used split option 8, which offers the highest degree of function centralization when compared to all other options, as can be seen in Fig. 2. However, it has unrealistic fronthaul data-rate requirements, making it an unfeasible option [7]. Split option 7 is currently the most popular for the fronthaul transport in 3-layer RU-DU-CU architecture [23]. It provides significant centralization gain and has a less stringent fronthaul data-rate requirement than split option 8. In this work, we have therefore considered the split option 7 for the transport of fronthaul traffic between RRHs and the fog nodes/BBU hotels. In addition to fronthaul traffic, the RU-DU-CU architecture also considers midhaul traffic routed through split option 2 between DU and CU [16]. Midhaul traffic is not considered in the proposed CF-RAN over WDM architecture, as total processing of all slice requests occurs at the RRH and fog nodes/BBU hotels.

# III. TRAFFIC AWARE JOINT BBU HOTEL AND FOG NODE ACTIVATION ALGORITHM (TAB-FAA)

We present a heuristic algorithm named TAB-FAA, as illustrated in Algorithm 1. The notations used in the proposed algorithm and its corresponding discussion are summarized in Table I. The algorithm determines the locations of minimum number of active BBU hotels and fog nodes based on the varying proportion of incoming slice requests. Moreover, it executes GRWA on all requests while isolating each slice and maximizing network connectivity. In the algorithm,  $\mathbb{N}$ ,  $\mathbb{R}$  and  $\mathbb{T}$  denote the set of nodes, requests and request types, respectively. The core CO is denoted by o. The maximum allowable fronthaul delay for type t is denoted as  $D_t$ . The request types are given as  $t \in \mathbb{T} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ , where t = 1 represents URLLC, t = 2 represents eMBB, and t = 3 represents mMTC requests types. The required fronthaul and

backhaul capacities for type t request are denoted as  $C_F^{(t)}$  and  $C_B^{(t)}$ , respectively. Finally, the path between two nodes is represented as  $P_{ab}$ .

Incoming requests in set  $\mathbb{R}$  are partitioned in predefined proportions for all slice types. A certain request r is identified by its generating node s and its type t (Line 2). If r is a type 1 request and s is associated with an active CO, the distance between s and the active COs is calculated. The path delay Q is then calculated using the calculated path length (Line 6), where total delay includes propagation delay, transmission delay, interface delay due to the used split option at RRH, and electronic switch delay. The path is used to route r if  $Q < D_t$ and the path's link capacity is sufficient for routing r (Lines **8-9**). If r cannot be served at an active CO, residual capacity is calculated in the link between s and the fog node associated with s, as well as the link between fog node and core CO o. If sufficient capacity is available, the fog node to which s is connected is activated if it is not already active, and r is carried along the computed path (Lines 14-15). If r cannot be satisfied at the fog node, then request is blocked (Line 17).

If incoming request r is a request of type 2 or type 3, and sis not associated with an active BBU hotel, then all reachable COs from s are examined. After that, the shortest path between the candidate CO and s is determined using the Dijkstra algorithm, and the delay of this path is calculated (Line 22). The required fronthaul latency is calculated by path length. If Q is less than or equal to the maximum allowable fronthaul latency and the remaining link capacity is sufficient to carry fronthaul and backhaul traffic for r, then s is associated with the CO. If candidate CO does not have an active BBU hotel, it is activated and r is routed via the computed path (Lines **24-25**). Moreover, r is blocked if no CO can host BBU for s (Line 27). If source s is already associated with an active BBU hotel upon the arrival of r, its location is determined (Line 29). If the residual capacity along the determined path is sufficient to transport  $C_F^{(t)}$  and  $C_B^{(t)}$ , then r is transported via the computed path (Line 31); otherwise, r is blocked (Lines 33). The process is repeated for all incoming requests.

#### V. CONCLUSION

In this work, we implemented the network slicing paradigm in the a CF-RAN over WDM network architecture. We investigated the effect of variation in the ratio of slice requests due to the random nature of incoming traffic on the overall performance of the architecture. A heuristic algorithm is presented to evaluate the impact of traffic variation. The objective of the algorithm is to serve the maximum number of incoming requests by activating the minimum number of fog nodes/BBU hotels while considering the constraints for fronthaul latency and link capacity. Furthermore, the algorithm performs grooming, routing, and wavelength assignment (GRWA) for all three types of incoming requests.

Our results demonstrate that variations in the slice requests ratio of incoming traffic have a significant impact on network performance, and an architecture that can dynamically adapt to variations in traffic scenarios is a key enabler for network slicing in 5G. Specifically, we observed that an increase in the proportion of URLLC requests, with their stringent latency requirements, necessitates higher resource allocation at the fog nodes, potentially leading to resource contention and degraded performance for other services. The proposed heuristic algorithm addresses this challenge by dynamically adapting the resource allocation and routing strategies based on the observed slice request ratios. By minimizing resource utilization while maintaining maximum network connectivity, our algorithm ensures efficient and flexible resource management, enabling the CF-RAN architecture to accommodate diverse traffic scenarios effectively.
