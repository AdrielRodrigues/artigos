# Federated Learning-Empowered Mobile Network Management for 5G and Beyond Networks: From Access to Core

Joohyung Lee<sup>®</sup>, *Senior Member, IEEE*, Faranaksadat Solat<sup>®</sup>, Tae Yeon Kim, and H. Vincent Poor<sup>®</sup>, *Life Fellow, IEEE* 

Abstract—The fifth generation (5G) and beyond wireless networks are envisioned to provide an integrated communication and computing platform that will enable multipurpose and intelligent networks driven by a growing demand for both traditional end users and industry verticals. This evolution will be realized by innovations in both core and access capabilities, mainly from virtualization technologies and ultra-dense networks, e.g., software-defined networking (SDN), network slicing, network function virtualization (NFV), multi-access edge computing (MEC), terahertz (THz) communications, etc. However, those technologies require increased complexity of resource management and large configurations of network slices. In this new milieu, with the help of artificial intelligence (AI), network operators will strive to enable AI-empowered network management by automating radio and computing resource management and orchestration processes in a data-driven manner. In this regard, most of the previous AI-empowered network management approaches adopt a traditional centralized training paradigm where diverse training data generated at network functions over distributed base stations associated with MEC servers are transferred to a central training server. On the other hand, to exploit distributed and parallel processing capabilities of distributed network entities in a fast and secure manner, federated learning (FL) has emerged as a distributed AI approach that can enable many AI-empowered network management approaches by allowing for AI training at distributed network entities without the need for data transmission to a centralized server. This article comprehensively surveys the field of FLempowered mobile network management for 5G and beyond networks from access to the core. Specifically, we begin with an introduction to the state-of-the-art of FL by exploring and analyzing recent advances in FL in general. ### <span id="page-0-0"></span>I. Introduction

HE FIFTH generation (5G) and beyond wireless networks can be realized by innovations in both core (i.e., virtualization technologies) and access capabilities (i.e., ultra-dense networks) aiming for multipurpose and intelligent networks on an integrated communication and computing platform. Specifically, mature virtualization technologies in the mobile core network, such as software-defined networking (SDN), network slicing, and network function virtualization (NFV), have been widely adopted on top of distributed multi-access edge computing (MEC) for providing the flexibility necessary for dynamic resource allocation [13]. In the access network, an ultra-dense network can be considered an emerging technology for serving a massive number of users with considerable capacity, which also can be easily integrated with extremely high frequencies such as millimeter wave (mmWave) and terahertz (THz) communications [14]. However, those technologies require increased complexity of resource management (e.g., resource allocation, interference management, network deployment, backhauling, congestion management, etc.) [14], and large configurations of network slices [13].

<span id="page-0-1"></span>Recently, with the renaissance and democratization of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) algorithms, AI-empowered mobile network management has been attracting considerable attention from industry and academia. Along with this trend, the 3rd generation partnership project (3GPP), an organization that standardizes mobile communication systems, has introduced the network data analytics function (NWDAF) at its technical specifications as a new type of standard control plane (CP) function within the core networks system. The newly proposed NWDAF gathers various data generated from multiple distributed network functions

(NFs) and provides data analytics (i.e., both training and inference) using ML/AI algorithms [\[15\]](#page-31-2), [\[16\]](#page-31-3). However, due to the increasing number of NFs over distributed base stations (BSs) associated with MEC servers, traditional centralized ML/AI methods, which collect raw training data samples at a centralized core NF, face two challenging problems. That is, 1) consuming enormous network resources and 2) causing data security problems. Hence, for these reasons, with consideration of the distributed nature of NFs, it is necessary to adopt distributed learning algorithms that enable privacy-enhanced and cost-effective AI-empowered mobile network management systems by distributed NFs cooperatively building the shared model. Moreover, such a shared model can anticipate future states (e.g., traffic load, computational load, etc.) and achieve automatic network management.

The concept of federated learning (FL), one of the most promising distributed AI approaches, has been proposed for designing AI-empowered mobile network management in a cost-effective and privacy-enhanced manner [\[17\]](#page-31-4), [\[18\]](#page-31-5), [\[19\]](#page-31-6), [\[20\]](#page-31-7), [\[21\]](#page-31-8). In this sense, under the distributed nature of FL, multiple distributed NFs over distributed BSs associated with MEC servers train their local models by using their local data. Then, the global AI model is constructed by the repeated aggregation process of locally trained models. For instance, leveraging the augmented data collected from distributed BSs associated with MEC servers, a quasiglobal mobile traffic prediction model can be constructed via the FL process as in [\[22\]](#page-31-9). Specifically, in this work, each MEC server trains its local model by utilizing its local mobile traffic pattern dataset and transmits the computed local model parameters (e.g., weights or gradients) to a centralized server. Then, the centralized server aggregates all local model updates and constructs an improved global model for mobile traffic prediction. Compared to conventional centralized training approaches, adopting FL for AI-empowered mobile network management over distributed NFs has the following advantages.

- <span id="page-1-7"></span>• Network efficiency: Instead of transmitting raw data to the centralized server, less information, such as model parameters, is delivered to the centralized server. Thus, the data transmission burden for generating the model over the distributed NFs is significantly reduced.
- <span id="page-1-4"></span>• Privacy: In distributed NFs, to support multi-tenant network management, privacy-sensitive data can be generated over distributed NFs. In FL, since the raw data is kept in local data storage, the leakage of privacysensitive data to unintended operators can be minimized. Thus, with such privacy protection features, the AI model for network management can be efficiently improved by utilizing distributed and plentiful datasets over multiple NFs, which might be associated with multiple operators.
- Low latency: In addition to less amount of data transmission, inference can be made at distributed NFs by utilizing the global model. Thus, low transmission delay and real-time decision-making for network management can be achieved by adopting the FL framework.

By leveraging the distributed datasets and computation power of multiple MEC servers with reduced privacy concerns, <span id="page-1-5"></span>FL has been applied for use in a variety of AI-empowered mobile network management problems, such as mobile traffic prediction, core, access network management, etc. For instance, FL has been applied to mobile traffic prediction by creating prediction models without sharing traffic load data across multiple BSs associated with MEC servers [\[22\]](#page-31-9). The emerging interest in integrating FL and AI-empowered mobile network management reveals that it is the right moment to attract more attention to this promising research area toward 5G and beyond networks.

### A. Key Concept of FL

The proliferation of primary computing devices such as smartphones and tablets has led to the generation and accumulation of an unprecedented amount of data on these devices. Federated learning (FL), a term coined in 2016 by MacMahan et al., obviates the need to store data on the centralized server and has a distributed training paradigm that allows users to collectively construct a global model by aggregating their locally trained models [17]. By offering new AI solutions in a distributed and privacy-preserving manner, FL has the potential to elevate AI-empowered mobile network management toward 5G networks and beyond. This is because the current 5G technology already includes diverse virtualized NFs distributed on multiple BSs or MEC servers associated with those BSs in a container. This means that the enormous amounts of data over these distributed NFs are collected and need to be analyzed for efficient mobile network management. Correspondingly, the success of FL with respect to mobile device management for diverse mobile applications could naturally be transferred and adapted to build AI-empowered mobile network management in a distributed and secure manner. This could be accomplished by pushing training functions, e.g., NWDAF, to the distributed NFs, where data are generated, rather than to the centralized server.

The typical architecture and training process of an FL involving *N* participants is depicted in Fig. 2, where the FL server is the model owner and the FL participants are contributors. In this sense, the FL server, a centralized server, coordinates FL tasks over FL participants. Then, each FL participant conducts local training on its local dataset and transmits the local update to the FL server. In the end, the FL server aggregates local updates from the FL participants and generates the global model. This overall procedure can be categorized into the following three steps:

Multiple rounds of the FL process, including Steps 2-3, are repeated until the global loss function at the FL server converges to the termination condition, or until the specified target accuracy is reached. Note that in the FL process, different types of ML models can be chosen depending on the application of the FL service. At the same time, many studies on FL utilized the convolutional neural network (CNN) model to obtain an image classification model. Various types of neural networks (CNN, long short-term memory (LSTM) model, etc.), regression models (e.g., linear regression), K-means clustering models, support vector machine (SVM) models, etc. can be used for the FL process. Furthermore, in Step 3, the global model generation, which is an integral part of FL, could be achieved by applying various algorithms for different purposes including safeguarding user privacy, enhancing convergence rates, and mitigating the harm caused <span id="page-5-1"></span>by malicious agents [\[6\]](#page-31-15), [\[28\]](#page-31-27). For instance, the simplest algorithm is Average Aggregation, which calculates the mean of updates from all FL participants. However, it may not perform optimally when dealing with non-Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) data. To improve upon this, Weighted Aggregation, such as the Federated Averaging algorithm (FedAvg), assigns varying weights to contributions from different FL participants based on performance or other factors (e.g., dataset size). Secure Aggregation employs techniques such as homomorphic encryption or secure multi-party computation to provide a high level of privacy protection. Nevertheless, it requires the cost of significant computational overhead, leading to slower convergence compared to other aggregation algorithms. Another approach to this perspective, the Differential Privacy Average Aggregation algorithm, ensures strong privacy protection by injecting random noise into the model updates before aggregation. However, it may still exhibit reduced speed and efficiency compared to other aggregation methods. There are numerous other aggregation algorithms in the literature, each with its own set of advantages and drawbacks. Some may be better suited for specific FL contexts than others. Hence, a thoughtful selection of aggregation algorithms is essential, considering the specific purpose of the services [\[28\]](#page-31-27).

### *B. FL Classifications*

Based on recent studies, we classify FL into three dimensions, namely, networking structure, synchronization approach, and data partitioning [\[10\]](#page-31-22), [\[29\]](#page-32-0).

<span id="page-5-3"></span><span id="page-5-2"></span>*1) Networking Structure:* The networking structures for FL can be categorized into three classes, including centralized FL, decentralized FL, and hierarchical FL [\[10\]](#page-31-22), [\[29\]](#page-32-0), [\[30\]](#page-32-1), [\[31\]](#page-32-2) as summarized in Fig. [3.](#page-5-0)

- Centralized FL (CFL): As exemplified in Fig. [3,](#page-5-0) the CFL is one of the most popular networking architectures for FL because it is simple and easy to deploy in practice. The initial study aiming to implement FL is based on this CFL [\[19\]](#page-31-6). In CFL, similar to the clientserver architecture, a single CFL server is involved in the aggregation process to construct a global model from the local models generated by FL participants. Even though this architecture has many advantages in terms of deploying FL with a less complex management protocol, these structures often experience long local and global model transmission delays depending on the physical distance and network status between the centralized server and FL participants. Moreover, the bandwidth at the CFL servers might become overloaded, which degrades the convergence speed. Obviously, the risk of a single point of failure in this centralized architecture always exists.
- Decentralized FL (DFL): The DFL was proposed to address the above-mentioned problem. In this structure, the model parameters of FL participants are exchanged only among interconnected FL participants without using a centralized server. Similar to peer-to-peer (P2P) architecture, all FL participants are connected in a P2P manner to generate the global model, which requires more complex management to achieve faster model convergence than CFL. Academic efforts have widely relied on the blockchain protocol to implement DFL in a practical manner.
- <span id="page-6-1"></span>• Hierarchical FL (HFL): The HFL was proposed to take advantage of the merits of both CFL and DFL. The topology for HFL can be managed in a dynamic manner [\[29\]](#page-32-0), or a static manner [\[32\]](#page-32-3). In HFL, localized aggregations at the intermediate aggregation node can avoid high communication costs and alleviate the burden at the centralized server. Furthermore, to boost the convergence speeds, clustering FL participants for intermediate aggregation in this structure can be widely studied to prevent model deviation under non-IID data [\[22\]](#page-31-9).
- *2) Synchronization Approach:* The synchronization approach for FL can be categorized into three classes, including strong synchronization, asynchronization, and weak synchronization [\[29\]](#page-32-0).
  - Strong synchronization FL: In strong synchronization FL, the model aggregation frequencies of all FL participants are the same. However, if participants have different computing power or different amounts of data, local training speeds may vary. For example, faster participants would have to wait for slower participants until their models are aggregated in a synchronized manner.
  - Asynchronous FL: To address this issue, asynchronous FL allows participants to independently push updates to the central FL server. This enables the global model to be updated whenever new, updated local models are received, without the need to synchronize all local models from all FL participants. This approach can eliminate stragglers and efficiently utilize computing resources, but it may degrade the convergence speed due to the severe staleness problem when data is non-IID and unbalanced.

- Weak synchronization FL: In weak synchronization, FL participants update their local models at different frequencies before model aggregation on the aggregation server in a synchronized manner. This architecture has recently been well-investigated in HFL architecture by addressing the trade-off between strong synchronization FL and asynchronous FL.
- *3) Data Partitioning:* Data partitioning for FL can be categorized into three classes, including horizontal FL, vertical FL, and federated transfer learning (FTL) [\[18\]](#page-31-5), [\[29\]](#page-32-0).
  - Horizontal FL: In horizontal FL, different sample datasets with the same feature space over FL participants are utilized for local training (e.g., the banking data of two regional banks have different user groups but the data have the same features).
  - Vertical FL (VFL): Vertical FL can be applied to the same sample datasets with different feature spaces (e.g., data of the same user groups served by companies from two different business sectors, such as a bank and an e-commerce company.)
  - FTL: The FTL can be applied to different sample datasets with different feature spaces.

# *C. Hardware Architecture*

In FL, the local training tasks are executed on distributed FL participants such as MEC servers, edge devices, etc. These participants typically have limited computational resources, memory footprints, or battery power compared to centralized servers. Thus, specialized hardware design for FL is a promising direction to accelerate local training tasks while using much less energy consumption compared to general-purpose processors. Therefore, to efficiently implement FL, various hardware accelerators have been considered for the hardware architecture of FL [\[7\]](#page-31-28), [\[33\]](#page-32-4).

- <span id="page-6-0"></span>• Graphics Processing Units (GPU)-based accelerators: A GPU is an efficient computing tool that leverages highly efficient matrix-based operations and offers a wide range of hardware choices. GPUs provide high memory capacity, bandwidth, and throughput. However, their power consumption is relatively high, which poses limitations when applying them to large-scale systems or battery-powered devices. Notably, the NVIDIA DGX1 and DGX2 are popular GPU-based accelerators that provide accelerated deep learning performance. It is worth mentioning that research efforts have been made to enhance the energy efficiency of GPU-based FL processes [\[34\]](#page-32-5).
- <span id="page-6-2"></span>• Field-programmable Gate Array (FPGAs)-based accelerators: FPGAs provide energy-efficient computing and offer reduced costs for large-scale operations. According to Microsoft Research's Catapult Project, FPGAs are estimated to be 10 times more power efficient than GPUs. However, utilizing FPGAs requires a significant amount of storage, external memory, bandwidth, and computational resources capable of handling billions of operations per second. In the context of FL, FL participants need to have sufficient storage capacity for local datasets.

- Further research is needed to optimize these limitations and explore ways to address them effectively.
- Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASIC)-based accelerators: ASIC architectures surpass FPGAs and GPUs in terms of both energy efficiency and computational speed. They also minimize memory transfer. An exemplary ASIC-based accelerator is EdgeTPU, developed by Google, which provides an open and end-to-end infrastructure for implementing AI solutions. However, it should be noted that ASIC development typically involves a longer development cycle and lacks flexibility in handling varying deep learning network designs, which requires further investigations.

# *D. Networking Requirements*

In FL, as illustrated in Fig. [2,](#page-4-1) the overall delay of the FL process encompasses the local training delay at FL participants from a computing perspective, as well as the uplink transmission delay (i.e., local model upload) and downlink transmission delay (i.e., global model download) from a networking perspective. The downlink and uplink transmission delays are dependent on factors such as the model size, the number of FL participants, their channel conditions, and the overall bandwidth allocated for the FL process. To achieve higher accuracy in a shorter time, involving more FL participants is desirable. However, a large number of FL participants, such as massive connectivity of IoT devices, can lead to increased networking delays due to reduced bandwidth per FL participant and increased interference, among other factors. Consequently, the networking bottleneck is recognized as a significant challenge in FL. For instance, wireless networks have significantly smaller bandwidth compared to wired networks. Therefore, the allocated bandwidth for each FL participant may be limited and further reduced as the number of FL participants increases. This limited network bandwidth not only hampers efficient networking between FL participants and the centralized server but also gives rise to straggler FL participants who fail to share their local updates with the centralized server during communication rounds. Both these issues serve as bottlenecks for the performance of FL deployment [\[33\]](#page-32-4). In mobile network management, most distributed MEC servers are interconnected through wired connections, which helps alleviate networking problems due to the high bandwidth of wired connections. However, resource exhaustion caused by control plane overhead in FL still persists, particularly in scenarios where UAV-assisted MEC server environments rely on wireless connections to interconnect MEC servers. Therefore, to meet the low-latency or ultralow latency requirements of FL, depending on the services involved, various techniques have been studied, including efficient FL participant selection, gradient compression, bandwidth allocation, and over-the-air computation, leveraging the superposition nature of wireless transmission [\[5\]](#page-31-20), [\[33\]](#page-32-4), [\[35\]](#page-32-6).

### <span id="page-8-6"></span><span id="page-8-5"></span>*A. Background Information About 5G and Beyond Networks*

The connection between the user equipment (UE) and the data network in 5G requires the establishment of Protocol Data Unit (PDU) sessions where 5G allows each UE to establish multiple PDU sessions simultaneously to support various services with different QoS requirements at the same time. Here, each PDU session can also belong to a different network slice. As shown in Fig. [4,](#page-8-1) the UE first connects to a next-generation NodeB (gNB) in the access network to initiate this PDU session. Then, the UE establishes the PDU session through a transport network connected to the 5G core networks, which provides the endpoint to the data network. Contrary to the 4G core network, which consists of dedicated types of equipment, the 5G core network consists of a collection of various virtualized NFs that implement a service-based architecture as follows [\[15\]](#page-31-2), [\[81\]](#page-33-10), [\[82\]](#page-33-11):

- <span id="page-8-16"></span>• Access and Mobility Management Function (AMF): The AMF conducts access and mobility management for UE and also provides authentication for UE.
- Session Management Function (SMF): The SMF helps establish, modify and release PDU sessions. The SMF also provides inactivity timers to UEs for managing the activation/deactivation of PDU sessions energyefficiently.
- User Plane Function (UPF): Whereas the AMF and the SMF are control plane functions, the UPF acts as a PDU session anchor to provide a connection for the access network to the 5G core as a UPF. Furthermore, it also provides inspection, routing, and forwarding packets and QoS handling services by applying specific traffic rules.

<span id="page-8-17"></span><span id="page-8-14"></span><span id="page-8-13"></span><span id="page-8-12"></span><span id="page-8-10"></span>Here, the service-based architecture comprises NFs that expose services through RESTful APIs. By using these RESTful APIs, NFs can invoke services in other NFs. To expose the services of NFs to service consumers, service producers must register their services with an NF repository function (NRF). Upon request from an NF, the NRF can provide a list of identifiers for suitable service producers by responding to the request [\[83\]](#page-33-12). Note that since NFs in 5G

# *B. Standard Activities for AI-Empowered Mobile Network Management*

*1) Access Network:* On the access network side, the pioneering O-radio access network (RAN) alliance, which introduces algorithmic and hardware-independent RAN deployment, was established in 2018 by leading operators with the vision of developing open specifications for an open and efficient access network that leverages AI for automating <span id="page-9-3"></span><span id="page-9-0"></span>different NFs and reducing operating expenses (OPEX). The O-RAN architecture comprises two essential components: the non-realtime RAN intelligent controller (non-RT RIC) and the near real-time RAN intelligent controller (near-RT RIC). The non-RT RIC is responsible for making non-real-time decisions, including policy management and analytics. These decisions can take longer than one second and are executed through the O1 interface. On the other hand, the near-RT RIC handles near-real-time control and optimization tasks, such as resource allocation and load balancing. These actions are performed within a short time interval, specifically between 10 milliseconds and 1 second, utilizing the E2 interface. The O-RAN architecture's versatility enables the implementation of AI-enabled RAN, facilitating various use cases of RAN automation. Examples of such applications include traffic steering, quality of experience (QoE) optimization, RAN slice service level agreement (SLA) assurance, and more [\[87\]](#page-33-16). In particular, ML-based network management and automation, known as xAPPs, running on RAN intelligent controllers (i.e., near-RT RIC) have been designed in the O-RAN architecture aiming for collecting near real-time RAN information to provide value added services. The authors [\[88\]](#page-33-17) mentioned that these xAPPs still have limitations in that large-scale datasets and an experimental testing infrastructure are not yet available. They designed the first publicly available large-scale O-RAN testing framework with software-defined radios-in-the-loop. In this testbed, deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based control of RAN slicing, scheduling, and online model training was implemented and evaluated.

<span id="page-9-6"></span><span id="page-9-5"></span><span id="page-9-4"></span><span id="page-9-1"></span>Moreover, 3GPP specification series 37 provides RANcentric data collection and utilization for long-term evolution (LTE) and New Radio and on next-generation self-organizing networks (SON) for 5G networks and beyond [\[89\]](#page-33-18). In particular, [\[86\]](#page-33-13) demonstrates that the AI/ML functionality and the following use cases are presently under investigation within 3GPP. It is important to note that the specific AI/ML algorithms and models for these use cases are considered implementation-specific and are beyond the scope of this standard specification. Consequently, the study mentioned in [\[86\]](#page-33-13) concentrates on the AI/ML functionality for the specified use cases, along with the potential inputs and outputs that can be collected and generated at the access network. Fig. [5](#page-9-0) depicts the functional framework for RAN intelligence specified in [\[86\]](#page-33-13). The data collection function serves as the provider of training/inference data to the model training and inference functions. It acts as the input data source for these processes. However, it is important to note that the data preparation process, including tasks such as data pre-processing, cleaning, formatting, and transmission, is performed separately and is not part of this function. Once the model is trained or updated, it can be delivered to the model inference function through the model deployment/update procedure. The model inference function is responsible for monitoring the performance of the AI/ML model and providing feedback to the model training function, facilitating continuous improvement. Subsequently, the actor receives the output from the model inference function and triggers corresponding actions to be taken by other entities or itself. The actor function plays a crucial role in the feedback loop, as it provides feedback to the data collection function, which is essential for deriving training data, inference data, and other relevant inputs. Based on this functional framework, the following use cases are proposed:

- AI/ML-based network energy saving: Cell activation/deactivation is one of the methods to achieve energy savings in the RAN by leveraging traffic offloading in a layered structure. In this context, UE location information and UE measurement reports are considered as input data from the UE. Additionally, input data for training can be also obtained from the local node, including UE mobility, trajectory prediction, current/predicted energy efficiency, and resource status
- AI/ML-based load balancing: The main goal of load balancing is to ensure a fair distribution of network traffic across cells and cell areas to alleviate congestion. This optimization process includes adjusting handover parameters and executing handover actions to achieve the desired load distribution and enhance network efficiency. In this context, UE location information, UE mobility history information, and UE measurement reports are considered as input data from the UE. Additionally, input data for training can be also obtained from the local node, including current and predicted own resource status, UE trajectory prediction, current and predicted UE traffic, and predicted resource status information of neighbouring RAN.
- AI/ML-based mobility optimization: Mobility management refers to the strategy used to ensure uninterrupted service during mobility. Its primary aim is to minimize call drops, radio link failures, unnecessary handovers, and ping-pong effects. In the context of future highfrequency networks (e.g., 5G and beyond networks), where the coverage of a BS decreases, frequent handovers become more common, particularly for high-mobility UEs. Similar input data, such as UE information, radio measurement data, and UE mobility history, can be utilized for mobility optimization purposes.

# *C. AI-Empowered Network Load/Status Prediction*

According to Zhang et al. [\[22\]](#page-31-9), mobile traffic prediction schemes can be categorized into three methodologies: simple methods, parametric methods, and non-parametric methods, as listed in Table [IV.](#page-11-1)

<span id="page-11-5"></span>Simple methods estimate future traffic flows based on historical averaging [\[98\]](#page-33-26). In the case of the exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA), which is one of the most widely adopted methods, we can control the sensitivity for the temporal dynamics of traffic volumes by calibrating the exponential smoothing factor from 0 to 1 [\[97\]](#page-33-27). For instance, by assigning the value of 1 to the factor, the EWMA method becomes a naïve predictor that takes the traffic value of the last time slot as the future one. Simple methods are likely to be implemented in a greedy manner, which would probably result in highly efficient runtime execution. Although this type of method can achieve considerable prediction accuracy when the traffic volumes change gradually and slowly, it is less desirable for application in today's complex communication networks where traffic dynamics are highly influenced by a combination of various spatiotemporal factors.

<span id="page-11-15"></span><span id="page-11-6"></span>Parametric methods construct probabilistic or statistical prediction models under the assumption that traffic dynamics are generated from the random variables following some specific probability distributions [\[113\]](#page-33-28). These methods make predictions by computing or simulating a constructed model. One of the most classical approaches is the auto-regressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) [\[98\]](#page-33-26). ARIMA and its variants are known to be capable of characterizing the burstiness and self-similarity hidden in traffic time-series [\[99\]](#page-33-29), [\[100\]](#page-33-30), [\[101\]](#page-33-31), [\[102\]](#page-33-32). Xu et al. [\[101\]](#page-33-31) showed that temporal traffic dynamics can be expressed by the sum of two components, each of which represents regular and random changes in time series, respectively. ARIMA-based approaches deliver excellent prediction performance when the regular change component is large. However, their performance deteriorates as the random change component increases. Apart from ARIMA, a number of probabilistic and statistical utilities such as the Markov model [\[103\]](#page-33-33), entropy theory [\[104\]](#page-33-34), covariance function [\[105\]](#page-33-35), and alpha-stable model [\[106\]](#page-33-36) are explored for mobile traffic prediction.

<span id="page-11-16"></span><span id="page-11-11"></span><span id="page-11-10"></span><span id="page-11-9"></span><span id="page-11-8"></span><span id="page-11-7"></span><span id="page-11-3"></span><span id="page-11-2"></span>Non-parametric methods build prediction models without any assumption about the probability distributions that should be followed. The recent emergence of deep learning technology has resulted in AI-based prediction being brought into the mainstream of non-parametric methods. Because traffic prediction essentially requires the temporal dependence of data in a time-series dataset to be captured, LSTM is often employed, because of its superior ability to recognize sequential patterns [\[22\]](#page-31-9), [\[107\]](#page-33-37), [\[108\]](#page-33-38), [\[109\]](#page-33-39). Sevgican et al. [\[114\]](#page-33-40), under the structure and the protocols of NWDAF, conducted mobile traffic prediction and anomaly detection using various ML techniques. Specifically, they generated a cellbased synthetic dataset for 5G networks based on the fields defined by the 3GPP specifications and used it to train ML models, namely, linear regression, LSTM, and recursive neural networks. With the simulation, they confirmed the performance of neural network algorithms to be superior to that of linear regression for network load prediction, whereas the tree-based gradient boosting algorithm outperformed logistic regression with respect to anomaly detection. Several research groups [\[22\]](#page-31-9), [\[107\]](#page-33-37), [\[108\]](#page-33-38), and [\[109\]](#page-33-39) combined an autoencoder, multi-task learning, and FL with LSTM, respectively, to characterize not only the temporal dependence but also the spatial dependence between cells or BSs. Others adopted deep belief networks (DBM) [\[110\]](#page-33-41), CNN [\[13\]](#page-31-0), [\[111\]](#page-33-42), and transfer learning [\[112\]](#page-33-43). Among them [\[111\]](#page-33-42) and [\[112\]](#page-33-43) attempted to model the spatiotemporal dependencies commonly arising in cross-domain datasets to enable city-scale traffic prediction.

<span id="page-11-14"></span><span id="page-11-13"></span><span id="page-11-12"></span><span id="page-11-4"></span>The mobile traffic prediction model offers diverse applications in mobile network management. It automatically identifies demand patterns and anticipates future demands,

# *D. AI-Empowered Networking Resource Management*

<span id="page-12-10"></span><span id="page-12-5"></span><span id="page-12-4"></span>AI-based approaches have been studied for implementation in access networks, radio resource management and allocation, and power allocation [\[120\]](#page-34-3), [\[124\]](#page-34-7), [\[125\]](#page-34-8), [\[126\]](#page-34-9), [\[127\]](#page-34-10). Specifically, an RL-based approach was used to achieve power control and rate adaptation in mobile networks [\[124\]](#page-34-7). Q-learning-based optimal resource allocation and network access mechanisms in license-assisted access LTE-based heterogeneous networks were proposed [\[125\]](#page-34-8). A DRL-based joint user association and resource allocation (i.e., channel allocation) algorithm was proposed to take into account that the formulated problems have non-convex and combinatorial characteristics [\[126\]](#page-34-9). Recently, as shown in Fig. [9,](#page-12-1) a RAN slicing mechanism for slice-level management by using DRL was proposed to allocate radio resources [\[120\]](#page-34-3). The DRL algorithm learns the best action, i.e., RB allocation, from various states specified to satisfy the slice requirements with the minimum RB allocation using trial and error. The RB allocation is based on multiple defined states, such as throughput requirement, delay requirement, number of allocated RBs, and number of packet arrivals.

<span id="page-13-2"></span><span id="page-13-1"></span>A DRL approach was adopted for load balancing within an intracluster group of small BSs, where handover management is automatically conducted based on the historical loads of the BSs [\[128\]](#page-34-11). DRL-based handover management was designed to minimize energy consumption resulting from frequent handovers [\[129\]](#page-34-12). This approach considers the local information, such as channel gain, angle of direction, required data rate, and the previous UE association vector as the state. This work was extended [\[130\]](#page-34-13) by incorporating the characteristics of the mmWave environment into a multi-agent DRL process. The A2-threshold-RSRP in the LTE standard is a configuration parameter used to determine the handover decision in practical handover procedures. The threshold value significantly impacts the throughput of cell-edge UEs. This value should be carefully determined by considering many factors, including the traffic load, the number of active users, and channel quality. To address this problem, Chuai et al. [\[131\]](#page-34-14) proposed an ML or data-driven approach to find the optimal A2-threshold-RSRP, using various factors as features for the model. Polese et al. [\[132\]](#page-34-15) designed architecture based on an edge controller for mobile networks to dynamically cluster and associate BSs and controllers based on the global mobility patterns of UEs, with the aim of minimizing the number of handover events. The proposed approach was evaluated using real data from hundreds of BSs of a major U.S. operator.

<span id="page-13-5"></span>In the context of 5G core network management, a DRLbased solution for managing PDU sessions embedded in the standardized NWDAF was proposed [\[82\]](#page-33-11). The solution utilized an inactivity timer-management scheme, which aimed to reduce both UE power consumption and network resource usage. Specifically, the inactivity timer problem is formulated as a Markov decision process (MDP), with the state space represented by a binary vector of the current and past states, the supported traffic type, and the time of day of each PDU session. The DRL model selects appropriate timer values for each PDU session, based on a reward function that balances the power consumption of the UE and the signaling overhead incurred. By using this approach, unnecessary signaling can be minimized while ensuring that the power consumption of the UE is optimized.

# <span id="page-15-2"></span>*A. Distributed NWDAF for FL-Empowered Mobile Network Management*

In 5G networks and beyond, because almost all NFs are related to each other, meaningful and valuable analytics can be obtained by aggregating several local models trained by various NFs with different types of input data. This approach is an example of data partitioning in FL. FL architecture in distributed NWDAF was proposed to enable the efficient and secure aggregation of local models in a distributed environment, as shown in Fig. [12](#page-15-1) [\[91\]](#page-33-20). The architecture includes multiple leaf NWDAFs that gather local datasets from associated NFs (e.g., NRF, AMF, and SMF) and conduct local training using the global model provided by the root NWDAF. The root NWDAFs in the centralized server communicate with multiple leaf NWDAFs to upload and download the model, respectively. Various aggregation methods, such as FedAvg, can be applied to the aggregation module located in the learning module. The root NWDAFs can manage the FL process efficiently using the management module. In this proposed architecture, because the leaf NWDAF is not connected with NFs other than the corresponding NF to

# <span id="page-17-8"></span>*C. FL-Empowered Networking Resource Management*

<span id="page-17-9"></span><span id="page-17-5"></span><span id="page-17-4"></span>Efficient bandwidth allocation and power control are crucial tasks in radio resource management for access networks owing to their limited availability. However, optimizing the bandwidth and power control mechanisms is challenging because ultra-dense networks are common, and large configurations of network slices are required. Moreover, sharing infrastructure over multiple operators is common; however, these operators want to avoid sharing operational information because of security concerns. To address this issue, the FL framework can be used for data-driven radio resource management by promoting data sharing among multiple BSs or operators in a distributed manner. For instance, the study of [\[175\]](#page-35-15) designed a bandwidth allocation problem for the Ultra Reliable Low Latency Communications (URLLC) and enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) UEs of an MVNO as a Markov decision process. In particular, because the data collected by each MVNO in different areas is rarely shared among operators due to privacy issues, the FL framework was adopted to promote cooperation among MVNOs to collaboratively generate a global model for better bandwidth allocation. Specifically, they proposed the federated DRL approach to train the bandwidth allocation model among MVNOs. For practical concern, the proposed work considered an O-RAN architecture. Nevertheless, the paper does not address the challenge of unbalanced and non-IID data across MVNOs, crucial for FL model training and convergence, as highlighted in Section [II.](#page-2-1) In [\[165\]](#page-35-5), a novel radio resource allocation method for RAN slicing was designed under the FL framework using KPI mini-datasets for FL. Particularly, by collecting mini-datasets that contain the hourly traffic of the top-10 over-the-top (OTT) applications, channel quality indicator (CQI), and MIMO full-rank usage as input features, the model for predicting i) the number of occupied downlinks, ii) physical RBs, and iii) CPU load as a resource allocation vector was trained offline via the FL framework while fulfilling SLA constraints. To meet the SLA constraints in FL training, a proxy-Lagrangian framework is utilized for local optimization tasks. However, the complexity of this framework could pose challenges for deployment in real-world systems. There have been studies to investigate the possibility of the FL framework under O-RAN to provide resource management for 5G slicing services [\[176\]](#page-35-16), [\[177\]](#page-35-17). Singh and Nguyen [\[176\]](#page-35-16) specified RAN intelligent controllers based FL framework for slice-level resource management under the O-RAN architecture. The process involves collecting and storing slice operational data into distributed databases through the E2 interface. Subsequently, this data is transmitted to the near RT-RICs deployed on distributed MEC servers for local training. Meanwhile, the non-RT-RIC on the centralized server performs global model updates by aggregating the local model updates received from the near RT-RICs via the A1 interface. Finally, in this paper, joint optimal resource allocation and selection of FL participants, which involves identifying representative instances of near-RT-RICs, was formulated and solved. Moreover, in [\[177\]](#page-35-17), under the O-RAN architecture, to minimize the delay of URLLC slices and maximize the throughput for eMBB slices, joint power control and radio resource allocation functions (i.e., xAPPs in the O-RAN) were proposed based on the federated DRL. Particularly, two xAPPs, which are MLbased network management and automation running on RAN intelligent controllers, such as a power control xAPP and a hierarchical radio resource allocation xAPP, are considered where the local models for the FL are trained at each xAPP agent and serve as the input for predicting a joint Q-table. Furthermore, for industrial IoT services, in [\[178\]](#page-35-18), an FLbased DRL scheme was proposed to enable dynamic network management and resource allocation with differentiated QoS requirements of industrial IoT services. Particularly, according to the slices' QoS requirements, the proposed network management and resource allocation were mapped to the spreading factor and transmission power. Correspondingly, a multi-agent deep Q-learning-based dynamic slice transmission power and spreading factor adjustment process was designed to maximize self-QoS in terms of throughput and delay, where multi-agent self-models are locally trained over distributed agents and aggregated at a central server to exploit shared experiences between agents. For practicality, they considered an network slicing architecture based on SDN and NFV tailored for industrial IoT, with a specific focus on Long Range (LoRa) slicing. However, the studies by [\[177\]](#page-35-17), [\[178\]](#page-35-18) also lack specific FL management strategies to address convergence challenges caused by unbalanced and non-IID data among FL participants.

<span id="page-18-2"></span>As ultra-dense networks are expected to experience more frequent handover events, efficient handover management in access networks is critical for load balancing and efficient radio resource use. Recently, extensive research has been conducted using FL to design distributed data-driven algorithms for the handover management of multiple BSs. Particularly, the authors of [\[179\]](#page-35-19) proposed a federated DRL-based UE access control aimed at improving throughput while reducing <span id="page-18-3"></span><span id="page-18-0"></span>handoff costs, where UEs need to determine the UE network slice-BS three-layer association to meet the QoS for the required service. In this study, a horizontal FL is adopted for parameter aggregation on the BS, whereas the VFL is adopted for network slice/BS pair selection aggregation on the encrypted party. The work introduced Shapley values for efficient VFL aggregation, reducing communication overhead during FL model updates. However, this approach faces challenges when applied to UEs with limited battery and computing capabilities, as some UEs may struggle with FL task processing. Additionally, due to the heterogeneity of UEs, datasets collected can be unbalanced and non-IID, which necessitates addressing FL management strategies to tackle convergence issues. Similarly, in [\[180\]](#page-35-20), under O-RAN, to solve the challenging problems of load balancing and frequent handovers in the massive BS deployment under the O-RAN, a federated DRL-based scheme was designed with a centralized global model server installed in the RIC to update the deep Q-network (DQN) parameters, where each UE trains two DQNs using its observations (i.e., at previous time slot, BS access indicator, a vector of the RB allocation, a vector of RSSI in different RBs and different BSs) and makes access decisions (i.e., selecting proper BS and RB to access) based on its DQNs to maximize the throughput and avoid frequent handovers. The trained DQN parameters at each UE were considered a local model and transmitted to a global model server. In this work, an upper-confidence bound-based UE selection mechanism for FL participation is proposed to reduce the signaling overhead between UEs and the global model server for model updates. To mitigate convergence issues arising from non-IID data, a dueling structure was introduced to decompose the DQN parameters for each UE. However, similar to [\[179\]](#page-35-19), this proposed framework also places a computational burden on UEs for FL participation. Addressing this, such as through incentivization strategies, optimal selection of UEs, or burden reduction, remains an open challenge in this problem.

<span id="page-19-6"></span><span id="page-19-5"></span>Given that the performance of network slices relies on the normal operation of VMs, detecting anomalies in VMs is critical for ensuring the quality of network slice services. The performance and security of network slices can be improved by proactively detecting and addressing anomalies in VMs. To address this issue, [\[185\]](#page-35-25) proposed a hierarchical anomaly-detection mechanism using the FL framework. The proposed mechanism utilizes two stages of the FL framework to efficiently detect anomalies. In the first stage, an anomaly detector is associated with an aggregation server at the management domain level. This detector uses a simple ML model with a relatively small database. In the second stage, an anomaly detector is associated with an aggregation server at the end-to-end management-domain level. This detector utilizes a more complex ML model and a relatively larger database than that at the first stage. By utilizing this hierarchical approach, the proposed mechanism can effectively detect anomalies in network traffic while minimizing the required computational resources. This algorithm is designed to learn the normal data distribution from high-dimensional metric datasets spread over multiple VM monitors, where the resource metrics are related to CPU usage, memory consumption, disk read/write, and network input/output. Nevertheless, the complexity of the multi-discriminator bidirectional Wasserstein GAN-gradient penalty algorithm may make the approach challenging to implement.

# *E. Architecture or Implementation Activities for FL-Empowered Mobile Network Management*

Considering virtualization perspectives in the MEC environment, there are few studies on FL-based architectures for FL-empowered mobile network management [\[83\]](#page-33-12), [\[91\]](#page-33-20), [\[150\]](#page-34-33), [\[168\]](#page-35-8). The study in [\[168\]](#page-35-8) provided a prototype of AI-driven Kubernetes orchestration in MEC-enabled mobile networks. Particularly, in the implementation of a prototype to exchange data (centralized learning) and model updates (FL) between the central server (master) and local MEC hosts, which are capable of hosting MEC applications (workers), Web socket server-client connections are utilized. A distributed streaming platform (e.g., Apache Kafka) is another option for implementing real-time data pipelines instead of WebSocket connections for practical deployment. Moreover, to realize predictive autonomic capabilities in Kubernetes orchestration, the Kubernetes architecture is extended to include a monitor, analyze, plan, and execute (MAPE) closed control loop, which is as follows:

- Monitor component: it collects operational data (e.g., CPU) from all deployed Pods in the worker nodes in the core network, either through a centralized or a distributed manner
- Analyze component: it utilizes the collected data to perform data analytics for predictive autoscaling. It decides the necessity of an adaptation for the deployed pods.
- Plan component: it defines an adaptation plan if it is required based on the result of the analysis component.
- Execute component: it executes the adaptation plan defined by the plan component. It is located at the Kubernetes master.

<span id="page-20-3"></span><span id="page-20-2"></span>To efficiently adopt the FL framework in the system, in the work of [\[166\]](#page-35-6), which is an extension of the work in [\[165\]](#page-35-5) using a similar dataset for the FL in [\[165\]](#page-35-5), an SLAdriven stochastic FL participant selection policy was proposed to optimize the FL computation time and the underlying resource consumption by selecting only a subset of active FL participants in each FL round. To emulate the virtualization environment widely used for cloud-native deployments, they provided implementation details of the Docker compose-based FL framework, which can be supported by the container orchestration engines specified in the ETSI NFV IFA 029. Another concern regarding securely deploying the FL framework for 5G networks and beyond without the intervention of centralized model aggregation nodes is that some approaches have adopted blockchain technology as an underlying platform to sustain the FL framework [\[196\]](#page-35-36), [\[197\]](#page-35-37), [\[198\]](#page-35-38), [\[199\]](#page-35-39). In FL, the centralized model aggregation can be easily exposed to server attacks, resulting in a single point of failure. This greatly biases the global model as intended by attackers. To address this issue and induce trust in FL communication for mobile network management, blockchain technology has emerged as a promising framework. In a blockchain, data is stored as blocks linked to an immutable chain, and the ledger state is communicated to all nodes in the blockchain network. Consequently, blockchain eliminates the need for third-party security management [\[199\]](#page-35-39). An exemplary adoption of blockchain technology for mobile network management can be observed in UAV orchestration, which has gained significant attention. In this context, the holistic integration of blockchain-leveraged FL-assisted UAVs ensures the utmost level of data privacy, trusted exchange, and traceable access under continuous local updates due to topology change with diverse UAV mobility, models, and network constraints. As another example, [\[197\]](#page-35-37) proposed a blockchain-based FL architecture called Skunk's FL system with a zero-trust security model. They eliminated the role of centralized model aggregation nodes by suggesting a blockchain-based DFL environment. In this architecture, multiple FL participants over different network slices can participate in FL, and individual FL participants can share local model parameters using the blockchain's smart contract. To use this architecture, building an ML model throughout the network slices to detect attacks from IoT networks in a 5G-enabled network-sliced environment was introduced as a use case. Therefore, various benefits of utilizing blockchain in FL, such as traceability, decentralization, scalability, immutability, non-reusability, enhanced security, and privacy, can be leveraged to design AIempowered mobile network management in a secure manner.

# V. LESSON LEARNED

<span id="page-21-0"></span>In this section, we summarize the key lessons learned from this survey, which thus provide an overall picture of the current research of FL-empowered mobile network management based on its connection to the existing challenges of AI-empowered mobile network management.

- *A. Lessons Learned From AI-Empowered Mobile Network Management*
- <span id="page-21-1"></span>*1) Standards Activities:* AI has emerged as a pivotal facilitator for achieving end-to-end network automation in all aspects of 5G networks, including the access network, core network, and OAM management systems. Consequently, our survey reveals significant standardization activities aimed at defining the specifications for AI-empowered mobile network management, spanning from access to core. Within the 3GPP specifications, there has been an extensive exploration of RAN-centric data collection utilization. A detailed functional framework for RAN intelligence has been established, accompanied by comprehensive use cases for RAN-level data collection including AI/ML-based network energy saving, load balancing, and mobility optimization. On the core network side, 3GPP specifications incorporate the NWDAF and MDAF, providing data analytics frameworks for the control plane of the 5G core network and OAM domain, respectively. Additionally, ongoing development within 3GPP encompasses an network slice management and orchestration framework under the ETSI. Moreover, the ETSI's Zero-touch network & Service Management framework has introduced a pioneering horizontal and vertical end-to-end architecture for AI-empowered mobile network management, focusing on closed-loop automation. In conclusion, these standardization efforts offer valuable initial guidelines concerning architectural aspects, interfaces, use cases, and requirements for the collection and analysis of diverse data generated within the network domain, thus paving the way for network management automation.
- *2) Network Load/Status Prediction:* In the pursuit of network load/status prediction, significant efforts have been dedicated to developing deep learning-based models including mobile traffic, network status, user behavior, or throughput prediction. Primarily, supervised learning techniques such as LSTM, CNN, and others have been extensively employed to capture spatial and temporal patterns without relying on the statistical distribution of traffic models. The research contributions in AI-empowered network load/status prediction present valuable potential for various applications. These include resource management, enhancing load balancing, implementing optimized BS sleeping strategies, advancing adaptive streaming mechanisms, and others, as previously discussed. However, a common assumption among these contributions is the availability of significant training data, which is crucial for achieving higher prediction accuracy. In a real-world implementation, the challenge lies in collecting such data from diverse network entities, leading to privacy concerns and scalability issues, including networking, storage, and computing bottlenecks.
- *3) Networking Resource Management:* AI-based approaches have been studied for designing radio resource management and allocation, power allocation, handover, and session management. Specifically, in this field, a DRL-based approach was widely used. This is due to the fact that DRL is a great tool to design the decision-making algorithm in complex and dynamic environments as discussed in the various studies. the collected dataset for mobile traffic prediction in wireless networks is neither independent nor identically distributed, leading to model accuracy degradation and slow convergence speeds. To address these challenges, studies have explored various FL management schemes for mobile traffic prediction under the different types of FL methods (e.g., CFL, HFL, and FTL). A prevalent strategy adopted in this field involves clustering FL participants to enhance convergence. This approach groups participants with similar patterns, mitigating the effects of non-IID data. Location information and traffic patterns are often used as criteria for clustering. Additionally, various optimization techniques have been investigated to improve clustering mechanisms, such as dual attention for efficient aggregation, random clustering, and genetic algorithms for effective group management within clusters. *3) Networking Resource Management:* FL-based approaches have been extensively studied in the field of networking resource management, particularly for tasks such as bandwidth allocation, power control, and handover management. Given the sequential nature of decision-making in networking resource management, a significant focus of research has been on applying DRL in this field, known as federated DRL. These methods enable collaboration among multiple agents to create a global policy model, effectively addressing privacy and scalability issues, particularly in scenarios with multiple service providers. While most studies primarily utilized CFL, some have explored VFL for different service types, such as network slices. As FL management in this field, FL participant selection and the use of Shapley values for aggregation are employed to minimize communication overhead in the FL process. Nevertheless, challenges with unbalanced and non-IID data among FL participants still exist, and the field lacks comprehensive FL management solutions to address the challenges compared to the mobile traffic prediction domain. Only a few studies have addressed these issues, proposing FL mechanisms such as incorporating DQN parameters and a Proxy-Lagrangian framework. Specifically, in FL-based handover management, UEs are considered as FL participants for global policy model generation. The limited computing and battery capacities of UEs pose a significant challenge to the FL process, necessitating careful strategies to either reduce this burden or provide incentives to the UEs.

*4) Computing Resource Management:* FL-based computing resource management has emerged as a prominent area of research, encompassing tasks such as computing resource scaling/provisioning, network slice placement, network slice orchestration, VM anomaly detection, and cache management. Similar to networking resource management, many studies have employed federated DRL for effective sequential decision-making, while some still rely on predictive models for future demand to guide heuristic-based resource management. However, as the application of FL in these areas is still in its infancy, considerations for FL management and diverse FL methods remain limited. Exploratory work is being done on DFL approaches to facilitate decentralized FL processes without a centralized parameter server. Nonetheless, given DFL's sensitivity to aggregation methods, further investigation into convergence issues with varying aggregation methods is necessary. ## VI. RESEARCH CHALLENGES AND DIRECTIONS

<span id="page-27-0"></span>While FL holds significant progress for distributed training in 5G and beyond networks, several key challenges must be addressed to ensure its effective deployment and operation in the context of mobile network management. We consider these challenges across five distinct dimensions such as practical deployment, scalability, privacy and security, OPEX, and FRL issues as presented in Table [X.](#page-26-0) In summary, while FL has tremendous potential, it is essential to address these challenges comprehensively to fully realize its benefits in the context of mobile network management for 5G and beyond networks.

# *A. Practical Deployment Issues*

*1) NWDAF for FL-Empowered Mobile Network Management:* In the 5G core, the 3GPP standardization body specifies the interfaces and procedures to support NWDAF for AI-empowered mobile network management, as exemplified by subscription requests and analytics information response. Nevertheless, a detailed procedure is yet to be specified to support interoperability among multiple NWDAFs for FL. Furthermore, for implementation concerns, to efficiently manage various NWDAFs for FL, the interface for acquiring information regarding the physical location of NWDAFs and computation/storage capacity should be carefully defined. Moreover, as discussed in [\[1\]](#page-31-16), most conventional studies cover horizontal FL, in which different sample datasets with the same feature space are used for local training. However, because the NWDAF collects various types of data from different NFs for a specified user (i.e., the same sample datasets with varying features), vertical FL can be widely applied to the 5G core. For example, the first dataset can include registration and authentication information, whereas the second dataset can contain information related to each user's choice of network slice. Nevertheless, most studies need to address the open problem of vertical FL in AI-empowered mobile network management, which could be an important research direction. In another research direction, the 5G core must effectively support AI services between user equipment and application servers. In this direction, an investigation of 5G system support for AI/ML-based Services (FS AIMLsys) was undertaken in 3GPP Release 18 [\[201\]](#page-35-40), [\[202\]](#page-36-0). This study aimed to leverage a 5G system as a platform by providing intelligent transmission support for AI/ML applications [\[202\]](#page-36-0). In this context, [\[75\]](#page-33-4) proposed FL participant selection utilizing NWDAF support, where trajectory prediction is considered.

<span id="page-27-2"></span><span id="page-27-1"></span>*2) Explainable AI for FL-Empowered Mobile Network Management:* Due to the lack of explainability, transparency, and trust inherited from black-box AI models, service providers are concerned with deploying AI-empowered mobile network management systems [\[203\]](#page-36-1). For instance, service providers may use AI models to make complex resource management decisions with financial and service-quality implications. Such critical issues open up a new AI approach

<span id="page-27-5"></span><span id="page-27-4"></span><span id="page-27-3"></span>that is required to make understandable and explainable decisions. In this regard, various XAI techniques have been studied to examine the decisions made by AI black box models by calculating the attributes of every participating feature. In particular, XAI techniques have gained popularity in certain domains, such as driving, image recognition, and speech analysis [\[204\]](#page-36-2). Nevertheless, there is a need for further studies in the mobile communication domain that consider XAI techniques that provide explainability in their proposed solutions. The work of [\[205\]](#page-36-3) first outlined the necessity for explainability in forthcoming 6G networks and then summarized the envisioned XAI for handover and resource allocation problems. The study of [\[206\]](#page-36-4) provides a comparative analysis of various XAI methods, such as XGBoost and SHapley Additive eXplanation (SHAP), to identify the root cause of SLA violations. The authors of [\[207\]](#page-36-5) illustrated a vast range of XAI metrics for qualitative assessments to ensure trustworthiness. Therefore, further research must be conducted on XAI-enabled mobile network management by considering distributed and isolated network slicing in 5G and 6G networks. Particularly, because the operator/slice tenant must understand the FL model's behavior at specific times (e.g., busy hours) to trust it for practical deployment, XAI-empowered FL for mobile network management can be a crucial research direction for efficiently handling dispersed network slices and helping any business/organization provide secure operation and trust when deploying AI models [\[206\]](#page-36-4), [\[208\]](#page-36-6).

### <span id="page-27-7"></span><span id="page-27-6"></span>*B. Scalability Issues*

<span id="page-27-8"></span>*1) Hybrid Federated and Centralized Learning for AI-Empowered Mobile Network Management:* As already discussed in the extensive survey, the FL framework has been widely adopted and has opened up prospects of application for AI-empowered mobile network management by providing benefits for overcoming the considerable overhead of transmitting distributed local datasets to a single centralized node for network management. However, FL requires significant computational resources and sufficient datasets from distributed network entities (e.g., mobile devices, IoT devices, and virtualized NFs over distributed MEC servers). In practice, not all network entities have sufficient computational resources or datasets for local training to participate in the FL process. The heterogeneity of such resources and datasets may result in poor convergence speed and degradation of model accuracy. Recently, as an alternative method to address these issues, HFCL approaches have been studied as novel architectures for FL frameworks that are yet to be applied to network management [\[9\]](#page-31-21), [\[200\]](#page-35-41), [\[209\]](#page-36-7). Consequently, under the assumption of relieving privacy concerns according to the scenarios, only network nodes with sufficient computational resources participate in the FL process, whereas the remaining nodes send their local datasets to the centralized node for centralized learning. As shown in Fig. [16,](#page-28-0) HFCL utilizes both centralized learning and FL by designating clients as active or inactive, depending on their computational capability. The work of [\[209\]](#page-36-7) is considered a client for conducting centralized learning and FL simultaneously. Therefore, the

*2) Decentralized Federated Learning for Mobile Network Management:* DFL, such as gossip learning, represents a fully decentralized learning paradigm that eliminates the need for a centralized parameter server, a requirement in conventional FL. The evident advantages of DFL lie in its infrastructurefree nature and the absence of a single point of failure [\[210\]](#page-36-8). By adopting such decentralized frameworks, mobile network management can achieve full decentralization, resulting in cost-effective scalability and improved robustness. For example, in a UAV-assisted MEC scenario [\[35\]](#page-32-6), deploying the FL framework without relying on a centralized MEC server on the ground is possible. Moreover, even distributed MEC servers on the ground, located far from a centralized parameter server, can collaboratively generate a global model in a P2P manner using DFL. Despite the numerous benefits offered by DFL, achieving higher accuracy and faster convergence compared to CFL is more challenging due to the phenomenon of client drift. Researchers have explored various solutions to enhance convergence speed and accuracy in DFL, including aggregation management, clustering, knowledge distillation, mutual knowledge transfer, and others [\[211\]](#page-36-9), [\[212\]](#page-36-10). However, designing an efficient DFL framework for mobile network management remains an open and critical research direction that demands further attention. The distributed and heterogeneous nature of network entities adds complexity to this problem, making it both challenging and promising for future investigations.

<span id="page-28-2"></span>*3) SFL for Mobile Network Management:* To alleviate the computational burden on participants in FL, an innovative

<span id="page-28-1"></span><span id="page-28-0"></span>technique known as split learning (SL) has emerged as a practical solution. SL involves dividing a full deep learning model into two sub-models, which can be trained both at a central server and across multiple distributed SL participants. While SL offers substantial advantages, its sequential training approach can lead to inefficient distributed processing and slow convergence. In response to these challenges, a novel approach called split federated learning, or simply SFL, has recently been introduced. SFL seeks to leverage the strengths of both FL and SL by introducing an additional federated learning server and allowing SFL participants to train their local models in parallel. As shown in Fig. [17,](#page-29-0) the SFL framework includes two key servers: i) a federated learning server (referred to as the fed server) and ii) a main server. In addition, there are multiple clients, known as SFL participants. The fundamental structure of this framework involves the division of the full model into two distinct sub-models such as i) the client-side model, and ii) the server-side model. At each global epoch, each SFL participant performs forward propagation on its local dataset using the client-side model. Subsequently, the SFL participants pass the smashed data and corresponding labels to the main server. The main server then undertakes forward propagation using the server-side model, followed by back-propagation from computing the loss, all of which can be executed in parallel. Following this step, the server-side model is updated utilizing the specific aggregation algorithm, and the main server transmits the gradient of the smashed data back to each client for updating their respective client-side local models. Subsequently, the fed server receives the updated client-side local models from all clients and performs an aggregation operation to generate the client-side global model. Finally, the fed server transmits

the updated client-side global model to all clients, facilitating the synchronization of client-side models across the entire SFL framework. This innovative approach shows significant promise for various applications [\[213\]](#page-36-11), [\[214\]](#page-36-12). SFL can offer efficient methods for training deep learning models on NFs with limited computational power. This can be achieved by alleviating the computational burden on NFs while simultaneously preserving their privacy. Correspondingly, these mechanisms can be applied to various aspects of mobile network management, encompassing mobile traffic and mobility prediction, SFL-based modulation recognition applications, and the efficient allocation and management of radio and computing resources in both core and access networks. One particularly promising application of SFL in mobile network management is its ability to support distributed training in scenarios involving MEC-assisted UAVs. In such cases, SFL plays a critical role in reducing the training workload on local UAVs, which often face constraints related to battery capacity and computing power. Moreover, for efficient SFL management, various methods for selecting cut layers and implementing differential privacy within SFL can be investigated to optimize performance factors such as privacy, latency, and energy efficiency in the context of mobile network management.

# *C. Privacy and Security Issues*

*1) Privacy and Confidentiality Preservation:* FL is widely recognized for safeguarding the privacy of each FL participant by conducting local model training without exposing raw data and exchanging only updated model parameters. Nevertheless, recent concerns have emerged regarding the potential for local models to disclose sensitive information during communications, leaving them vulnerable to adversaries or third parties <span id="page-29-1"></span>who may try to extract raw data [\[6\]](#page-31-15). This concern becomes particularly critical in scenarios like mobile network management, where shared infrastructures host multiple MVNOs. Due to the increased adoption of high-frequency networks like 5G and beyond, multiple MVNOs often deploy and share infrastructures to overcome limited BS coverage. For efficient resource allocation and network management policy, these MVNOs can collaborate through the FL process. To ensure privacy and security, robust security solutions such as differential privacy and collaborative training should be applied in FL-empowered mobile network management [\[6\]](#page-31-15). This challenge is amplified in UAV-aided MEC systems, where model updates from UAVs are transmitted via wireless channels. Nie et al. [\[35\]](#page-32-6) proposed the use of Gaussian differential encryption to protect the uploading messages during local model exchanges from UAVs. However, adopting such robust security measures sacrifices model accuracy and requires significant computation from FL participants. It is crucial to strike a well-balanced tradeoff between privacy guarantee and system performance while implementing FL-empowered mobile network management, especially considering the limited computation resources available to FL participants who are MEC servers or BSs serving other service requests from NFs or UEs.

*2) Poisonous or Noisy Model Issues:* Malicious BSs or MEC servers pose a threat by potentially injecting poisoned or noisy models into an FL server. Such instances may occur if some of the BSs or MEC servers are infected with viruses while participating in the FL process. Additionally, model poisoning can also arise from distributed Denial-of-Service (DoS) and jamming attacks conducted by attackers aiming to degrade mobile network management performance [\[6\]](#page-31-15). The vulnerability is particularly pronounced during local model updates via wireless channels, such as in UAV-assisted MEC <span id="page-30-0"></span>server scenarios, due to the exposed nature of the wireless medium, which facilitates easier model poisoning compared to wired mediums. To mitigate this issue, anti-jamming schemes like frequency hopping, involving the transmission of multiple copies of model updates over different frequencies, can be adopted. Moreover, blockchain technology can be leveraged, as demonstrated in [\[215\]](#page-36-13), to audit all local updates during the FL process, thereby preventing malicious FL participants. The integrity of records on the blockchain ensures that any malicious activities of FL participants can be traced back, as the records are untampered. In this context, the protection mechanisms for FL-empowered mobile network mechanisms should be designed aiming for a balanced tradeoff between model accuracy and system performance.

### *D. OPEX Issues*

*1) Energy-Efficient FL-Empowered Mobile Network Management:* Energy efficiency is a critical metric for costeffective AI-empowered mobile network management, which reduces the OPEX of service providers. While conventional optimization algorithms, such as the matching theory or other heuristics, require high computational complexity and result in latency, the computational complexity of datadriven AI algorithms is transferred from online optimization to offline training. Therefore, the energy consumption on the network side rapidly increases as the size of the AI model increases (i.e., as the number of variables to be predicted increases). Thus, an energy-efficient FL framework can be designed to cost-effectively automate mobile network management using AI technologies by considering systemwide issues to improve energy efficiency. During the FL process, the overall energy consumption involves three main components: i) Computational energy consumption by FL participants (e.g., mobile devices, MEC servers) during local training. ii) Transmission energy consumption for delivering local model and global model updates. iii) Computational energy consumption for model aggregation. In this context, energy consumption for model aggregation is relatively small. To effectively reduce computational energy consumption during local training and transmission energy, sophisticated FL management techniques should be designed. These may include dynamic control of local iterations, FL participant selection, and more, aiming to decrease the OPEX of service providers. For example, increasing the number of local iterations may result in a smaller number of required rounds for convergence. However, this comes at the expense of higher energy consumption and latency per round. Balancing the transmission energy and computational energy is essential for minimizing the total energy consumption through sophisticated management strategies. By finding the right balance, energy-efficient FL processes can be achieved. However, most conventional studies on energy-efficient FL frameworks have focused on reducing the power consumption of mobile devices with limited batteries. There is a lack of research on energy-efficient FL frameworks for mobile network management on a system-wide scale. This gap exists because most studies focus on energy consumption issues <span id="page-30-1"></span>in scenarios with limited battery power, such as mobile devices. However, it is important to recognize that energy efficiency concerns in mobile network management remain significant for reducing OPEX when achieving network management automation. Furthermore, third-generation neural network models can also be utilized to improve the energy efficiency in the training phase, rather than using conventional deep neural networks. Recently, spiking neural networks (SNNs), which use discrete spikes to transmit information and are similar to the information-processing mechanisms of the human brain, have been suggested as energy-efficient alternatives to deep neural networks [\[216\]](#page-36-14), [\[217\]](#page-36-15), [\[218\]](#page-36-16). From an application perspective, as the FL process involves multiple distributed nodes (e.g., NFs, mobile devices, and BSs) for AI-empowered mobile network management, there is vast scope for leveraging the energy efficiency provided by SNNs. Correspondingly, [\[30\]](#page-32-1), [\[219\]](#page-36-17) applied an SNN to the FL framework. In the first study on this issue, the authors of [\[219\]](#page-36-17) designed an FL framework for the decentralized and privacy-preserving training of SNNs. Furthermore, [\[30\]](#page-32-1) proposed a decentralized energy-efficient computing method based on SNN that enables edge devices to exploit brain-like biophysiological structures to collaboratively train a global model in the FL process. They revealed that adopting SNN reduces energy consumption by more than 4.5 times compared with the standard FL process. Despite its importance as an early investigation stage for SNN-based FL frameworks, training SNNs in FL-empowered mobile network management has not been researched extensively.

<span id="page-30-2"></span>*2) Accuracy Loss Correction and Model Compression:* Because of the distributed and heterogeneous NFs connected via wired and wireless links, it is essential to train FL models over NFs cost-effectively by considering accuracy loss correction and model compression methods that can be applied to wired and wireless connected NFs or MDs. In this regard, some studies on wireless transmission environments have investigated the theory of accuracy loss correction and model compression (i.e., network model compression and parameter compression) [\[220\]](#page-36-18). Thus, various accuracy loss corrections and model compressions must be further investigated for the FL framework over NFs by considering specific reliability constraints that depend on the mobile network management scenario.

# <span id="page-30-3"></span>*E. Federated Reinforcement Learning for Mobile Network Management*

RL has been widely adopted in sequential mobile network management decision-making problems such as handover management, cache management, radio resource management, and computation resource scaling. However, implementing RL in practical scenarios poses several challenges. For example, when the action and state spaces are large, the performance of agents is affected by the collected samples. This is because it is nearly impossible to explore all sampling space. Therefore, promoting information exchange among multiple agents while protecting agent privacy is necessary for implementing RL in the real world. These challenges have led to FRL, in which FL and RL are integrated under a secure agent model exchange. FRL can be classified into horizontal and vertical FRL, depending on the distribution characteristics of the data, similar to the data partitioning for FL [\[221\]](#page-36-19). The horizontal FRL can be applied in scenarios where agents may be distributed geographically; however, they face similar decision-making tasks and have very little interaction with each other in observed environments, such as autonomous driving decisionmaking model generation. In a horizontal FRL, agents share their experiences by exchanging masked models to enhance sample efficiency and accelerate the learning process. In this regard, [\[222\]](#page-36-20) proposed FRL-based computation offloading management for IoT systems, where IoT devices train the RL model, and the updated RL models can be aggregated at the edge server by utilizing the FL framework. In contrast, in the vertical FRL, samples of multiple data sets have different feature spaces, but these samples may belong to the same groups or common users. The role of FL in the vertical FRL is to aggregate the partial features observed by various agents. Currently, there are limited studies on vertical FRL compared with horizontal FRL. Therefore, in addition to the horizontal FRL, vertical FRL for mobile network management needs to be further investigated, considering the specific feature integration, such as state/observation and action spaces, of the agents in the mobile network management scenario.

<span id="page-31-26"></span>This article has presented a tutorial on FL and a comprehensive study of the challenges involved in FL-empowered mobile network management. This work was motivated by the need for a comprehensive survey on the use of FL for 5G and beyond mobile network management. To bridge this gap after reviewing the state of the art in FL, we have explained the rationale for AI-empowered mobile network management, and how FL can enable cooperative model training for mobile network management at both access and core networks, including distributed NWDAF, mobile traffic or other predictions, and core/access network management, in terms of standardization and research activities. Several main lessons learned from this extensive survey have been summarized and analyzed. Lastly, the paper has explored future research areas and challenges.
