#### Abstract

Future internet aims to function as a neutral in-network storage and computation platform, essential for enabling 6G and beyond wireless use cases. Information-Centric Networking and Edge Computing are key paradigms driving this vision by offering diversified services with fast response times across heterogeneous networks. This approach requires effective coordination to dynamically utilize resources like links, storage, and computation in near real-time within a non-homogenous and distributed computing environment. Additionally, networks must be aware of resource availability and reputational information to manage unknown and partially observed dynamic systems, ensuring the desired Quality of Experience (QoE). This paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of edge computing technologies, starting with an introduction to its architectural frameworks. We examine contemporary research on essential aspects such as resource allocation, computation delegation, data administration, and network management, highlighting existing research gaps. Furthermore, we explore the synergy between edge computing and 5G, and discuss advancements in 6G that enhance solutions through edge computing. Our study emphasizes the importance of integrating edge computing in future considerations, particularly regarding sustainable energy and standards.

## <span id="page-1-0"></span>1. Introduction

Cisco forecasts that the global number of connected devices will surpass 75 billion by the year 2025 (see [Fig.](#page-1-1) [1\)](#page-1-1). Cisco predicts that the amount of devices connected to the Internet will reach 500 billion by 2030 [\[1](#page-18-4)]. A centralized cloud system by itself will not have the capability to efficiently handle upcoming networks [\[2](#page-18-5)[–4](#page-18-6)]. In this setting, there will be a vast number of devices that are outfitted with sensors and designed to gather massive quantities of data. These devices will then use this data to make inferences and carry out certain actions. The transfer of substantial volumes of data from interconnected devices to the cloud for processing will result in a highly congested network infrastructure [[5–](#page-18-7)[9\]](#page-18-8). Furthermore, the bidirectional movement of data between the cloud and specific devices leads to an increase in latency. A multitude of emerging technologies, including autonomous vehicles, telemedicine procedures, Augmented Reality/Virtual Reality (AR/VR), high-resolution 8 K video, sophisticated manufacturing robots and drone surveillance systems, necessitate instantaneous and extremely little delay in performance [[8,](#page-18-9)[10](#page-18-10),[11\]](#page-18-11). The current status of the Fifth-Generation (5G) network will become even more important if Sixth-Generation (6G) networks are introduced. Considering the updated requirements, networks (5G/6G) will require bands with higher frequencies and decreased latency in comparison to 5G networks [\[12](#page-18-12)[–14\]](#page-18-13). Moreover, the implementation of more resilient Edge Computing (EC) will help address the needs for bandwidth, privacy and autonomy. The rapid digital transformation and increased adoption of cloud and edge technologies are leading to significant changes in WAN (Wide Area Network) edge architecture [[15\]](#page-18-14). These changes are shaping the strategies about infrastructure and operations in the next future networks. Furthermore, EC

#### <span id="page-2-1"></span>*2.1. Edge computing*

The growth of Internet traffic has been exponential and is gaining new momentum on account of enhanced mobile broadband, IoT, and mission-critical applications. The number of mobile Internet users is projected to increase from 3.8 billion in 2019 to 5 billion by 2025. According to Cisco's forecast, there will be more than 500 billion devices connected to the Internet by 2030 [\[1](#page-18-4)]. Moreover, emerging applications, such as remote surgery, self-driving vehicles, AR/VR, 8 K video, drone surveillance communication and advanced robotics in manufacturing, which require high reliability, ultra-low latency and high bandwidth will add to the burden. [Fig.](#page-3-4) [3](#page-3-4) illustrates some tasks based on wireless networks. McKinsey predicts that network performance demands would grow ten times

Even with 5G and beyond wireless networks – all of which entail costly infrastructure investments [\[28](#page-19-3),[29\]](#page-19-4) – the same latency and congestion problems will persist due to the exponential growth in traffic and devices. #### <span id="page-3-0"></span>*2.1.1. Solutions based on edge*

Solutions based on edge enable data processing to be at the gateway or device level. This leads to minimize the necessity of transferring data between data center and/or the cloud, hence reducing bandwidth demands, latency and reliance on connectivity. In addition to technical considerations, decentralized computing offers energy efficiency by reducing the electricity and cooling expenses associated with largescale data centers. The motivation behind research on EC stems from concerns over security and privacy associated with the centralized cloud, as seen by states, corporations, and consumers [\[30](#page-19-5)[–35\]](#page-19-6). Simultaneously, the decreasing costs of compute and storage, along with the increasing prevalence of Machine Learning (ML), are driving the implementation of EC. As per the International Data Corporation (IDC), at least 40% from the generated data by the IoT will be analyzed, processed, acted and kept upon in nearby to, or directly at, the network edge. The systems commonly referred to as EC encompass cloudlets [\[36](#page-19-7)], Cyber Foraging [\[37](#page-19-8)], Fog Computing [\[38](#page-19-9)] and Mobile Edge Computing (MEC).

The advancement of this computing paradigm has undergone multiple phases since 1990. [Fig.](#page-4-4) [4](#page-4-4) demonstrates that every step had a direct or indirect impact on the notion and altered the perception of EC [[39](#page-19-10)[,40](#page-19-11)].

#### <span id="page-3-1"></span>*2.1.2. Content delivery networks (CDNs)*

A team of researchers at MIT developed content delivery networks (CDNs) to address the flash crowd problem, which occurs when a single server is not able to handle a high volume of requests [[41](#page-19-12)[,42](#page-19-13)]. Scientists from MIT suggest duplicating the content on multiple strategically distributed edge servers. Furthermore, CDNs were the initial technology that could provide storage resources at the network edges. Over the past ten years, the utilization of CDNs has gained significant popularity among website owners. They store their data (HTML, images, Java-Scripts) with a CDN provider to enhance the web experience for their consumers.

### <span id="page-3-2"></span>*2.1.3. Ubiquitous computing*

Ubiquitous computing, often referred to as "pervasive computing," is a paradigm that envisions the integration of computing capabilities into every aspect of daily life. The fundamental idea is to embed computing devices and technologies seamlessly into the surrounding environment, making them an intrinsic part of our everyday activities. This concept transcends traditional computing models, emphasizing the creation of an interconnected network of smart devices that collaborate to enhance efficiency, convenience, and user experience. In a ubiquitous computing environment, devices communicate with each other, collect data from the surroundings, and adapt their functionality to suit the context. This pervasive integration aims to create a more natural and user-friendly computing experience, where technology fades into the background, becoming an invisible enabler of tasks and interactions in both personal and professional spheres. As technology advances, ubiquitous computing continues to shape the future of human– computer interaction, fostering a world where computing is seamlessly interwoven with the fabric of our daily lives.

#### <span id="page-3-3"></span>*2.1.4. P2P*

Peer-to-peer (P2P) refers to a decentralized network architecture that enables direct communication and resource sharing between individual computers, or peers, without the need for a central server [\[16](#page-18-15)]. In a P2P network, each node has equal status, and participants can both contribute and consume resources. This approach contrasts with traditional client–server models, offering increased resilience, scalability, and reduced dependency on a single point of failure. P2P networks are commonly associated with file sharing, where users can exchange digital files directly between their devices. However, the concept extends to various applications, including communication, distributed computing, and decentralized cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin. P2P systems empower users by fostering a more inclusive and collaborative digital environment, promoting the efficient sharing of information and resources across a distributed network [\[43](#page-19-14)].

#### <span id="page-4-4"></span><span id="page-4-0"></span>*2.1.5. Cloud computing*

Cloud computing is a transformative paradigm in information technology that revolutionizes the way individuals and organizations access and manage digital resources. In essence, it involves the delivery of computing services-such as the capacity of storage, processing power, and applicationsover the internet, with users accessing these resources on a pay-as-you-go basis. Cloud computing eliminates the need for organizations to invest in and maintain extensive physical infrastructure, allowing them to leverage the capabilities of remote data centers [\[44](#page-19-15)]. This model offers scalability, flexibility, and cost-effectiveness, as users can scale their computing resources up or down based on demand. Key service models within cloud computing include Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS), and Software as a Service (SaaS). Whether for individuals storing files in the cloud or enterprises running complex applications, cloud computing has become an integral part of the modern digital landscape, enabling efficient collaboration, innovation, and resource optimization.

#### <span id="page-4-1"></span>*2.1.6. Cloudlet*

A cloudlet is a concept in cloud computing that refers to a small-scale data center or a specialized computing node located at the edge of a network, closer to end-users and devices [\[36](#page-19-7),[45\]](#page-19-16). Positioned between mobile devices and larger cloud infrastructure, cloudlets aim to reduce latency and enhance the performance of real-time applications by bringing computing resources closer to the point of need. Acting as an intermediary layer, cloudlets facilitate offloading computational tasks from mobile devices to improve efficiency, response time, and conserve device resources. This approach is particularly beneficial for applications requiring low latency, such as AR/VR, and other time-sensitive tasks. Cloudlets represent a distributed computing model that complements the broader cloud infrastructure, providing a more responsive and adaptive computing environment for emerging EC applications [\[46](#page-19-17)].

#### <span id="page-4-2"></span>*2.1.7. Mobile edge computing*

The term MEC has lately been substituted with Multi-Access Edge Computing. The European Telecommunication Standards Institute (ETSI) introduced Multi-Access Edge Computing in 2014, specifically targeting Virtual Machine (VM) technologies and mobile networks [\[47](#page-19-18)]. In 2017, its range was broadened to include other virtualization technologies such as

non-mobile network needs. The initial concept aimed to extend cloud-computing features to the edges of mobile networks. In addition, the solution is based on RAN by deploying MEC servers at Long-Term Evolution (LTE)/LTE-A macro base station (eNodeB) sites, multi-Radio Access Technology (RAT) sites and 3G Radio Network Controller (RNC) sites. The ETSI project was tasked with defining the Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) that link the mobile edge platform and applications to encourage innovation in an open environment.

#### <span id="page-4-3"></span>*2.1.8. Fog computing*

The concept of Fog computing was pioneered by Cisco in 2012. It can be seen as expansion of the cloud computing model that encompasses both the central infrastructure and the network's periphery [[48\]](#page-19-19). Therefore, in contrast to MEC, fog computing is closely connected to the cloud and cannot function independently. Consequently, there has been particular emphasis on the communication between the cloud and fog [\[49](#page-19-20)[–51](#page-19-21)]. In contrast to MEC, which is often implemented at a fixed location such as a base station, fog nodes have the flexibility to be installed in many locations that have a network connection, such as the factory floor, on top of a power pole, railway tracks, vehicles, and so on. Cisco provides application platforms that streamline the creation of fog applications, along with Cisco Fog Data Services for data analytics [\[52](#page-19-22)]. Simultaneously, major Internet corporations are deploying edge infrastructure. Facebook is constructing smallscale data centers tailored for various applications' types and workloads. The company of Amazon has introduced Amazon Web Services (AWS) Greengrass, which enables developers to offload certain operations to the device itself. Additionally, there are companies such as EdgeConneX and vXchnge that identify themselves as edge companies. These companies are actively constructing networks of urban data centers. One example is Vapor IO, a firm that creates micro-centers that can be installed in any location.

The majority of the suggested applications incur the expense of deploying supplementary infrastructure, either by putting edge devices at individual fixed sites (such as factories or shopping malls) or throughout particular geographical regions (such as metropolitan centers). Therefore, the scalability is adversely impacted by the substantial rise in the number of individuals conducting transactions within a certain edge domain. Our proposed method offers increased scalability as adoption increases. Put simply, with a large number of users needing to do storage and computing tasks on the network. A significant increase in computational speed, which will enable the emergence of a genuinely global network, is anticipated.

## <span id="page-5-0"></span>*2.2. Literature review*

The emergence of edge-computing brings up new opportunities to balance the trade-off between distributed network architectures and centralized [[53\]](#page-19-23). The literature on edge systems has largely focused on service time delay and to a lesser extent there is some literature, which takes into consideration application specifications. Research has also highlighted the need of coordination between cloud computing and EC [[54–](#page-19-24) [56\]](#page-19-25). [[55\]](#page-19-26) highlights decision-making on what actions to perform locally and what actions to be sent to the cloud. The study centers on utilizing network-wide knowledge and historical data stored at the cloud center to direct EC units in meeting diverse performance needs of different wireless IoT networks. There is a lack of scientific understanding on how different edge architectures impact system efficiency and performance, especially in terms of overall service time. Moreover, there is limited understanding of how the different offloading strategies can impact end-to-end performance of IoT applications and services, especially, when resource patterns and task characteristics are dynamic [\[57](#page-19-27)].

A majority of the literature on the EC environment focuses on minimizing service time, in particular, computational delay and communication delay as well as with other objectives such as cost and energy. In [\[58](#page-19-28)], an optimization framework of offloading from a single mobile device (MD) has been proposed to several edge devices to minimize the computational latency and power consumption of MDs. [\[59](#page-19-29)] propose low-complexity suboptimal algorithm to solve the computation offloading problem in a mixed fog/cloud system by jointly optimizing the offloading decisions and the allocation of computation resource, transmit power, and radio bandwidth while guaranteeing acceptable computational delay. In [\[60](#page-19-30)], authors design an optimal task scheduling policy to reduce the overall latency taking into consideration the average each task delay and the power consumption average at the mobile device. The computation tasks are scheduled based on the states of queuing of the task buffer, transmission unit and execution of the local processing unit. [\[61](#page-19-31)] proposes a method with a dual focus on computation and communication elements to minimize service delay in a scenario with two cloudlet servers. Processing delay is controlled via VM migration and improving transmission delay. The work proposed in [[62\]](#page-19-32) presents algorithms to reduce latency when using unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to connect mobile devices to the fog node and the fog node to the remote data centre. Authors take into consideration both transmission power, processing frequencies of mobile devices and the fog node.

[[63\]](#page-19-33) proposes an edge simulated framework approach to allocate sufficient resources to network function virtualization (NFV) enabled nodes on time to support latency-sensitive multimedia applications by guaranteeing latency requirements under changing workload and reduced operational cost. In [[64\]](#page-19-34), authors propose a strategy to minimize task completion time with an efficient task scheduling and resource management strategy. A fog computing supported software-defined embedded system is considered where task images lay in the storage server while computations can be conducted on either embedded device or a computation server. [[65\]](#page-20-0) proposes a scheduling method to address the multi-workflow scheduling problem with proximity constraint in the EC environment. The primary objective is to reduce expenses. The investigation was conducted using both experimental and real-world datasets. [\[66](#page-20-1)] concentrated on dynamic resource allocation in an edge context for IoT systems. Finally, a reinforcement learning has been applied in this study to establish a policy for allocating resources.

There is also some literature focusing on the different applications' types that are supported by edge- cloud system [\[67](#page-20-2)]. [[68\]](#page-20-3) focuses on the optimal application deployment in term of resource-constrained distributed edges. It has a goal to determine the deployment issues of microservice-based applications in edge networks. The application deployment cost has been minimized based on a new strategy was, while considering resource and performance constraints. [\[69](#page-20-4)] proposes an approach taking into consideration the type of application based on allocating the offloading tasks to suitable resources in the edge-cloud system to overcome the issue of computational and communication delay. [[45\]](#page-19-16) proposes a strategy of selecting the edge node as a target based on application specification with an eye to reduce power consumption and delay. In [\[70](#page-20-5)] authors propose a two-stage approach for identifying the ideal solution for Distributed Stream Processing (DSP) applications in EC. This strategy aims to tackle the joint operator scaling and identify the issue. The goal is to provide increased cost efficiency while taking into account user-specified QoS limitations. The study was conducted using real-world DSP test scenarios as the basis for experiments. The study referenced by [\[71](#page-20-6)] examines data-intensive applications that are implemented using numerous service components at the edge. The approach was developed using the Genetic Algorithm (DSEGA). Five algorithms were given to get an ideal deployment plan. Most recently, there has been a growing body of research on edge intelligence. [[72\]](#page-20-7) discusses how AI solutions can contribute to address EC problems.

An increasing amount of research is currently centered on utilizing user equipment (UE) as resources and the connectivity among user equipment to process tasks. Therefore, the end-user stratum and edge stratum have been combined. Collaboration is a key aspect in literature. The authors in [\[73](#page-20-8)] suggest a concept in which mobile devices collaborate to create "mobile clouds" for doing tasks within a local network. "Transient clouds" were proposed in [\[74](#page-20-9)] as a collaborative computing platform in which neighboring devices create an ad-hoc network to offer different capabilities as cloud services. The study in [\[75](#page-20-10)] introduced a resource sharing technique that leverages mobile devices via opportunistic interactions to highlight resource aspects of mobile cloud computing. A different publication, in [[76\]](#page-20-11), concentrates on VM technology to utilize the whole potential of local hardware at the peripheries of the Internet. Alternatively, an adaptive resource discovery strategy is suggested by [\[77](#page-20-12)] to handle service provisioning in opportunistic computing settings, managing increased requests without generating instability as discussed in [\[78](#page-20-13)]. The architecture proposed by [[79\]](#page-20-14), known as Vehicular Fog Computing (VFC) for vehicular applications, utilizes vehicles as edge infrastructure.

Caching at the edge: Edge servers have a key advantage of bringing storage capability from the source to the edge. The literature provides various caching strategies at the edge based on popularity or re-play possibility of multimedia streams. In [\[80](#page-20-15)], authors propose video segment caching at the network through users' announcements. [\[81](#page-20-16)] proposes an edge-based server caching scheme considering context-aware RAN condition, video popularity and the Channel Quality Index (CQI) informed by the UE. These works basically utilize only the edge server the users are associated with. There are also a number of cooperative solutions that utilize more than one edge server. [[82\]](#page-20-17) proposes cooperative mobilityaware hierarchical caching among edge servers. [\[83](#page-20-18)] presents an optimization for DASH-based QoE-aware edge caching placement through coordination among edges. [[84\]](#page-20-19) proposes a joint QoE traffic optimization with collaborative edge caching through adjustments of backhaul and fronthaul traffic ratio parameters. Although an important portion of time, energy saving and backhaul traffic reduction can be achieved via caching, the proposed solutions do not utilize any caching strategy considering popularity or replay possibility of any of the segments.

Pre-fetching at the edge involves many methods for prefetching streams on either the user equipment (UE) or the network side. Pre-fetching methods on the UE-side involve social networking suggestions [[85\]](#page-20-20), downloading multiple segments/chunks simultaneously using multi-path Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) citeE38 and a recommendation system that forecasts videos likely to be seen by users [\[86](#page-20-21)]. Userside pre-fetching techniques may not adequately account for the entire journey from the RAN to the cloud, thereby leading to suboptimal TCP performance. On the network-side, pre-fetching methods involve using pre-fetching proxies at access points (APs) [\[87](#page-20-22)] or cellular BSs [[88\]](#page-20-23) with specific pre-defined policies, such as segment-based pre-fetching. Another approach is pre-fetching segments from the cloud to the MEC server to stay ahead of the user's request progress [[89\]](#page-20-24). However, these studies do not take into account collaborative pre-fetching among network entities/edges. Therefore, implementing pre-fetching on just one network edge may not function effectively and could potentially harm the QoE in crowded networks because of potential buffer overflows.

Prospects and challenges of information-centric networking (ICN) architectures: In recent years, information-centric networking (ICN) architectures, in particular, Named Data networking (NDN), have emerged to mitigate traffic congestion and prioritize critical messages in both wired and wireless networks [[90,](#page-20-25)[91](#page-20-26)]. NDN has advantages over traditional IP networks due to its delay-tolerant and content-centric features and can deliver traffic over a disrupted and highly dynamic network environment [\[92](#page-20-27)]. However, when using NDN, there is a significant overhead and depending on traffic congestion and application type, throughput can be up to 30% lower compared to TCP/IP data [\[93](#page-20-28)]. Yet, it is still useful architecture to prioritize critical traffic over the network, especially the multimedia flows. For example, in a network scenario where there is TCP, background and multimedia traffic on the same line, multimedia can be prioritized making use of the NDN. There is a growing body of work that applies the NDN concept to EC due to its intrinsic qualities. Most significantly, NDN allows for computation to be executed remotely by any node in the network that supports the required compute capabilities making the client and the network agnostic of the list of providers and their corresponding IP mapping [[94\]](#page-20-29). However, there are a number of challenges in an NDN design driven communication including enabling compute reuse, mobility management, and ensuring secure and trustworthy task offloading [[94\]](#page-20-29).

Reputation-based trust in NDN: Commonly, cryptographic signatures and certificates are utilized to establish trust, also known as credential-based [[95\]](#page-20-30) or policy-based [[96\]](#page-20-31) trust. Alternatively, a "reputation-based trust" approach has been put forth to mitigate cache- and PIT-centric attacks, and strengthen credential-based NDN security schemes. In a reputation based approach, agents decide whether to trust another agent or not by relying on evidence of past behavior supplied by trusted sources. However, the research focus on reputationbased schemes has been primarily limited to mitigating privacy and security attacks and other use-cases have not been thoroughly explored [[97](#page-20-32)]. This proposal leverages a reputationbased scheme for secure, reliable and low latency routing and distributing computation and storage.

# <span id="page-7-3"></span>*3.5. Security and privacy*

Due to the distinct nature of the network edge environment, EC security concerns are seen as one of its most significant obstacles. Data in cloud computing is housed in numerous expansive data centers that are meticulously safeguarded by robust physical security measures, including the presence of numerous guards, fortified fences, and stringent security regulations. Cloud providers are making significant investments in cybersecurity in addition to their focus on physical security. On the other hand, the circumstances in EC are distinct. The physical edge devices are widely spread out and varied, which increases their susceptibility to physical attacks. An example of such attacks is the injection of additional thermal load on the cooling systems, known as cooling system attacks. In addition, the enormous volume of data being offloaded and circulated at the network edge increases the susceptibility of edge servers to cyber vulnerabilities [\[101](#page-20-36),[102\]](#page-20-37). Due to the proximity of data storage to end-users, EC offers superior privacy protection compared to Cloud Computing.

#### <span id="page-8-1"></span>4. Resource allocation

<span id="page-8-2"></span>This section will provide a detailed explanation of resource allocation, namely in terms of link, capacity and computation. To ensure optimal user experience (QoE), resources should be provided promptly and in real-time. Extensive research has been conducted on this matter. The following concepts on resource allocation will be presented, along with illustrative case studies.

Within the research, the resource allocation problem in EC/MEC is commonly denoted as the task offloading, task scheduling and joint resource allocation problem (see [Fig.](#page-8-3) [6\)](#page-8-3). The task offloading problem involves determining the optimal decisions for what, how much and where to offload to edge servers or cloud [\[110](#page-21-2)]. On the other hand, the task scheduling problem involves choosing the appropriate computational resources, such as servers, that will be utilized to complete each task. The task allocation problem [\[111](#page-21-3)] involves the process of ranking and mapping each task to the most suitable computation resource. Furthermore, the term "task scheduling problem" is commonly has a relationship with joint resource allocation problem [\[112](#page-21-4)]. The term "joint resource allocation" commonly denotes the integration of offloading and scheduling task difficulties to efficiently offload and schedule an application's jobs to the most suitable cloud/edge servers [\[113](#page-21-5)]. Task offloading in MEC is a crucial aspect that involves the distribution of computational tasks between mobile devices and edge servers [[110](#page-21-2)[,113](#page-21-5)]. The goal is to optimize resource utilization, reduce latency, and enhance the overall performance of mobile applications. In addition, MEC extends cloud computing capabilities to the edge of the network, closer to mobile devices. To reach this goal task Offloading involves deciding whether to execute a task locally on the mobile device or offload it to an edge server. Furthermore, the decision to offload a task depends on factors like task characteristics, device capabilities and network conditions. However, offloading decisions may need to be dynamic, considering the varying workload and network conditions. For instance, let  $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$  be a binary variable indicating whether task i is offloaded ( $x_i = 1$ ) or executed locally ( $x_i = 0$ ). Such as the computation resource can be modeled as following:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i \cdot C_{task}(i) \le C_{device} \tag{1}$$

where  $C_{task}(i)$  is the computation resource required for task i and  $C_{device}$  is the total computation capacity of the mobile device. It is worth to mention that data size has also a huge importance in this context where:

$$D_{offload}(i) \le D_{max} \tag{2}$$

where  $D_{offload}(i)$  is the data size to be offloaded for task i, and  $D_{max}$  is the maximum allowed data size for offloading. Moreover, mobile devices often have limited computational power, storage and energy resources, which must be taken in consideration. Based on that, offloading tasks may lead also to increased data transmission, which leads to affect the network bandwidth. Nevertheless, certain applications require low latency, making it essential to minimize the communication delay between the mobile device and the edge server. The latency could be represented as following:

$$l = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_{local}(i) + T_{offload}(i)$$
(3)

where  $T_{local}(i)$  is the local execution time of task i, and  $T_{offload}(i)$  is the offloading time for task i. Such as  $T_{offload}(i) \leq T_{max}$  where  $T_{max}$  is the maximum tolerable latency for offloading task i. Finally, the current network must be evaluated in terms of latency, bandwidth and reliability.

In order to improve and optimize offloading task some strategies have been used [104]. For instance, execute tasks on the mobile device itself is suitable for lightweight tasks with minimal processing requirements. Dividing tasks and executing some parts locally while offloading the remaining to the edge server can be seen as an effective strategy. However, offloading the entire task to the edge server could be a necessity in some cases and will be suitable for resource-intensive tasks. Moreover, energy consumption is also a big challenge and must be optimized by Minimizing energy consumption on mobile devices and edge servers. Energy consumption can be seen as:

$$E = \sum_{i=1}^{N} E_{local}(i) + E_{offload}(i)$$
 (4)

where  $E_{local}(i)$  is the energy consumed when task i is executed locally and  $E_{offload}(i)$  is the energy consumed when task i is offloaded.

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} C_i \tag{5}$$

Optimizing energy efficiency is vital for prolonging the operational lifespan of battery-powered edge devices and reducing the environmental impact of EC infrastructure. Furthermore, task scheduling in EC must address the heterogeneity of edge devices and their varying computational capacities. The scheduling algorithm needs to balance the load across devices, ensuring that resource-intensive tasks are allocated to devices with sufficient capabilities (see Eq. (6)). Addressing the joint resource allocation problem is crucial for enhancing the overall performance, reliability, and efficiency of edge and MEC systems. In EC, joint resource allocation encompasses the allocation of computational resources, such as CPU and memory, network resources, and storage capacities across edge devices. The objective is to balance the workload, prevent resource bottlenecks, and ensure that tasks are executed efficiently. This requires adaptive algorithms that can dynamically allocate resources based on the changing demands of applications and the availability of resources in the edge network.

In the realm of MEC, the joint resource allocation problem extends to the coordination of resources between edge servers and mobile devices. Mobile devices typically have limited computational power and battery life, making it imperative to offload certain tasks to more capable edge servers. The challenge lies in determining the optimal allocation strategy, considering factors such as task characteristics, network conditions and the energy constraints of mobile devices. Mathematically, the joint resource allocation problem involves formulating optimization objectives and constraints that capture the trade-offs between various resources and system goals. The objective functions may include minimizing energy consumption, reducing task completion time or optimizing resource utilization. Constraints can include limitations on the computation capacity of edge devices, network bandwidth constraints and latency requirements. Moreover, the joint resource allocation problem often requires collaboration between edge devices and edge servers. This collaborative approach involves making decisions collectively to achieve system-wide optimization. Advanced algorithms, including game theory and distributed optimization techniques are employed to coordinate resource allocation decisions across the decentralized edge environment.

Security and privacy considerations also play a significant role in joint resource allocation. Sensitive data may be processed either on the mobile device or at the edge, and careful allocation decisions need to be made to ensure data privacy and compliance with regulatory requirements. Many studies in the field of EC are actively exploring innovative solutions to the joint resource allocation problem (see [Table](#page-11-0) [2](#page-11-0)). ML and AI techniques are being leveraged to develop adaptive algorithms that can learn from historical data and make realtime decisions. As EC continues to evolve, addressing the joint resource allocation problem will be pivotal in unlocking the full potential of edge and MEC systems, enabling efficient and responsive applications in diverse and dynamic environments.

## <span id="page-13-0"></span>*4.1. Integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) in EC*

ISAC is a promising technology for B5G/6G networks that enables simultaneous wireless communication and environmental sensing. Recent studies have shown that ISAC can significantly enhance MEC by providing real-time contextaware services and optimizing resource allocation [[157–](#page-22-7)[159\]](#page-22-8). For instance, ISAC can be used to dynamically adjust network resources based on real-time sensing data, improving the efficiency and responsiveness of EC systems. ISAC systems leverage the ability of communication signals to perform dual functions-transmitting data and sensing the environment. This dual functionality allows for the collection of contextual information such as user location, mobility patterns, and environmental conditions. Integrating these sensing capabilities with EC can lead to several key benefits [\[160](#page-22-9),[161](#page-22-10)]. ISAC provides real-time environmental and user context, which can be used by edge computing systems to optimize resource allocation and service delivery. For instance, knowing the precise location and movement patterns of users can help in dynamically adjusting computational resources to meet changing demands. In addition, by using sensing data to monitor network conditions and user activities, ISAC can facilitate more efficient management of network resources. This includes optimizing the placement and migration of EC tasks, leading to reduced latency and improved QoE. ISAC can help in conserving energy by adjusting the operational parameters of edge devices based on real-time sensing information. For example, edge servers can enter low-power states during periods of low activity, or adjust their processing power based on the sensed workload.

## <span id="page-15-0"></span>7. Contractual, reputational, and opportunistic wireless networking

<span id="page-15-1"></span>Addressing the challenges identified in the previous sections, we propose a novel scheme designed to enhance the performance and efficiency of EC. This section outlines the key features, implementation details, and expected benefits of the proposed scheme.

Future imaginative applications of 6G and beyond networks depend on extensive computation resources and ultra-low latency communication (human reaction time about 250 ms, AR/VR < 5 ms, 10−100 GB data generation per entity [\[166](#page-22-15)]) that entail costly infrastructure investments [\[28](#page-19-3),[29\]](#page-19-4). Increasing capacities with more of the same approach will not meet exponentially growing demand. EC or pushing computation and data storage closer to end-users has emerged to meet capacity and low latency requirements of emerging applications. However, edge solutions rely on static and predeployed infrastructure, falling short in harnessing the abundant resources at the network's edge. One key issue is the failure of resource discovery in exascale distributed computing systems with near real-time requirements due to fundamental challenges of:

- Complex entropy mapping of resources in heterogenous and dynamic network environments: Discovering resources and services is a challenging task due to the highly unpredictable nature of the edge ecosystem with heterogeneity of resources, diversity of traffic, and varying link conditions [\[167\]](#page-22-16).
- Lack of orchestration of resources in a dynamic environment of user/resource mobility, on and off resource availability and accessibility: The decentralized structure of EC requires coordination. However, controllers to assure fault tolerance lead to a high overhead [[168\]](#page-22-17). Edge architectures must rely on available local information and decision making based on partial information leading to sub-optimal resource utilization decisions, which negatively impacting users' QoE [[167](#page-22-16)[,168](#page-22-17)]. Mobility of connected devices also leads to some unpredictable requests, such as offloading task and resource allocation [[19\]](#page-18-19).

In order to address these challenges we propose Contractual, Reputational, and Opportunistic Wireless Networking (CROWN). CROWN tackles these problems making based on:

• A resource mapping framework to enable all available resources – with respect to link (*l*), storage (*s*) and

Reformulating the resource mapping problem will entail the following steps: We will introduce a three-dimensional resource mapping architecture leveraging NDN. A resource includes contracts (with respect to the desired task/content) and opportunities (grasped with respect to availability of set of nodes). For grasping opportunities, availability is a vector and defined with respect to ⌈*l*,*s*, *c*⌉ capacity per node. We project this vector onto three novel metrics:

- *primacy* provides the rank when the node will be grasped as a resource,
- *supremacy* determines with what type of tasks a node will be grasped for quality of experience and,
- *s*w*ay* gives how long a node will be requested to be occupied.

We then develop a practical framework for the above theoretical resource mapping solution introducing three novel schemes — Contractual and Opportunistic Broadcasting (COB), Proof of Reputation (POR) and Proof of Consensus (POC). COB scheme is a proactive protocol that enables each edge unit to broadcast its context information and available resources defined as device capacity, CPU, memory, usage rates, speed, direction, preferences, past and future actions/behaviors, per node. COB uses contracts and requires information about node capability to build the opportunity map. Proof of Reputation (POR) is another parameter in the decision to predict which node forwards the traffic; each node has a reputation score based on historical COB data including tasks it has completed, whether it successfully routed traffic, etc. POR uses reinforcement learning to determine the *primacy*, *supremacy* and *s*w*ay* metrics (PASS) as seen in [Fig.](#page-8-3) [6.](#page-8-3) POC mechanism over blockchain provides a consensus and coordination among all nodes for PASS values. Hence, PASS with POR becomes the practical projection of the network capacity (as the Shannon capacity formula provides for subcarriers of OFDMA) and is broadcasted among nodes (e.g. embedded in the NDN Interests as .../PASS/...).

An opportunity will be under the sway of the node in a contract. Finally, we will use the time projection function of sway and apply network coding theory to project opportunities over time. The latter will form the basis of our novel pre-computing, prefetching and compute re-use systems based on COB and POR predictions to improve network speed, reduce latency and congestion and avoid repeat computations. #### <span id="page-17-0"></span>8. Discussion of the future EC and MEC

<span id="page-17-1"></span>In recent years, the landscape of computing has undergone a transformative shift with the advent of EC and MEC. As these technologies redefine the boundaries of traditional cloud computing, we find ourselves at the forefront of a new era where processing power is distributed closer to the data source, promising unprecedented gains in efficiency, latency reduction and improved user experiences. EC, with its emphasis on localized data processing and reduced latency, has become a cornerstone in the era of IoT and 5G connectivity. MEC, an extension of this paradigm, takes computing capabilities to the every edge of the mobile network, offering a fertile ground for innovation in applications ranging from AR to autonomous systems. However, as we stand at the intersection of theoretical potential and practical implementation, numerous research avenues await exploration. One crucial area that requires focus is the smooth incorporation of EC technologies into current network topologies. Researchers are encouraged to delve into the intricacies of interoperability, standardization and the development of robust frameworks that ensure the smooth coexistence of edge devices in diverse ecosystems.

The future integration of vehicles and mobile devices into EC and MEC is poised to revolutionize various industries, offering enhanced connectivity, real-time processing and novel applications. For instance, Autonomous vehicles generate vast amounts of data that require rapid processing for real-time decision-making. Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) will facilitate onboard processing of critical data, enabling quicker responses to changing road conditions, traffic patterns and ensuring the safety of passengers and pedestrians. In addition, Integration with EC enables vehicles to communicate with each other and with roadside infrastructure to optimize traffic flow, reduce congestion, and enhance overall transportation efficiency. Furthemore, mobile devices will leverage MEC to offload resource-intensive AR and VR processing tasks to edge servers, ensuring a seamless and immersive user experience without straining the device's hardware. MEC empowers mobile devices to access ultra-low latency applications, such as real-time language translation, gesture recognition and interactive gaming. These applications benefit from the proximity of edge servers, minimizing latency and improving responsiveness.

Mobile devices will act as gateways to the IoT, connecting a myriad of smart devices. MEC facilitates the processing of IoT-generated data at the edge, reducing the need to send vast amounts of data to centralized cloud servers. In addition, vehicles can leverage edge servers to enhance navigation systems. Real-time updates on road conditions, weather, and potential hazards can be processed at the edge, providing drivers with more accurate and timely information. Furthermore, fleet management systems integrated with EC can optimize delivery routes, monitor vehicle health in real time, and enhance overall logistics efficiency. Mobile devices carried by drivers act as nodes in this network, facilitating seamless communication with edge servers.

Essentially, the merging of vehicles and mobile devices with EC and MEC signifies a fundamental change towards a connected, intelligent, and efficient future. The interdependent connection between these technologies is anticipated to generate advancements that enhance safety, elevate user experiences and revolutionize our perception and interaction with our mobile surroundings.

The convergence of EC and 5G/6G networks brings several critical issues that need thorough examination, particularly regarding energy consumption, energy efficiency design and security. As edge devices and servers perform increasingly complex computations locally, their energy consumption rises significantly, necessitating the development of energyefficient architectures and algorithms to ensure sustainable operations. This includes optimizing resource allocation, implementing power-saving techniques, and leveraging AI to predict and manage energy usage dynamically. Additionally, the integration of edge computing with 5G/6G networks introduces heightened security challenges, such as safeguarding data privacy, preventing cyber-attacks and ensuring secure communication channels. Robust security frameworks must be designed to protect sensitive information processed at the edge, incorporating advanced encryption methods, secure authentication protocols and real-time threat detection mechanisms. Addressing these energy and security concerns is crucial for the successful deployment and operation of EC within the 5G/6G ecosystem, ensuring both efficiency and protection in next-generation networks.

The integration of advanced ML and AI techniques with EC remains a fertile ground for innovation, particularly in improving resource allocation and predictive maintenance. Moreover, addressing security and privacy concerns is paramount, necessitating the development of robust frameworks to safeguard data at the edge. Additionally, energy efficiency continues to be a significant challenge, highlighting the need for research into low-power edge devices and sustainable architectures. The deployment of EC in 6G networks also presents new challenges and opportunities, such as enhancing network reliability and reducing latency through novel communication protocols and infrastructure. Lastly, the scalability of EC solutions across diverse and large-scale environments requires innovative approaches to ensure seamless operation and management. By focusing on these areas, future research can drive the evolution of edge computing, unlocking its full potential in various applications and industries.
