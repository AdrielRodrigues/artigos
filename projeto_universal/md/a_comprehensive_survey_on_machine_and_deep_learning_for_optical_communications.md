**ABSTRACT** The increasing complexity of optical communication systems and networks necessitates advanced methodologies for extracting valuable insights from vast and heterogeneous datasets. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have emerged as pivotal tools in this domain, revolutionizing data analysis and enabling automated self-configuration in optical communication systems. Their adoption is fueled by the growing intricacy of systems and links, driven by numerous adjustable and interdependent parameters. This complexity is particularly evident in areas such as coherent transceivers, advanced digital signal processing, optical performance monitoring, cross-layer network optimizations, and nonlinearity compensation. While ML and DL offer immense potential, their application in optical communications is still in its early stages, with significant opportunities remaining unexplored. Many algorithms have yet to be fully deployed in practical settings, underscoring the emerging nature of this research area. This paper presents a comprehensive survey of ML and DL applications across optical fiber communication (OFC), optical wireless communication (OWC), and optical communication networking (OCN), highlighting the challenges, current advancements, and future potential of these approaches. To address the identified gaps, this survey evaluates and compares ML and DL algorithms in terms of their performance, complexity, objectives, input data, metrics, and applications in optical communication. Specific emphasis is placed on identifying how these algorithms enhance system performance and optimization. Furthermore, the advantages and limitations of existing methods are analyzed, offering a clear perspective on the role of ML and DL in this domain. The survey also includes updated visual representations and domain-specific examples to elucidate the practical applications of ML and DL in OFC, OWC, and OCN. It concludes by discussing the open challenges, proposing potential solutions, and identifying promising future research directions to foster advancements in intelligent optical communication systems.

**INDEX TERMS** Machine learning, deep learning, optical fiber communication, optical communication networking, optical wireless communication.

The growing demand for efficient and resilient optical communication systems has accelerated the adoption of ML and DL techniques. These methods have demonstrated their potential in diverse applications, from the physical layer to optical communication networking (OCN), and across various media such as optical fiber communication (OFC) and optical wireless communication (OWC). Despite these advancements, the full potential of ML and DL in optical communications remains underexplored, highlighting the importance of surveys that consolidate recent developments and identify opportunities for further research [\[4\],](#page-45-3) [\[5\],](#page-45-4) [\[6\],](#page-45-5) [\[7\].](#page-45-6)

<span id="page-1-3"></span><span id="page-1-2"></span>In the physical layer, ML and DL algorithms have been effectively utilized to address several challenges. These include enhanced modeling, where complex relationships between system parameters are captured to develop accurate and reliable models for performance evaluation and system design. Additionally, adaptive signal processing optimizes tasks like noise reduction, error correction, and adaptive modulation in real-time, improving quality of service. Nonlinearity equalization helps mitigate signal distortions caused by nonlinear optical effects, ensuring robust and high-quality signal transmission. ML and DL are also applied in resource allocation to efficiently manage network resources, such as bandwidth and power, thereby enhancing scalability and efficiency. Moreover, constellation shaping optimizes signal patterns, improving transmission efficiency and error resilience [\[8\],](#page-45-7) [\[9\],](#page-45-8) [\[10\].](#page-45-9)

<span id="page-1-6"></span><span id="page-1-5"></span><span id="page-1-4"></span>In OCN, ML and DL are employed for various purposes. Network optimization is a key area, improving network routing, resource allocation, and traffic management to achieve more efficient and resilient communication systems. Fault detection and mitigation techniques are used to proactively identify and resolve issues such as fiber cuts or optical impairments, maintaining network reliability. Resource management optimizes power consumption and spectrum usage, ensuring system sustainability and efficiency. ML and DL also enable optical performance monitoring (OPM), providing real-time monitoring of network performance parameters to detect and diagnose problems. Furthermore, quality of transmission (QoT) estimation evaluates network performance metrics, ensuring data integrity and reliability.

<span id="page-1-18"></span><span id="page-1-17"></span><span id="page-1-16"></span><span id="page-1-15"></span><span id="page-1-14"></span><span id="page-1-13"></span><span id="page-1-12"></span><span id="page-1-11"></span><span id="page-1-10"></span><span id="page-1-9"></span><span id="page-1-8"></span><span id="page-1-7"></span>ML algorithms used in optical communication are generally classified into three categories. Supervised learning (SL) algorithms learn from labeled data to make predictions and classifications. Common techniques in this category include support vector machines (SVMs) [\[11\],](#page-45-10) artificial neural networks (ANNs) [\[12\], k](#page-45-11)-nearest neighbors (kNNs) [\[13\], e](#page-45-12)nsemble learning algorithms [\[14\], a](#page-45-13)nd linear models [\[15\],](#page-45-14) [\[16\].](#page-45-15) Unsupervised learning (USL) algorithms analyze unlabeled data to identify patterns or clusters. Examples include hierarchical clustering [\[17\], K](#page-45-16)-means [\[18\],](#page-45-17) expectation maximization (EM) [\[19\], p](#page-45-18)rincipal component analysis (PCA) [\[20\], a](#page-45-19)nd independent component analysis (ICA) [\[21\]. R](#page-45-20)einforcement learning (RL) algorithms, on the other hand, interact with an environment to learn optimal decision-making strategies, such as policy-based RL and value-based RL [\[22\].](#page-45-21)

![](_page_2_Picture_1.jpeg)

DL, as a subset of ML, uses artificial neural networks with multiple layers to extract complex patterns from large datasets. DL applications in optical communications include deep neural networks (DNNs) [\[23\], w](#page-45-22)hich generalize neural networks by employing multiple layers to analyze complex data relationships. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [\[24\]](#page-45-23) are designed for sequential data and are particularly useful for optical signal processing and traffic prediction in dynamic networks. convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [\[25\]](#page-45-24) excel at processing spatial data, such as optical channel images or network topology visualizations. Lastly, deep RL (DRL) combines RL and DL [\[26\]](#page-45-25) to facilitate intelligent decision-making in complex and dynamic optical network environments.

#### <span id="page-2-3"></span><span id="page-2-2"></span>A. RELATED WORKS

<span id="page-2-6"></span><span id="page-2-5"></span><span id="page-2-4"></span>ML and DL have become pioneering tools across various fields, including optical communications. Numerous surveys have examined the advancements and applications of ML/DL in this domain, providing valuable insights into their potential and identifying opportunities for future exploration (Table [2\)](#page-3-0). In [\[27\], t](#page-45-26)he authors investigate the use of ML for failure management in optical networks, highlighting its ability to transform fault detection practices and enhance network resilience. Similarly, ML techniques have been applied for routing optimization in software-defined networks (SDNs). The survey in [\[28\]](#page-45-27) categorizes ML approaches for routing optimization into three main groups: SL, USL, and RL. The work in [\[29\]](#page-45-28) provides a comprehensive review of routing and resource allocation (RRA) challenges in optical networks. It discusses critical problems such as routing and wavelength allocation (RWA) in wavelength division multiplexing (WDM), routing and spectrum allocation (RSA) in elastic optical networks (EONs), and routing, core, and resource allocation (RCSA) in spatial division multiplexing (SDM) systems. This survey also explores ML's role in addressing issues like QoT estimation, traffic prediction, and crosstalk mitigation. The potential of ML to optimize optical network performance is explored in [\[30\]. T](#page-45-29)his work focuses on algorithm selection, data and model management, and ML integration into network control systems. Four practical case studies demonstrate ML's versatility in addressing real-world challenges such as predictive maintenance, virtual network topology management, capacity optimization, and optical spectral analysis. The applications of ML in intelligent optical networks are further reviewed in [\[31\]. T](#page-45-30)he authors classify ML use cases into two broad categories: network control and resource management (e.g., RWA, bandwidth allocation, traffic management, self-healing networks) and optical network monitoring and survivability (e.g., fault detection, performance monitoring, network planning, and intrusion detection).

A broader review of AI applications in optical communications is provided in [\[32\], e](#page-45-31)ncompassing both optical transmission and network management. This work <span id="page-2-13"></span><span id="page-2-12"></span><span id="page-2-11"></span><span id="page-2-10"></span><span id="page-2-1"></span><span id="page-2-0"></span>highlights AI's potential to improve signal processing, optimize resource allocation, and enhance network resilience. In a related study, [\[33\]](#page-46-0) delves into the motivations for ML adoption in optical networks, emphasizing its capacity to address challenges such as nonlinear equalization, constellation shaping, adaptive modulation, and fault detection. Mathematical foundations of ML techniques, including SL, USL, and RL, are explored in [\[34\]. T](#page-46-1)his survey also examines ML-based solutions for channel estimation, signal detection, and equalization to tackle physical layer issues such as optical nonlinearities and interference. ML's application in SDNs, particularly for traffic classification, routing optimization, quality of service (QoS) prediction, and security, is analyzed in [\[35\].](#page-46-2) The integration of DL in optical communications, focusing on DNNs, RNNs, and DRL, is highlighted in [\[36\]. T](#page-46-3)his work emphasizes the innovative potential of DL in tasks like optical signal processing and network traffic prediction. A focused review of ML for coherent optical-orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) systems is presented in [\[37\]. T](#page-46-4)his survey systematically analyzes ML-based approaches, including linear prediction, Volterra series, and neural networks, and compares them with conventional digital signal processing solutions. QoT prediction techniques utilizing random forests, neural networks, and SVMs are discussed in [\[38\]. F](#page-46-5)inally, [\[39\]](#page-46-6) reviews ML applications in OPM. This includes tasks such as signal quality monitoring, impairment detection, fault localization, and Bit Rate-Modulation Format Identification (BR-MFI), demonstrating ML's effectiveness in enhancing OPM capabilities and enabling proactive network management.

<span id="page-2-16"></span><span id="page-2-15"></span><span id="page-2-14"></span>These studies collectively underscore the significant advancements and diverse applications of ML/DL in optical communications. Regression algorithms, including linear regression, Lasso regression, and ridge regression, are widely used in optical communication systems for tasks such as signal prediction, parameter estimation, and impairment compensation. These techniques model the relationship between input variables and output signals by finding the best-fitting function that minimizes the difference between observed and predicted values. In optical communication, regression algorithms are primarily employed for mitigating the effects of nonlinearities, such as inter-channel and intra-channel Kerr nonlinearity, which are common challenges in systems with high-speed data transmission.

Among the regression techniques, Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression has gained significant attention, particularly for compensating nonlinearities in SDM-based transmission systems. Lasso regression incorporates a penalty term in the cost function, which encourages sparsity in the model by driving some coefficients to zero. This sparsity-promoting property makes Lasso particularly effective for identifying the most relevant perturbation terms in nonlinear optical systems while minimizing computational complexity. By reducing the number of active terms in the model, Lasso regression effectively simplifies the overall computational load, making it well-suited for real-time implementation in high-speed optical communication systems, such as those involving SDM.

<span id="page-9-0"></span>Several studies have demonstrated Lasso regression's ability to mitigate nonlinearities and enhance the performance of SDM-based transmission systems. For instance, Sorokina et al. This capability makes Lasso regression particularly useful in SDM systems, where the increased number of channels and the complex interactions between them exacerbate nonlinear distortion. The reduction in nonlinearity-induced distortion directly translates into improvements in key performance metrics, such as BER and quality factor (Q-factor), which are crucial for maintaining reliable high-speed communication.

Despite its effectiveness, Lasso regression has certain limitations. The main challenge lies in the selection of the regularization parameter, which controls the strength of the penalty term. A poor choice of this parameter can lead to either overfitting (when the penalty is too weak) or underfitting (when the penalty is too strong), resulting in suboptimal performance. Furthermore, Lasso regression assumes that the relationships between input features and output variables are linear, which may not always hold true in highly nonlinear optical systems. While Lasso can approximate nonlinearities effectively in many cases, it may struggle with more complex, non-linear relationships that require more advanced models.

In terms of computational complexity, Lasso regression is generally considered efficient due to its sparse nature. This makes it faster and less resource-intensive compared to other regression methods like ridge regression, which does not promote sparsity and may result in higher computational overhead. The low complexity of Lasso also makes it an attractive choice for real-time applications, where fast processing is crucial.

Lasso regression's performance can be further enhanced when combined with other techniques, such as the Sparse Identification for Nonlinear Optics method and Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP), which help in identifying sparse nonlinear perturbations in optical systems. These hybrid approaches are especially useful for high-capacity SDM transmission, where they facilitate efficient nonlinearity mitigation over long transmission distances. As SDM technology continues to evolve, the role of Lasso regression in optimizing high-speed optical communication systems is expected to expand, offering a scalable and computationally efficient solution for nonlinear compensation.

However, a key comparison with conventional methods reveals that while Lasso regression excels in low-complexity, real-time applications, traditional techniques such as Volterra series and polynomial models may offer more accurate nonlinear compensation at the cost of increased computational complexity. These traditional methods are often better suited for systems where high precision is required in mitigating complex, multi-dimensional nonlinearities, such as those present in dense WDM systems or when multi-band and multi-core fibers are used. In contrast, Lasso regression's simplicity and effectiveness make it an ideal choice for applications where computational resources are limited, and real-time performance is essential.

In conclusion, Lasso regression provides a valuable tool for compensating nonlinearities in optical communication systems, particularly in the context of SDM transmission. Its low complexity, real-time implementation capability, and ability to reduce nonlinearity-induced signal distortion make it a promising technique for high-speed optical networks. However, its limitations, such as sensitivity to regularization parameter selection and its assumption of linear relationships, suggest that it may not always be the best solution for highly nonlinear systems. Combining Lasso regression with other advanced techniques could offer a more comprehensive solution, balancing the need for computational efficiency and high performance in next-generation optical communication systems.

To mitigate this issue, various methods, such as multiple initializations or more sophisticated optimization techniques, can be used, but they may introduce additional computational complexity. Furthermore, while EM clustering is powerful in noise mitigation, its performance may degrade in highly nonlinear or non-Gaussian noise environments where more advanced techniques, such as DL, may provide better results.

In comparison to conventional methods, such as RF or SVM, EM clustering has a clear advantage in its ability to model the underlying probabilistic structure of noise and perform iterative optimization to enhance noise estimation. Traditional methods often rely on fixed, predefined models that may not adapt well to varying channel conditions or changing noise characteristics. EM clustering, on the other hand, can dynamically adjust to the observed data, providing more accurate estimates of noise parameters. However, conventional methods, like SVM, tend to perform better in scenarios where the decision boundaries between signal and noise are more complex, especially in high-dimensional spaces. In these cases, ML techniques like SVM and XGB may offer superior performance by leveraging their ability to model non-linear decision boundaries.

In summary, EM clustering provides a robust method for compensating laser amplitude and phase noise in optical communication systems, offering improvements in signal quality, launch power tolerance, and OSNR. While it has some limitations, such as sensitivity to parameter initialization, its computational efficiency and adaptability make it a valuable tool for modern high-speed optical networks. As optical communication systems evolve, it is likely that EM clustering will continue to play a significant role, especially when combined with other ML techniques, to address the growing challenges of noise mitigation in increasingly complex and dynamic communication environments.

#### 9) INDEPENDENT COMPONENT ANALYSIS ALGORITHM

ICA is a statistical technique designed for uninformed source separation, particularly useful in multi-user FSO communication systems where signals transmitted at different wavelengths must be separated at the receiver. ICA operates by assuming that observed mixed signals are linear combinations of independent source signals and attempts to recover these independent components without prior knowledge of the mixing process. This capability is particularly valuable in multi-user detection (MUD) scenarios, where optical signals transmitted by different users must be distinguished to ensure reliable data recovery [\[132\],](#page-48-20) [\[133\].](#page-48-21)

<span id="page-25-2"></span><span id="page-25-0"></span>Despite its effectiveness, a fundamental limitation of ICA is that it traditionally requires an equal number of transmitters and receivers to perform accurate signal separation. This constraint poses a significant challenge in practical FSO systems where the number of active transmitters may exceed the available receivers, leading to underdetermined systems where classical ICA fails. Addressing this limitation, [\[134\]](#page-48-22) proposes two enhanced approaches for ICA-based signal separation. The first method leverages the sparsity of transmitted signals, where only a few dominant components contribute significantly at any given time. By exploiting sparsity, ICA can isolate these components even when the number of transmitters exceeds the number of receivers. The second method integrates ICA with non-orthogonal multiple access and successive interference cancellation, allowing the system to separate overlapping signals by iteratively removing stronger components before extracting weaker ones. This hybrid approach significantly improves ICA's applicability in practical multi-user FSO links.

Compared to conventional signal separation techniques such as matched filtering and MMSE detection, ICA offers the advantage of being uninformed, meaning it does not require CSI or training sequences. This characteristic makes it particularly useful in dynamic FSO environments where CSI acquisition is challenging due to atmospheric turbulence and mobility. However, ICA's performance is sensitive to the statistical independence of source signals—highly correlated optical signals can degrade separation accuracy. Additionally, iterative ICA algorithms may suffer from convergence issues and computational complexity, particularly in real-time applications with high-dimensional data streams.

Future improvements could involve DL-assisted ICA models that leverage prior knowledge from historical signal patterns to enhance convergence and robustness. Furthermore, integrating ICA with adaptive filtering techniques could improve its ability to handle correlated signal sources in dense multi-user FSO networks. Overall, ICA remains a promising solution for enhancing spectral efficiency and mitigating interference in next-generation optical wireless systems, particularly when combined with sparsity-based and non-orthogonal multiple access-assisted approaches.

11) VALUE-BASED REINFORCEMENT LEARNING ALGORITHM Figure [13\(b\)](#page-19-0) illustrates the core components of value-based RL, where an ''Agent'' learns a ''Value Function'' that estimates the long-term reward potential of different states and actions. The process begins with the ''Environment'' providing the ''State,'' and the agent uses its value function to select an ''Action'' based on the action that maximizes the expected future return. The ''Policy Selection'' step, often implicit, is derived from the value function, where the agent chooses actions that lead to states with higher values.

Unlike policy-based RL, which directly learns a policy function mapping states to actions, value-based RL focuses on estimating the value of states and actions, allowing for more informed decision-making. This approach can be particularly useful in environments with large or continuous action spaces, where directly learning a policy function can be challenging.

RL has demonstrated its effectiveness in solving complex optimization problems in dynamic environments, particularly in scenarios where explicit modeling is impractical or prior knowledge is unavailable. Value-based RL methods, such as Q-learning and deep Q-networks (DQN), estimate the expected rewards of different actions to iteratively improve decision-making policies. These approaches are well-suited for problems involving resource allocation and power optimization in optical wireless communication networks.

Performance metrics such as throughput, delay, packet loss, utilization, and energy consumption demonstrate substantial improvements over traditional virtual network embedding (VNE) approaches, such as VNE-EACH and VNE-USE.

While RL-based power allocation offers superior adaptability and efficiency, it comes with certain challenges. One key limitation is the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, where excessive exploration can slow down convergence while insufficient exploration may lead to suboptimal policies. Moreover, value-based RL methods often suffer from instability and divergence when applied to high-dimensional or continuous action spaces. These issues can be mitigated by incorporating function approximation techniques such as deep neural networks, enabling DRL approaches like DQN to handle complex decision-making problems in large-scale optical networks.

Future research directions could explore combining RL with federated learning to enable decentralized power allocation across distributed FSO systems, reducing dependency on centralized controllers. Additionally, integrating RL with emerging AI-driven optimization techniques, such as multi-agent RL and hierarchical RL, could further enhance system scalability and efficiency. As RL research progresses, its applications in power allocation, interference mitigation, and adaptive resource management in optical communication networks will continue to expand, addressing increasingly complex challenges while improving network performance and reliability.

#### 2) RECURRENT NEURAL NETWORK ALGORITHM

RNNs, particularly Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have demonstrated significant improvements in optical wireless communication, including FSO and VLC systems. Unlike feedforward neural networks, RNNs retain memory of past inputs, making them particularly suited for sequential data processing, such as time-series signal prediction and equalization. Their ability to model temporal dependencies has led to enhanced performance in mitigating nonlinearity, inter-symbol interference, flicker, and ambient interference.

In [153], a GRU-based model was developed for FSO channel estimation, utilizing RSS and weather information to predict future channel conditions. The model achieved an absolute percentage error below 6.9% in 90% of cases, outperforming conventional methods such as Backpropagation Neural Networks (BPNN), SVM, and Random Forest in both accuracy and computational efficiency. By leveraging its ability to maintain historical signal strength information, GRU reduced estimation errors while significantly lowering the computational cost compared to traditional ML models, which often require extensive feature engineering and struggle with long-term dependencies.

<span id="page-28-3"></span>A hybrid LSTM-DNN algorithm was introduced in [154] to equalize channel distortions in VLC systems. Unlike conventional MMSE, DFE, and Volterra-based nonlinear equalizers, which rely on heuristic models of the channel, the LSTM-DNN approach learns from the received symbols to estimate the optimal equalization function. This method improved the SNR by at least 2.8 dB at a target BER of 10<sup>-3</sup>, achieving a BER of 10<sup>-5</sup> under real-world VLC conditions. The LSTM-DNN model demonstrated robustness

#### 4) DEEP REINFORCEMENT LEARNING ALGORITHMS

RL algorithms, particularly deep RL methods, have demonstrated significant potential in optimizing resource allocation, adaptive decision-making, and network management in optical communication systems. These methods dynamically adapt to changing environments, making them particularly effective in scenarios with varying channel conditions, interference, and mobility constraints.

In FSO systems, RL-based techniques have been employed for relay selection, power allocation, and transmission optimization. A DQN-based approach in [\[172\]](#page-49-19) enhances relay selection in cooperative decode-and-forward FSO systems by effectively balancing data rate and relay reliability, outperforming conventional greedy selection methods. Similarly, in hybrid FSO/RF links, DQN improves link selection under varying weather conditions [\[173\],](#page-49-20) achieving faster convergence while reducing frequent switching via an ensemble consensus mechanism.

<span id="page-30-6"></span>For throughput enhancement in FSO communication, [\[174\]](#page-49-21) employs a DQN-based spinal code transmission strategy, surpassing traditional methods in long-range scenarios. Multi-agent DQN frameworks have also been explored for MIMO underwater optical wireless communication systems [\[175\],](#page-49-22) where agents optimize channel access to improve spectral efficiency. The proposed method achieves a 66.67% higher throughput than ALOHA and a 50% improvement over TDMA, making it viable for real-time communication.

Power allocation in hybrid RF/VLC networks is another critical application of deep RL. In [\[176\],](#page-49-23) a DQN-based approach dynamically adjusts transmission power to meet targeted data rates while ensuring efficient energy usage, achieving a convergence rate of 96.1%, significantly higher than Q-learning's 72.3%. A more advanced Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) model [\[177\]](#page-49-24) further improves power control by outperforming QL, DQN, genetic algorithms, and Particle Swarm Optimization, demonstrating robustness in mobile environments.

Handover management in emerging 6G hybrid networks benefits from RL-driven optimization. A Deep RL-based <span id="page-30-3"></span><span id="page-30-2"></span>mechanism in [\[178\]](#page-49-25) improves downlink data rates by 13% compared to QL and Sarsa while reducing signaling overhead, leading to more efficient mobility handling. In VLC networks, beamforming optimization is critical for maintaining signal quality and security. In [\[179\],](#page-49-26) a DRLbased beamforming strategy significantly reduces BER while improving secrecy rates against potential eavesdropping, surpassing conventional beamforming techniques.

Overall, RL techniques provide substantial improvements in optical communication networks by enabling intelligent decision-making in real time. Compared to traditional heuristic and rule-based approaches, RL-based methods demonstrate superior adaptability to dynamic channel variations, interference mitigation, and resource optimization. However, challenges remain, including high computational complexity, real-time training overhead, and the need for efficient exploration-exploitation trade-offs. Future research should focus on developing hybrid RL architectures that combine model-free and model-based techniques, integrating RL with federated learning for decentralized optimization, and designing lightweight RL models for real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments.

#### 2) ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORK ALGORITHM

ANNs have gained prominence as powerful tools for OPM and enhancing the reliability and performance of OCNs. #### 5) REGRESSION ALGORITHMS

Minimizing fiber faults is a crucial aspect of ensuring reliable data transmission in OCNs. In [\[199\],](#page-50-7) the authors explored the use of linear regression as an alternative to enhance the accuracy of fault localization. Linear regression is a statistical method that models the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables by fitting a linear equation to observed data. In this context, the dependent variable corresponds to the fault location, and the independent variables are the measured signals and other network parameters. The study demonstrated that linear regression could significantly improve fault localization accuracy, reducing fault tracing delay and thus minimizing the associated revenue loss. This application of ML techniques highlights the potential of regression models to outperform traditional methods in terms of precision and speed, enabling faster restoration of service and more efficient fault management in optical networks.

One of the key advantages of regression-based methods, such as linear regression, lies in their simplicity and interpretability. Furthermore, the relationships between the variables are straightforward to understand, which aids in identifying and correcting potential issues in the fault localization process. Despite these challenges, the integration of ML techniques like regression into fault detection and localization systems represents a promising shift in how OCNs manage and mitigate network failures. As research progresses, it is expected that ML will become increasingly pivotal in fault management for optical communication systems, offering not only faster restoration times but also enhancing the overall resilience and reliability of the network.

#### 7) EXPECTATION MAXIMIZATION CLUSTERING ALGORITHM

EM is an iterative algorithm used for parameter estimation in the presence of incomplete or noisy data. It consists of two main steps: the expectation (E) step, which estimates the likelihood of missing data given the observed data, and the maximization (M) step, which updates the parameters to maximize the likelihood. This approach is particularly useful in applications where the underlying model involves hidden variables or incomplete data, making it a valuable tool for a variety of tasks, including clustering and multi-user detection in optical communication systems.

In [\[202\],](#page-50-10) the authors propose EM-based multi-user detection schemes for optical code division multiple access (OCDMA) systems. These systems are designed to allow multiple users to share the same optical bandwidth by encoding each user's signal with a unique optical code. However, the main challenge in OCDMA systems is managing multiuser interference, which arises when signals from different users overlap in the optical channel. Traditional detection methods, such as multistage and decorrelating detectors, typically suffer from performance degradation due to their inability to effectively handle the complex interference between multiple users.

The EM-based multi-user detection schemes proposed in [\[202\]](#page-50-10) address this challenge by iteratively estimating the interference via an unconstrained likelihood function, followed by a constrained Boolean likelihood function for bit detection. This approach significantly improves detection accuracy by better managing the interference between users. Simulation results indicate that EM-based detectors achieve BER within 2 dB of the optimal detector, outperforming traditional sub-optimal methods. Furthermore, EM-based schemes offer a complexity of *O*(*K* 3 ), which, although higher than some simpler methods, is still lower than that of multistage detection methods, making it a viable solution for practical implementation in OCDMA systems.

One of the key advantages of the EM-based detection schemes is their ability to function effectively even in environments with limited or no CSI, which is often the case in real-world optical networks. In such settings, where the channel characteristics are not explicitly known, the EM algorithm can robustly estimate the necessary parameters (such as signal power and interference levels) and achieve reliable detection performance. This makes EM a promising approach for improving the performance and scalability of OCDMA systems, especially in dynamic environments where CSI is not readily available.

However, while the EM algorithm offers significant advantages in terms of performance, its computational complexity can be a limitation, particularly for large-scale systems with a high number of users. The *O*(*K* 3 ) complexity, where *K* represents the number of users, may become prohibitive in scenarios with large networks. Additionally, EM's performance is sensitive to the initialization of its parameters, and poor initialization can lead to suboptimal results or slow convergence. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed various improvements, such as the use of smarter initialization techniques or hybrid approaches that combine EM with other algorithms, like matched filters or iterative methods, to improve robustness and reduce computational cost.

In summary, the EM-based multi-user detection schemes presented in [\[202\]](#page-50-10) offer a powerful and efficient solution for mitigating multi-user interference in OCDMA systems, particularly in the absence of CSI. As research advances, EM detectors are expected to play an increasingly important role in enhancing the performance of optical CDMA networks, although further optimization of their complexity and initialization methods will be essential for their widespread adoption in large-scale systems.

QL's time complexity is *O*(*n*), lower than shortest path selection's *O*(*n* 2 ) and dynamic wavelength routing's *O*(*n* 3 ), making it more efficient for path selection in large-scale networks.

<span id="page-39-0"></span>Despite its advantages, QL has limitations. For instance, in [\[220\],](#page-50-28) QL was extended to path and wavelength selection in OBS networks, but the approach was constrained by a single-agent architecture where only the ingress node provided feedback. This limited its ability to adapt to network-wide changes. To overcome this, [\[221\]](#page-50-29) proposed a multi-agent QL approach, where agents situated at different nodes in the network collaboratively optimize path selection. This multi-agent system outperformed both the traditional shortest path selection and the single-agent QL, achieving a 50% reduction in BLP and 30% lower delay, which enhanced the overall network performance in terms of throughput and fairness.

Moreover, QL has also been applied to optimize resource allocation in OWC systems. In [\[139\],](#page-48-27) QL was used to allocate resources in hybrid LiFi-WiFi access networks, improving service quality and provider revenue by reducing IoT migration ratios. In [\[222\],](#page-50-30) QL was implemented for access point selection in hybrid WiFi-VLC networks, improving throughput and fairness. Furthermore, QL-based approaches in wavelength and access point allocation have provided scalable solutions, with QL outperforming conventional mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) methods in [\[223\].](#page-50-31) This scalability is one of QL's strengths, making it suitable for large, complex networks.

<span id="page-39-1"></span>In [\[224\],](#page-50-32) a multi-agent QL approach was used for power allocation in hybrid RF-VLC networks, where it outperformed conventional methods while ensuring that QoS constraints were met. For vertical handover in RF-VLC networks, QL was used to optimize time-to-trigger parameters, achieving higher throughput compared to fixedtime methods [\[225\],](#page-50-33) [\[226\].](#page-50-34)

<span id="page-39-2"></span>In conclusion, QL's adaptability to dynamic network conditions and its ability to reduce the computational burden make it a valuable tool for optimizing resource allocation in optical burst switched and optical wireless communication systems. However, its performance is sensitive to the accuracy of the state representation and the reward function, and it may struggle in highly dynamic environments with frequent state changes. As research advances, QL is expected to play a key role in evolving network technologies, especially as networks become more complex and require decentralized decision-making to handle increasing traffic demands.

<span id="page-41-5"></span><span id="page-41-4"></span><span id="page-41-3"></span>Beyond OSNR monitoring, LSTMs have been employed in multi-domain optical network routing. ## 3) CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORK ALGORITHM

CNNs have emerged as powerful tools for OPM and QoT estimation, leveraging their ability to extract spatial features from optical signals and constellation diagrams. These DL models have demonstrated superior accuracy and efficiency compared to traditional signal processing and ML approaches.

CNNs are also highly effective in OSNR estimation. In [\[242\],](#page-51-9) CNNs were used to predict OSNR from asynchronously sampled data obtained from a digital coherent receiver. The model achieved bias errors below 0.4 dB for DP-QPSK, 16-QAM, and 64-QAM, while maintaining lower computational complexity than conventional methods. In addition to OSNR monitoring, CNNs excel in joint monitoring of MFI and OSNR estimation. Beyond OSNR and MFI, CNNs have been successfully applied in multi-impairment monitoring. Similarly, [\[245\]](#page-51-12) explored CNN-based monitoring of CD, cross-talk, and OSNR, achieving 99% and 96% accuracy, significantly surpassing traditional techniques that typically yield 80-90% accuracy. The ability to monitor multiple impairments in real-time highlights CNNs' effectiveness in complex optical network environments.

Their reliance on large training datasets and high computational requirements can pose challenges in real-time deployment for ultra-fast networks. Moreover, their opaque nature makes interpretability difficult, which can be problematic in critical network management scenarios. Hybrid approaches, such as combining CNNs with LSTMs for sequential data processing or integrating attention mechanisms for enhanced feature extraction, have been explored to address these issues.

## 4) DEEP REINFORCEMENT LEARNING ALGORITHMS

DRL has emerged as a crucial methodology for optimizing performance and resource allocation in optical networks. Unlike conventional optimization techniques, DRL leverages an agent-based approach, where an agent interacts with the network environment and learns optimal strategies through trial and error, guided by a reward function. This capability allows DRL to adapt dynamically to network conditions, making it particularly suitable for complex and non-stationary environments such as EONs and hybrid optical-electrical systems.

In [\[249\],](#page-51-16) a double-agent DRL was applied for survivable routing, modulation, and spectrum assignment (RMSA) in EONs. This approach significantly improved spectrum utilization by 30% while enhancing network survivability. However, the method's computational complexity posed challenges for large-scale networks. Similarly, in [\[250\],](#page-51-17) DRL algorithms, including DQN and Actor-Critic with Experience Replay, were employed for RMSA in dynamic multi-core fiber EONs. These methods achieved a 77% reduction in blocking probability compared to heuristic approaches, but their scalability remained a concern for larger network topologies.

DRL has also demonstrated effectiveness in various network optimization tasks. For instance, in [\[251\],](#page-51-18) a DRLbased RMSA policy improved spectrum utilization by 15%, while exhibiting lower computational complexity than traditional RMSA algorithms. Additionally, [\[252\]](#page-51-19) introduced a multi-task transfer learning DRL framework that reduced training time and blocking probability by 17.7%, achieving a 10% decrease in latency. Such transfer learning strategies enhance model generalization across different network scenarios, addressing one of DRL's major limitations: the high data and computation requirements for training.

Beyond resource allocation, DRL has optimized dynamic service function chaining in metro-core optical networks[\[253\],](#page-51-20) leading to a 20% increase in network throughput and a 30% reduction in latency. In [\[254\],](#page-51-21) a DRL-based approach was proposed to enhance routing decisions by incorporating detailed network state representations, including network topology, traffic matrices, and routing constraints. The model employed DQN to iteratively learn an optimal routing policy through interactions with the network environment. This method significantly improved network performance, reducing blocking probability by 30%, packet delay by 20%, and overall energy consumption by 15% compared to traditional K-Shortest Paths algorithms. Additionally, DRL achieved a 70% reduction in end-to-end delay under varying traffic conditions, demonstrating its potential in real-time adaptive routing for high-speed optical networks.

In hybrid optical-electrical networks, DRL has been instrumental in traffic management. For instance, in [\[255\],](#page-51-22) DRL-based flow scheduling reduced flow completion time by 20%, with a time complexity of *O*(*n* log *n*) compared to the *O*(*n* 2 ) complexity of conventional approaches. Similarly, DRL-driven dynamic provisioning in [\[256\]](#page-51-23) outperformed heuristic methods by lowering blocking probability and reducing training time.

Furthermore, DRL has been applied to FSO communication systems. In [\[172\],](#page-49-19) deep Q-learning and DDPG were used for relay selection in cooperative FSO systems, improving capacity by 20% compared to greedy methods while maintaining a linear time complexity of *O*(*n*). Additionally, in [\[173\],](#page-49-20) a DRL-based approach for FSO/RF link selection reduced switching costs by 50%, demonstrating efficiency in real-time applications.

Overall, DRL significantly enhances the efficiency, adaptability, and scalability of optical networks. Compared to conventional heuristic-based methods, DRL offers superior performance in terms of resource utilization, blocking probability reduction, and adaptability to dynamic network conditions. However, challenges such as high computational complexity, data requirements, and model interpretability remain critical barriers to widespread deployment. Future research should focus on developing lightweight DRL models, integrating knowledge-driven learning mechanisms, and leveraging hybrid AI approaches to further optimize optical network operations.

## <span id="page-43-0"></span>**V. DISCUSSIONS, CHALLENGES, AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS**

# A. DISCUSSION OF KEY FINDINGS

In the context of optical communication systems, both ML and DL techniques have demonstrated considerable potential for enhancing system performance. A number of studies have showcased how these technologies can improve tasks such as signal detection, modulation recognition, and error correction. For instance, supervised learning algorithms, such as SVMs, have been widely used in OFC to improve modulation format classification, thereby contributing to better channel estimation and interference management.

However, despite the promising performance of these models, several issues remain unresolved. DNNs, while offering higher accuracy in tasks like channel equalization and signal demodulation, come with significant computational costs. This raises concerns about the feasibility of deploying such models in real-time systems, particularly for large-scale optical communication networks.

## B. CATEGORIZATION OF CHALLENGES

Several challenges hinder the wide-scale adoption of ML and DL in optical communication systems:

Data-Related Challenges: One of the primary challenges is the availability of high-quality labeled data for training ML models. In optical communication, acquiring sufficient labeled data, especially under diverse operating conditions (e.g., varying signal-to-noise ratios, different modulation

formats), is time-consuming and costly. Moreover, a lack of labeled data often results in overfitting, compromising model generalization [\[257\].](#page-51-24)

<span id="page-44-0"></span>Modeling Challenges: Choosing the appropriate ML or DL model for a given optical communication task is a nontrivial decision. For instance, while DNNs are widely adopted for signal classification and detection, simpler models such as decision trees or random forests may provide comparable performance with reduced computational overhead. Furthermore, model interpretability remains a significant challenge, especially in the context of opaque DL techniques, making it difficult to understand and justify model decisions in realworld scenarios [\[258\].](#page-51-25)

<span id="page-44-1"></span>Computational and Resource Challenges: DL models, especially those involving multiple layers and parameters, require substantial computational resources. This includes both high-performance computing for model training and memory for model deployment. These constraints limit the practical application of DL in large-scale networks, where real-time decision-making and resource constraints are critical [\[259\].](#page-51-26)

<span id="page-44-2"></span>Integration and Scalability Issues: Integrating ML and DL techniques into existing optical communication infrastructures, especially in large-scale, high-speed optical networks, presents additional hurdles. Many traditional optical systems were not designed with these advanced ML techniques in mind, requiring significant modifications in terms of architecture, hardware, and network design [\[260\].](#page-51-27)

# C. GAPS IN EXISTING RESEARCH

While there has been substantial progress in applying ML and DL to optical communication systems, there remain significant gaps in existing research. Most of the current literature focuses on theoretical models or laboratory-scale setups, with limited attention to large-scale real-world implementations. Few studies have investigated the long-term stability and scalability of ML models when deployed in live optical networks. Furthermore, the integration of RL techniques—particularly for dynamic optimization in network control—remains an underexplored avenue. RL has the potential to continuously adapt to changes in network conditions, optimizing network performance without human intervention, but its application in optical communication systems has yet to be fully explored.

Moreover, there is insufficient attention given to the interpretability of ML models. While DL techniques such as CNNs and RNNs have demonstrated high accuracy in tasks like signal classification, understanding why and how these models make specific decisions is still a challenge. This lack of transparency poses problems for the widespread adoption of ML in critical communication systems.
