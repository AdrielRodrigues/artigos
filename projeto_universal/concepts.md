# Key Concepts

Reference notes synthesizing the core concepts needed to read this large collection of papers on 6G networks, AI-native optical/wireless infrastructure, edge-cloud computing, network automation, and machine learning for optical networks. Given the collection's breadth, concepts are grouped thematically.

## 6G Vision, Standardization, and Performance Requirements

### 6G Standardization Roadmap and Performance Envelope
<!-- sources: optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited, toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 0243b2fe66ab -->
6G is oriented toward sustainability, inclusivity and ubiquitous intelligence, and is framed as an "Intelligent Network of Everything" that integrates AI with the Internet of Everything (IoE) through "mobile intelligence" embedded from the core to the air interface. It follows analog voice (1G), digital voice/SMS (2G; GSM/CDMA, GPRS/EDGE), mobile internet (3G; HSPA, CDMA-2000), all-IP LTE broadband (4G; MIMO and OFDM, 3GPP Releases 8-14) and 5G (ultra-high data rates, mmWave, massive MIMO, network slicing, IoT connectivity including RedCap, Reduced Capability, devices for AR and IoT). The roadmap is guided by the ITU-R IMT-2030 recommendations, with 3GPP Release 20 (5G-Advanced) as a bridge and Release 21 expected to start the first normative 6G specifications. It comprises three extensions of the IMT-2020 pillars and three innovations. The extensions are: eMBB redefined as immersive communication (supported by extremely large-scale MIMO, XL-MIMO, and cell-free MIMO, CF-MIMO); mMTC expanded into massive communication integrating non-terrestrial networks (NTNs) for global IoE coverage; and URLLC evolved into hyper-reliable low-latency communication (HRLLC), relying on extreme-edge computation and the THz band for critical tasks such as remote surgery. The innovations are ubiquitous connectivity (seamless access through cell-free architectures combining sensors and devices, e.g., precision farming), integrated sensing and communication (ISAC), and integrated AI and communication (IAAC), which embeds AI into the protocol stack. Standardization is coordinated by ITU-R (IMT-2030 framework), 3GPP (studies in Release 20, normative standards in Release 21) and World Radio Conferences (spectrum allocation); requirements are defined around 2024-2026, radio interface technologies selected around 2027-2030, and commercial deployment projected around 2030. Open challenges are the lack of global consensus on performance requirements, possible divergence between bodies such as 3GPP and ETSI, difficulty integrating AI across protocol-stack layers, spectrum planning with 5G backward compatibility, and security and privacy. Global research began about 2018 with the Finnish 6G Flagship, expanded in 2020-2021 with investments from South Korea, Japan, the USA and the EU (e.g., Hexa-X) focused on THz and advanced MIMO, and shifted in 2022-2023 toward AI-driven optimization and NTNs. Relative to IMT-2020, targets are: peak data rate from 20 Gbps to 1 Tbps; user-experienced rate from 100 Mbps to 1-10 Gbps; area traffic capacity from 10 Mbps/m² to 1-10 Gbps/m²; latency from 1 ms to 0.1 ms; reliability from 1-10^-5 to between 1-10^-7 and 1-10^-9 (up to 99.99999% or more); connection density from 10^6 to 10^7-10^8 devices/km²; mobility from 500 to 1000 km/h; and positioning accuracy of 1-10 cm (down to 1 cm). Six defining features are named: extreme capacity/performance, ultra-flexibility/agility (SDN, dynamic spectrum management), high intelligence/awareness, ubiquitous availability/reliability through terrestrial-NTN integration, true sustainability (energy harvesting, green design) and comprehensive zero-trust security. Use cases span communication-oriented ones (capacity, latency, mobility) and beyond-communication ones (intelligence, sensing, energy efficiency), including holographic communications, the Metaverse, digital twins (including ultra-precise ones), smart cities and factories, autonomous vehicles, remote robotic surgery and AI-driven healthcare.

From the optical transport layer, these goals translate into peak per-device rates above 1 Tbps (a 50-100x increase over 5G, with some holographic streams potentially needing more than 4 Tbps); deterministic end-to-end latency of 10-100 μs; "seven to nine nines" reliability (99.9999% to 99.9999999%); 10^7 to 10^8 devices per km²; nanosecond-level synchronisation for beamforming and distributed computing; a 100x improvement in bits per joule (although total energy may still rise with traffic volume); and AI/ML embedded in the network fabric for design, operation and optimization rather than overlaid. Meeting these simultaneously shifts the bottleneck from the radio interface to transport and computing, so the x-haul (fronthaul, midhaul, backhaul) must evolve from a passive "bit pipe" into an active platform integrating communication, computation and sensing within a "computational continuum"; edge-computing placement becomes a primary architectural concern, since slow transport would otherwise neutralize Tbps radio capability. Fixed networks are positioned as a complement: F5G Advanced targets at least 10 G fiber-to-the-room, latency below 1 ms and autonomous networking, while F6G and the IOWN (Innovative Optical and Wireless Network) vision move to All-Photonic Networks (APNs), shifting connectivity from site-to-site to memory-to-memory with bandwidth above 10 Tbit/s per endpoint. To reduce mobile-traffic latency further, an extended Cooperative Transport Interface (CTI) is proposed that lets the APN controller receive information directly from the CU/DU, coordinating optical resources with RAN scheduling rather than reacting after the fact; these generations are expected to combine AI-driven automation, photonic switching and optical bypass of the OLT for an end-to-end all-optical path. From the operator viewpoint (Orange), the transition should be evolutionary rather than revolutionary to avoid massive hardware overhauls; global mobile traffic growth is slowing owing to market saturation and better compression, yet incremental capacity and performance gains are still needed. Key KPIs include area capacity up to 3 Tb/s/km² in dense urban areas and a tenfold energy-efficiency improvement over 5G. Enablers include the upper mid-band (FR3, 7.125-24.25 GHz) with 6 GHz and 7-15 GHz ranges for capacity; higher-order MIMO and narrow beamforming; sub-THz bands for sensing and obstacle penetration; satellite integration; and cloud-native, AI-native architectures (Level 4 automation) with Open RAN softwarization for security and interoperability. Sustainability is a central constraint given the ICT sector's carbon footprint: Orange targets net-zero emissions by 2040, with most emissions in Scope 3 (indirect), so 6G design must favor power-efficient technologies and circular-economy principles. Efficiency directions include green hardware, self-organizing networks, expanded RedCap standards and further use of flexible 5G numerologies (sub-carrier spacing) for QoS-based customization. In the optical layer, fully optical networks minimizing optical-electrical-optical (O-E-O) conversions are estimated to cut greenhouse-gas emissions by up to 88%, with low-power photonic integrated circuits and AI-driven transceiver sleep modes at low traffic adding savings; energy-efficiency key value indicators (KVIs) are proposed as mandatory hardware-procurement criteria, with sustainability (environmental and social, including the digital divide) treated as a design requirement. Realizing the vision depends on an ecosystem of manufacturers, cloud providers, regulators and standards bodies (ITU, IEEE, 3GPP, O-RAN, ETSI).

### Shannon-Capacity Levers for Wireless Generations
<!-- sources: toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges | gen: 331c2df01ea7 -->
Wireless evolution from 1G to 6G can be read through Shannon's capacity theorem, C = N B log2(1 + SINR), in which data rate grows with three levers: bandwidth B, the number of parallel antenna pairs N, and the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio. Generational gains, driven by advances in VLSI circuits, MIMO and OFDM, have been obtained by pushing these levers in turn. For bandwidth, the move toward higher frequencies (mmWave in 5G, the THz band in 6G) raises per-user bandwidth from MHz to GHz. For antenna pairs, scaling from high-order MIMO to massive MIMO, and in 6G to extremely large-scale MIMO (XL-MIMO), creates more parallel data paths and spatial degrees of freedom; cell-free MIMO (CF-MIMO) removes traditional cell boundaries by serving users from distributed cooperating access points. Together XL-MIMO and CF-MIMO are the enablers of immersive communication, the 6G redefinition of eMBB. For channel conditions, reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) transform the propagation environment from an uncontrollable impairment into a controllable variable that enhances received signal strength and thus SINR. The same view motivates the pairing of THz spectrum with large arrays and RIS to compensate propagation losses, and it frames 6G capacity growth as a combination of all three levers rather than of any single technology.

### Terahertz (THz) Communication
<!-- sources: toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges | gen: 149abae7df0d -->
Terahertz (THz) communications, covering roughly 0.1-10 THz, are presented as the main spectrum route to Tbps data rates (peak rates up to 1 Tbps) in 6G and to high-precision sensing, complementing the cmWave and mmWave bands; a central question is whether THz spectrum can be used effectively to meet future capacity needs. The wide contiguous bandwidth is offset by extreme free-space and molecular-absorption path loss, short range, strong sensitivity to blockage, and RF front-end impairments (phase noise, nonlinearity, limited transmit power). Spectrum planning is further complicated by the lack of regulatory consensus on THz allocations and by the need for backward compatibility with 5G for a smooth transition. Ultra-massive MIMO (UM-MIMO) is the principal mitigation: arrays with hundreds to thousands of antenna elements, made feasible by the sub-millimeter wavelength, form highly directional beams whose gain compensates the path loss and which also provide spatial resolution for sensing and positioning. Because the arrays are large, beam alignment and tracking, channel estimation, and hybrid analog-digital architectures become central design issues. In the broader 6G picture, THz and UM-MIMO are paired with reconfigurable intelligent surfaces and AI-based beam management to extend coverage around blockages, and sub-THz bands are also considered for sensing in ISAC scenarios.

Orbital angular momentum (OAM) multiplexing adds a spatial multiplexing dimension to sub-THz links: beams with helical phase fronts carry mutually orthogonal integer modes (topological charges) that can share the same frequency and carry independent data streams. A proof of concept in the 135-170 GHz range generated and separated the OAM beams with a wideband Butler matrix, a passive analog beamforming circuit that produces orthogonal phase progressions across array elements, so mode generation and demultiplexing require no digital signal processing. Using uniform circular arrays (UCAs) 6 cm in diameter over a 1 m link, the system reached 1.58 Tbps with mode isolation above 15 dB between OAM modes. The range is short because OAM beams diverge with distance and THz propagation losses are high; parabolic reflectors are noted as a means of extending the distance to about 20 m. The result shows that Tbps-class wireless links are feasible at short range and that sub-THz bandwidth combined with OAM multiplexing can deliver such rates, in line with the 6G aim of using higher spectrum through innovative multiplexing techniques, while range, alignment, and regulatory issues of THz spectrum remain open.

### Optical Wireless Communication (OWC) and Millimeter Wave (mmWave)
<!-- sources: a_comprehensive_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications, enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity | gen: f5b47dc50dc8 -->
Optical wireless communication (OWC), using infrared, visible and ultraviolet spectra, is a 6G spectrum option for high-bandwidth indoor access and outdoor backhaul. In OWC, ML addresses atmospheric turbulence in free-space optics (FSO) and interference, positioning and resource management in visible light communication (VLC). For FSO, ANNs perform adaptive wavefront correction (Strehl ratio > 0.9), CNNs provide efficient wavefront correction and demodulate OAM modes under strong turbulence with >99% accuracy, SVMs maintain reliability under noise and turbulence, and GRUs predict channel conditions from weather data with <6.9% error. Random Forest and XGBoost predict RSSI in hybrid FSO/RF systems for seamless switching during blockages (Random Forest reaching 100% accuracy versus Gradient Boosting). ICA separates wavelength-multiplexed signals for multi-user detection, including underdetermined cases and NOMA integration; K-means identifies users in multi-user FSO; hierarchical clustering supports FSO MANET routing. Beam management for FSO, especially toward UAVs, uses computer vision and RL for automated beam steering and tracking, and physical-layer adaptive modulation and coding is driven by environmental data such as rain and fog. For VLC, SVMs raise data rates by 35% over direct detection and cut BER by 60% at 10 dB SNR in phase estimation versus Viterbi; ANNs mitigate artificial-light interference and lift a MIMO system from 200 kbps to 1.8 Mbps; LSTMs with attention mitigate ISI and flicker; DNN post-equalizers outperform LMS and Volterra in underwater VLC. Indoor positioning uses kNN RSSI fingerprinting (~2.7 cm), linear regression (~90% error reduction), kernel ridge regression (up to 48% further), policy-based RL (5 cm MAE) and CNN preprocessing (50% gain). EM provides pilotless channel estimation for OOK-FSO and OFDM-VLC (BER 10^-3 at 10 dB SNR). Q-learning and DRL handle power allocation, relay selection and handover in hybrid LiFi/RF networks for 6G. Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) are combined with LiFi/VLC to bypass line-of-sight limitations.

### Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RIS)
<!-- sources: toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges | gen: 6afe7ffdf574 -->
Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) and holographic MIMO (HMIMO) are two antenna paradigms proposed for 6G to raise spectral efficiency and coverage. A RIS is a programmable metasurface whose passive elements adjust the phase (and in some designs amplitude) of incident electromagnetic waves, steering reflections toward chosen directions. In terms of the Shannon capacity expression, RIS acts on the SINR term: it turns the propagation environment from an uncontrollable impairment into a controllable variable that can be tuned to enhance signal strength. This lets the network bypass blockages, extend coverage in shadowed or high-frequency (mmWave/THz) areas and shape the propagation environment at low power cost, since no active RF chains are needed on the surface; RIS has also been combined with LiFi/VLC to overcome line-of-sight limitations. Holographic MIMO uses dense, contiguous sub-wavelength elements that act as a single continuous aperture, approaching a continuous electromagnetic-field description and enabling near-ideal spatial multiplexing and very fine beam control beyond conventional massive MIMO. Both are positioned alongside ultra-massive MIMO as enablers for THz links, sensing and cell-free deployments, with open issues in channel estimation, control signaling and optimization of large numbers of elements, where AI/ML is expected to help. Future research directions include seamless RIS integration with low-complexity channel estimation to overcome THz attenuation and enable adaptive beamforming, and the use of RIS for physical layer security combined with federated learning.

### Multi-Waveform Air Interface, NOMA, and Grant-Free Access
<!-- sources: toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity | gen: 844cc2270528 -->
Transmission schemes proposed for the 6G air interface aim to raise capacity and cut latency, particularly for massive IoT. Multi-waveform schemes make the air interface adaptable, using different waveforms tailored to specific environments and services rather than a single fixed waveform. Advanced modulation and coding employs higher-order QAM together with probabilistic shaping to balance throughput and reliability near the channel capacity limit. Non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) allows multiple users to share the same time-frequency resources through power-domain or code-domain separation, increasing spectral efficiency and connection density, which suits massive IoT. Grant-free medium access lets devices transmit immediately without waiting for a base-station scheduling grant, removing the request-grant handshake and reducing latency and signaling overhead for sporadic IoT traffic. Learning-based solutions are also envisioned to replace inflexible PHY and MAC algorithms with adaptive ones in the intelligent air interface.

### Non-Terrestrial and Ultra-Dense Network Integration (SAGIN/INTN, UDN, IAB)
<!-- sources: toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges | gen: 3127c4246fe7 -->
Ubiquitous coverage in 6G relies on architectural changes that integrate non-terrestrial and terrestrial resources. Integrated non-terrestrial networks (INTNs), in the form of space-air-ground integrated networks (SAGIN), combine satellites, high-altitude platforms and drones (UAVs) with terrestrial cells to provide global coverage, including remote and disaster areas, and to support cellular-connected UAVs for logistics and surveillance. In the IMT-2030 framing, this integration is what extends 5G massive machine-type communication into 'massive communication': NTNs and satellites provide global coverage for the Internet of Everything (IoE), and they are part of the ubiquitous-connectivity innovation, which aims at seamless access regardless of location by combining sensors and devices through cell-free architectures, with use cases such as precision farming. Ultra-dense networks (UDNs) deploy massive numbers of small cells to raise capacity in urban areas. Integrated access and backhaul (IAB) uses the same spectrum and nodes for user access and wireless backhaul, reducing dependence on fiber in dense deployments. Cell-free massive MIMO removes cell boundaries by having a distributed set of cooperating access points serve users jointly, aiming at uniform service quality across the coverage area. Together with AI-native control and softwarization, these architectures underpin the 6G goal of high availability and reliability through terrestrial-NTN convergence.

### RF Energy Harvesting and Backscatter Communication
<!-- sources: toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity | gen: dcac8d3fd120 -->
Sustainability is one of the defining features of 6G, and several techniques target the energy footprint of networks and devices. Green networking focuses on powering down underutilized network elements and sharing virtualized resources so that energy use follows traffic load. Energy harvesting (EH), in particular RF energy harvesting, converts ambient or dedicated radio signals into electrical power so that autonomous IoT devices can operate without batteries or with much longer lifetimes. Backscatter communication reflects and modulates ambient RF signals instead of generating its own carrier, allowing ultra-low-power links for battery-free IoT devices. Combined with energy-efficient hardware, AI-driven resource management and circular-economy design, these approaches aim to reduce the carbon footprint of ICT while supporting the connection densities of 10^7-10^8 devices/km² targeted for 6G.

### Advanced Channel Coding for 6G
<!-- sources: toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity | gen: 02f20aab4794 -->
Supporting peak rates of about 1 Tbps with low latency is expected to require channel coding beyond the LDPC and Polar codes used in 5G. Candidate options include: non-binary codes, which use higher-dimensional alphabets for improved resilience at high data rates; fountain (rateless) codes, which generate a potentially unlimited stream of encoded symbols so that a receiver needs only a sufficient subset to reconstruct the data, making them robust to packet loss; lattice codes, which exploit multidimensional signal-space structure for efficient packing and interference management in MIMO; sparse regression codes (SPARCs), which use structured codebooks to approach capacity with low complexity; and application-layer coding, which provides flexible protection tailored to data formats such as multimedia and IoT traffic. The trade-offs among decoding complexity, latency, throughput and reliability determine which scheme suits each 6G service.

## RAN Architecture, Disaggregation, and X-Haul Transport

### RAN Architecture Evolution (D-RAN → C-RAN → HC-RAN/F-RAN → vRAN → O-RAN)
<!-- sources: toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: 3e05ab25254d -->
The radio access network (RAN) has passed through several architectural stages, each changing where baseband processing is located and therefore how much capacity the fronthaul, which connects Remote Units (RUs) to Distributed/Digital Units (DUs), must carry. Distributed RAN (D-RAN) collocates the baseband unit (BBU) with the remote radio head (RRH) at each cell site; it is simple but lacks flexibility and scalability. Centralized RAN (C-RAN) pools BBUs in a central location, which enables coordinated multipoint (CoMP) and carrier aggregation and reduces maintenance cost, but requires a high-capacity fronthaul between pooled BBUs and radio heads. Heterogeneous C-RAN (HC-RAN) combines macro and small base stations to improve throughput and energy efficiency in dense deployments. Fog-RAN (F-RAN) decentralizes functions toward the network edge to reduce latency for IoT and autonomous applications. Virtualized RAN (v-RAN) replaces proprietary hardware with software running on commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) servers (virtualized BBUs), with reported energy savings of up to 84% versus D-RAN. Open RAN (O-RAN) is an industry initiative that defines open, interoperable interfaces between the O-RU, O-DU and O-CU, avoiding vendor lock-in and facilitating AI integration. As functions are disaggregated and centralized, the RU-DU fronthaul becomes the main bottleneck for the Tbps capacity and sub-millisecond latency targeted by 6G, which is why the choice of functional split (from Option 8 to the O-RAN 7.x family) and of optical transport (point-to-point, PON or free-space optics) becomes central. Because different use cases need different trade-offs between RU complexity and transport capacity, standardized multivendor interfaces are regarded as necessary so that components of different transport technologies can coexist.

### O-RAN Architecture: RU/DU/CU Split and RIC Hierarchy
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research, optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: 2fd60793a0bd -->
O-RAN disaggregates the traditional baseband unit (BBU) into a Radio Unit (RU), a Distributed Unit (DU) and a Central Unit (CU), the latter split into Control Plane and User Plane parts, with RAN functions implemented as software components (VNFs). Its open, interoperable interfaces between O-RU, O-DU and O-CU are intended to avoid vendor lock-in, to allow functional splits (such as the 7.x family) to be tailored to specific use cases, and to ease the integration of AI. For the optical fronthaul, an urgent need is standardized multivendor interfaces so that point-to-point, PON and FSO components can coexist. A hierarchical control structure is added through two RAN Intelligent Controllers (RICs). The Non-Real-Time (Non-RT) RIC handles non-time-critical tasks (timescales above 1 s) through rApps and provides policies to the Near-RT RIC over the A1 interface; it is managed within the Service Management and Orchestration (SMO) framework. The Near-RT RIC manages O-RAN nodes through xApps on a timescale of 10 ms to 100 ms, using the E2 interface for data monitoring and action execution; the O1 interface carries feedback and management to nodes such as the O-CU. AI/ML applications are hosted along three control loops: Loop 1 at the O-DU (per transmission time interval, TTI), Loop 2 at the Near-RT RIC (10 ms to 1 s) and Loop 3 at the Non-RT RIC (above 1 s). Lightweight distributed applications (dApps) can additionally run close to the O-CU. This structure is the substrate on which AI-driven functions such as spectrum management, power allocation and slicing, and their explanation counterparts, are deployed. In fixed-mobile convergence, Open RAN and Cloud RAN are presented as enablers of softwarization that improve security and interoperability, and as the basis for network slicing over shared infrastructure. A proposed step is to converge OLT management with the RICs, so that the optical access controller and the radio controllers coordinate resource allocation across fixed and mobile services.

### Fronthaul, Midhaul, and Backhaul Segmentation (X-Haul)
<!-- sources: ai-driven_converged_metro-access_optical_network-as-a-service_with_point-to-multipoint_coherent_optics_for_6g_x-hauling, enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, forthcoming_optical_x-haul_infrastructure_supporting_6g_mobile_network_requirements, optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 217b9a7019a7 -->
The x-haul network of 6G (IMT-2030) comprises the fronthaul (FH, RU to DU), the midhaul (MH, DU to CU) and the backhaul (BH, CU to core network). In the disaggregated RAN, the choice of functional split (FS) places distinct capacity and latency demands on each of these segments, so transport infrastructure has to be designed per segment rather than uniformly. RAN architecture has evolved from Distributed RAN (D-RAN, BBU co-located with the radio head at each cell site, simple but inflexible and costly for slicing) through Centralized RAN (C-RAN, pooled BBUs that cut maintenance cost but burden fronthaul, in fully or partially centralized forms), Heterogeneous C-RAN (HC-RAN, integrating macro and micro cells for dense urban 5G), Fog-RAN (F-RAN, fog nodes bringing compute closer to users to cut latency for holographic communication and robotics) and Virtualized RAN (V-RAN, software on COTS servers, with energy savings of up to 84% versus D-RAN) to Open RAN (O-RAN), which adds open multi-vendor interfaces and eases AI/ML integration for dynamic resource allocation. In this progression the DU-RU fronthaul becomes the main bottleneck for the Tbps capacity and sub-millisecond latency targeted by 6G. Current 5G transport cannot meet these targets for three reasons: CPRI and eCPRI lack the scalability for 6G fronthaul, which may require more than 500 Gbps per cell and more than 10 Tbps per site; standard Ethernet introduces packet delay variation (jitter) incompatible with microsecond budgets, and TDM-PON dynamic bandwidth allocation adds more than 100 μs of latency; and PTP over standard Ethernet struggles to deliver stable nanosecond synchronisation. The proposed response is a unified, open and intelligent x-haul with five traits: convergence of fronthaul, midhaul and backhaul over shared physical infrastructure to optimize resources across user, control and AI traffic; flexible functional splits (FFS) that dynamically adjust the DU/CU division to trade bandwidth demand against latency (for example moving processing closer to the RU for URLLC); AI-native integration, with ML embedded in optical elements for real-time automation; openness through O-RAN interfaces to avoid vendor lock-in; and reconfigurable WDM networks with ROADMs extended to the network edge, moving toward mesh or flattened topologies for resilience. This architecture sits in a Cloud-Edge-Device continuum in which multi-access edge computing (MEC) and Edge AI handle latency-sensitive tasks, and joint communication-computation optimisation (JCC) manages optical bandwidth and CPU/GPU resources as a single entity for task placement. Flexibility moves functions in real time but increases management complexity across a multidimensional resource space (wavelengths, compute, storage), making AI orchestration indispensable, and the stringent KPIs push optical networks to behave as real-time systems similar to industrial control or Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN), where temporal stability matters as much as bandwidth. Disaggregated RANs split base-station functions into Central Units (CUs), Distributed Units (DUs) and Radio Units (RUs), with fronthaul (RU-DU) and midhaul (DU-CU) transport segments that must carry high capacity under strict latency, while the backhaul aggregates traffic toward the core and is the segment where ultra-wideband (UWB) and space division multiplexing (SDM) scaling is targeted. The fronthaul interface is organized in functional layers: RRC (signaling), PDCP (header compression and security), RLC (reliability), MAC (scheduling and HARQ) and PHY (modulation, beamforming, MIMO). Splitting options 2-8 represent a trade-off between the benefits of centralization and the bandwidth required on the fronthaul. Option 8, the lower-layer split, is fully centralized and gives the simplest RU, but has the highest bandwidth and latency constraints: in extreme configurations with massive MIMO, fronthaul capacity can exceed 800 Gbps with latency requirements under 250 μs. Higher option numbers (such as 6 and 7) move more processing to the RU and reduce the required bitrate. The O-RAN 7.x family divides the PHY into 7.1, 7.2 and 7.3; Option 7.3 is the most efficient in bitrate because modulation/demodulation is moved to the RU, at the cost of greater RU complexity. Each deployment scenario therefore needs a different balance of RU complexity versus transport capacity, and O-RAN open interfaces allow splits to be tailored to use cases. Fiber is the primary medium for all x-haul segments, and 6G requires Tbps-range capacity, deterministic low latency and minimal jitter. Several fixed-access options are considered. Point-to-point (PtP) connectivity remains preferred for Fiber-to-the-Antenna (FTTA) and Fiber-to-the-Office (FTTO) because it offers full bandwidth and minimal signal-processing latency; current evolution targets 100 Gbit/s bidirectional transmission over 40 km, with a longer-term path toward 800 Gbit/s interfaces derived from data-center technology. Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) is considered where fiber is scarce; to limit chromatic dispersion at 50 or 100 Gbit/s, O-band transmission is preferred over the C-band, and PAM4 is the likely initial modulation for these rates. TDM/TDMA passive optical networks (PONs) suit high-density residential (FTTH) and small-business (FTTE) connections but are poorly matched to mobile fronthaul because the Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA) cycle introduces latency and packet delay variation; very high-speed PON (VHSP) at 200G may nonetheless serve private campus networks. Sustainability of x-haul can be improved with higher PON split ratios, highly dynamic sleep modes in network terminations and coordinated intelligent controllers. The proposed optical infrastructure addresses the three segments with distinct building blocks: a PIC-based optical filter for dynamic resource allocation and high-speed switching in fronthaul/midhaul, a multi-granular optical node (MG-ON) with a PIC-based waveband selective switch (WBSS) in backhaul, and energy-efficient optical-DAC transceivers, all programmable through a Service Management and Orchestration (SMO) framework for automation and end-to-end slicing. The O-RAN Alliance Option 7-2x split balances RU complexity against fronthaul bandwidth: for a 100 MHz radio channel with 64-QAM, peak fronthaul demand is about 22.2 Gb/s. This maps well onto DSCM-based optics, where a single 25 Gb/s digital subcarrier carries one fronthaul flow, giving an efficient one-to-one match between traffic demand and optical resources in a converged metro-access network. Cost pressure grows with network density and is mitigated by fiber sharing through resource pooling, topology optimization and power-saving modes in ONUs, as well as by reusing 5G assets, adopting Open RAN for vendor competition and sharing infrastructure among operators; latency and jitter, critical for industrial automation, can be reduced with CPRI over Ethernet (CoE). Feasibility under split 7-2x was assessed with a Python Monte Carlo simulator on two topologies, N1 (37 nodes, moderate connectivity) and N2 (63 nodes, high redundancy/diversity), composed of metro ROADM nodes (M-nodes) and access nodes (A-nodes) with DUs placed at M-nodes. The physical-layer budget includes fiber attenuation, ROADM insertion loss, amplifier noise and TRX impairments; BER is derived from generalized SNR (GSNR) combined with SNR_TRX, and latency from propagation delay (about 5 μs/km). Route feasibility falls as the hop limit grows, and beyond 8 hops most additional routes are infeasible because of accumulated physical impairments rather than latency; N2 is more sensitive than N1 owing to longer average paths. DU placement strongly affects the share of feasible routes: placing DUs at highly connected metro nodes shortens end-to-end paths and limits cumulative noise, and in a 3-DU scenario optimized placement increased strictly feasible routes (BER < 10^-3, latency < 250 μs) relative to suboptimal placements, making position more influential than DU count alone.

### Point-to-Point (P2P) Optical Fronthaul and Transceiver Form Factors
<!-- sources: toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: 9e4a878ff308 -->
Point-to-point (P2P) optical fronthaul uses dedicated fiber links between each Remote Unit and the Distributed Unit. It offers the lowest latency, the highest security and the maximum capacity of the optical options, and is therefore the natural fit for the most demanding functional splits, Option 8 and O-RAN 7.1, where fronthaul bitrates are highest. Its drawbacks are economic and operational: the number of fibers needed makes deployment cost extremely high, and because each link is dimensioned for peak demand, resources are fixed and poorly utilized. To mitigate this, XR optics uses coherent subcarrier aggregation, so that a single coherent transceiver can be divided into subcarriers that serve several endpoints, lowering cost and adding flexibility through software-driven reprogramming. For latency targets below 10 μs, future work points to P2P architectures with a unified management layer and real-time monitoring tools that isolate latency violations across network domains. P2P is regarded as the choice for ultra-high capacity, whereas PON targets cost-efficient density and free-space optics wireless flexibility, and hybrids of the three are considered the most likely way to meet the diverse requirements of 6G.

### Point-to-Multipoint (P2MP) Coherent Optics and Digital Subcarrier Multiplexing (DSCM)
<!-- sources: ai-driven_converged_metro-access_optical_network-as-a-service_with_point-to-multipoint_coherent_optics_for_6g_x-hauling, enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, forthcoming_optical_x-haul_infrastructure_supporting_6g_mobile_network_requirements | gen: b046b882012c -->
Current 5G fronthaul relies on intensity-modulated/direct-detection (IM/DD) transmission with wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM), but 6G is expected to shift toward coherent systems, which allow higher-order modulation across amplitude, phase and polarization, better compensation of impairments and superior receiver sensitivity. Conventional point-to-point (P2P) coherent architectures scale hardware linearly with the number of endpoints, fragment spectrum and handle asymmetric traffic poorly in dense deployments. Time division multiplexing (TDM) in passive optical networks (PONs) likewise limits capacity and adds access delay. Point-to-multipoint (P2MP) coherent transmission based on digital subcarrier multiplexing (DSCM) addresses this by dividing a wavelength into independently allocatable digital subcarriers (DSCs), enabling fine-grained bandwidth assignment and better spectral efficiency. In a representative design, a central 400G Hub is connected to multiple leaf nodes through a passive splitter/combiner; with DP-16QAM modulation, one 400G wavelength is divided into 16 DSCs of 25 Gb/s each. Bidirectional (BiDi) full-duplex operation over a single fiber is obtained with circulators, and the passive distribution segment avoids active electronics. A spatially diverse PtMP (SD-PtMP) variant built on subcarrier multiplexing adds flexible association of cell sites with different central offices (COs) through a PIC-based circular interlacer. This element consists of two stages of cascaded half-band Nyquist-shaped interleaver filters arranged in a butterfly pattern, shuffling subcarriers from four input sources into four unique output signals. Some subcarriers are lost in the filter transition bands (75% utilization), yet the architecture yields a net 3x capacity gain and offers lower loss and a smaller footprint than cyclic AWGR routers. Evaluation on the TEFNET24 topology shows that requirements vary significantly with ring size and transceiver density, calling for adaptive energy strategies. Transceiver (TRX) impairments were characterized on a back-to-back testbed with a 400G DSCM coherent pluggable, varying received optical power (ROP) with a variable optical attenuator: SNR_TRX saturates at high ROP and degrades steeply near the receiver sensitivity limit, and this model was embedded in the network simulator. In a techno-economic comparison against P2P over a 10-year traffic projection (17.2% CAGR), P2MP reduced transceivers at DU sites by about 75% and network-wide transceivers by up to 74.1%, delayed router port expansion thanks to subcarrier-level aggregation, and cut power consumption by 27-30% under high spectral loads, lowering OPEX.

### Precision Time Synchronisation for Optical Transport
<!-- sources: towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 077fb181cb51 -->
Nanosecond-level synchronisation is a hard requirement of 6G optical transport and acts as a 'hidden' enabler, since even small timing deviations can collapse beamforming or URLLC performance. It is mandatory for coordinated multipoint (CoMP) and beamforming, which depend on phase alignment of signals from multiple antennas or RUs; for carrier aggregation, which needs timing alignment across frequency bands; for hyper-reliable low-latency communication (HRLLC), which relies on deterministic resource scheduling for industrial control; and for integrated sensing and communication (ISAC), where precise distance and velocity measurements require tightly aligned clocks. Current 5G transport falls short because Precision Time Protocol (PTP) over standard Ethernet struggles to reach stable nanosecond precision, and packet delay variation (PDV) from Ethernet and from TDM-PON dynamic bandwidth allocation degrades timing. PTP (IEEE 1588) is the primary solution, built on a clock hierarchy of Grandmaster Clocks (GMC), Boundary Clocks (BC) and Ordinary Clocks (OC). Meeting 6G accuracy requires high-precision hardware timestamping and the ITU-T G.827x profiles, which are needed to minimize PDV along the path. The main difficulty is maintaining nanosecond precision across heterogeneous links (fibre, free-space optics, satellite) that exhibit different jitter, which calls for advanced compensation and strict interoperability between vendors' equipment. Recommended standardisation work includes harmonizing PTP profiles across flexible fronthaul architectures, and the requirement links to the broader view of 6G optical networks as real-time systems comparable to Time-Sensitive Networking, where temporal stability is as important as raw bandwidth.

## Optical Access Technologies for Fronthaul (PON, WDM, RoF, FSO)

### PON Multiplexing Variants (TDM, WDM, TWDM, OFDM, OCDM, NOMA, PDM)
<!-- sources: toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 842b7688aa20 -->
A passive optical network (PON) uses a point-to-multipoint (P2MP) architecture in which passive power splitters share a feeder fiber among several optical network units, which makes it a cost-efficient option for dense fronthaul deployments. Standards have progressed from GPON to XGS-PON and on to 50G high-speed PON (HSP). Several variants are compared. TDM-PON is cost-effective but suffers from high latency because of the Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA) cycle, about 125 μs (latency above 100 μs in 6G assessments), which is a poor match for the sub-100 μs latency targets of 6G fronthaul. WDM-PON assigns dedicated wavelengths per user, giving logical point-to-point connections over a P2MP physical plant, low latency and high capacity without DBA, and is useful for traffic segmentation, but it has higher optical component cost. TWDM-PON combines time and wavelength multiplexing, is flexible and remains compatible with legacy systems. OFDM-PON and OCDM-PON offer high spectral efficiency and security and are suitable for Tbps targets. NOMA-PON uses power-domain multiplexing to serve more users per subcarrier. PDM-PON exploits polarization states to exceed 100 Gbps per wavelength. Beyond roughly 100 Gbps, intensity-modulation/direct-detection (IM/DD) PON runs into power-budget and dispersion limits. 50G-PON is an intermediate step that uses DSP for dispersion compensation but is insufficient for high-end fronthaul. Reaching Tbps aggregate rates is expected to require coherent PONs (CPON) delivering at least 100 Gbps per wavelength: coherent detection brings higher receiver sensitivity, spectrally efficient modulation (QAM) and linear dispersion compensation in DSP, while the main challenges are transceiver cost and burst-mode handling in the upstream. Research directions include reducing cost by repurposing existing residential PON fiber, PON virtualization for multi-tenant sharing, and energy savings through ONU sleep modes driven by AI-based DBA that enters low-power states at low traffic, together with decomposed arrayed waveguide grating routers (AWGR). Latency and jitter can be further reduced by encapsulating CPRI over Ethernet (CoE), and standardization of CPON is among the priorities. Authentication protocols and robust encryption are listed as priorities for PON security. PON is generally regarded as the choice for cost-efficient density, in contrast to point-to-point fiber for maximum capacity and free-space optics for wireless flexibility.

### Radio-over-Fiber (RoF): Analog, Digital, and Hybrid
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g | gen: 3261cc6c428b -->
Analog radio-over-fiber (ARoF) and its free-space variant RoFSO carry radio signals directly over optical channels, eliminating digital processing (such as digitization and baseband functions) at the Radio Unit. This simplifies the RU and supports massive MIMO and the low-latency requirements of 6G, avoiding the bandwidth expansion that digitized fronthaul interfaces impose. Power over Fiber (PoF) delivers optical power over the same fiber infrastructure, so that data and energy are transmitted simultaneously to Remote Radio Heads (RRHs). Because the RRH is powered remotely, it can enter deep-sleep states, which saves energy and reduces the need for local power supplies at cell sites. Among fiber-based fronthaul options compared in the literature, TWDM-PON is identified as the most cost-effective for high-speed, long-reach networks owing to its superior signal quality (Q-factor). Current 5G fronthaul relies on IM/DD with WDM, while coherent systems are expected to take over in 6G for higher-order modulation and better receiver sensitivity.

### Free-Space Optics (FSO) for Fronthaul
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 0deda50003bb -->
Optical wireless links (free-space optics, FSO, and LiFi/VLC) complement fiber in 6G fronthaul, and hybrid architectures are used to combine their strengths. FSO transmits data by laser or LED light beams through the atmosphere, offering about 100 Gbps capacity, unlicensed spectrum and easy deployment in hard-to-reach areas and non-terrestrial networks (NTNs), but it requires line of sight (LoS) and is highly susceptible to weather such as fog and rain. Mitigation combines adaptive optics against atmospheric turbulence with hybrid RF/FSO backup, for example with mmWave. Fiber integrated with microwave or mmWave radio improves versatility (easier deployment in rural or dense urban areas), resilience (the wireless path backs up cut fibers) and cost-effectiveness. FSO is sensitive to fog and dust, whereas mmWave penetrates fog and is more affected by rain; hybrid FSO/mmWave systems exploit this complementarity to reach carrier-grade availability of 99.999%. Applications include terrestrial tower links, non-terrestrial links with UAVs and high-altitude platforms (HAPs), and underwater communication. Analytical modeling adopts an intensity-modulation/direct-detection (IM/DD) system in which the received signal is y(t) = R·h·x(t) + n(t), with photodetector responsivity R, channel gain h and noise n. The total gain h is the sum of line-of-sight (LOS) and non-line-of-sight (NLOS) components. LiFi LOS gain follows a Lambertian radiation pattern determined by the LED half-power semi-angle and the receiver field of view. FSO LOS gain is the product of geometric spreading loss (h_geo), atmospheric attenuation following the Beer-Lambert law (h_atm) and pointing error from wind or vibration-induced jitter (h_point). NLOS gain matters mainly for indoor LiFi, where reflections from walls and ceilings contribute. The electrical SNR is (R·P)^2/σ_n^2, where the noise variance includes shot noise (proportional to received optical power, dominant in FSO and in LiFi with strong ambient light), thermal noise from receiver electronics such as transimpedance amplifiers (dominant at low power or long distance) and background noise from sunlight or artificial lighting. Recent work includes MINLP models for FSO link alignment and power allocation, FSO integrated with PON or mmWave to reduce fiber reliance while keeping throughput, RIS to circumvent LOS blockage in LiFi, and distributed MIMO LiFi with POF-wired fronthaul for high-capacity indoor IoT. Future directions include higher-order modulation formats to push FSO toward Tbps, adaptive modulation to improve reliability in low-cost deployments, adaptive optics against atmospheric turbulence, and physical-layer security such as beamforming and quantum key distribution. Spatial modulation with sparse massive MIMO (SM-MIMO) is proposed to increase FSO throughput and mitigate atmospheric beam wander through spatial diversity. AI orchestrates hybrid links by switching traffic among FSO, mmWave and fiber to maintain SLAs during weather events. Heterogeneous links (fiber, FSO, satellite) also exhibit varying jitter, which complicates nanosecond-level synchronisation across them.

### Silicon Photonics and Photonic Integrated Circuits
<!-- sources: towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: fd726dcc6a15 -->
Silicon photonics uses CMOS fabrication to integrate lasers, modulators and photodetectors on a single chip, forming photonic integrated circuits (PICs) that reduce size, cost and power consumption of optical transceivers and switching elements. Co-packaged optics (CPO) go further by integrating optical components directly with electronic packages, shortening electrical paths and lowering energy consumption. Constraints are economic as well as technical: there is a tension between extreme performance goals and CapEx/OpEx, so deployment is to be chosen per use case.

### Security of Optical Fronthaul
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: d0bc9a712128 -->
Optical physical layers in fronthaul are vulnerable to eavesdropping through fiber tapping and to denial of service through coordinated fiber cuts. Open architectures such as O-RAN with multi-vendor interfaces enlarge the attack surface, which motivates a Zero Trust security model based on continuous verification rather than implicit trust. Global standardisation is considered critical not only for cost but for systemic resilience: interchangeable, interoperable components prevent a single-vendor failure from causing widespread outages. Distinguishing malicious attacks from ordinary component failures is difficult, so AI-driven Attack Detection and Identification (ADI) systems are proposed that use supervised, unsupervised and semi-supervised learning: supervised models recognize known attack signatures, unsupervised and semi-supervised ones flag low-visibility anomalies and previously unseen or coordinated attacks. Effective ADI requires an evolution of Network Management Systems (NMS) toward high-frequency telemetry collection and Root Cause Analysis (RCA), so that signal and power anomalies can be attributed either to degradation (such as fiber aging or power budget violations) or to intrusion. Research on fronthaul emphasizes physical-layer security and ML analysis of Optical Performance Monitoring (OPM) data for intrusion detection, with RCA supporting autonomous security management. Complementary examples include PCA-based fiber perimeter intrusion detection (99% detection, 0% false positives). Future priorities include robust encryption, authentication protocols for PONs, and physical-layer security for FSO links such as beamforming and quantum key distribution. For 6G more broadly, a holistic zero-trust security architecture is proposed to span all layers from PHY to application, with deep learning applied at the physical layer against three threats: spoofing, addressed by CNNs/DNNs that analyze channel state information (CSI) for authentication; jamming, addressed by reinforcement learning (DQN) for adaptive frequency hopping; and eavesdropping, addressed by autoencoders (AE) that learn secure encoding and decoding of messages. Challenges include scarce data for rare events, black-box opacity and susceptibility of the AI models themselves to adversarial poisoning; mitigations include explainable AI, federated learning for privacy and digital twins to validate decisions before deployment.

## Elastic Optical Networks, Routing/Spectrum Assignment, and Resource Allocation

### Routing and Spectrum Assignment Problem Family
<!-- sources: deep_reinforcement_learning_for_resource_allocation_in_multi-band_optical_networks, next-generation_optical_networks_to_sustain_connectivity_of_the_future_all_roads_lead_to_optical-computing-enabled_network, optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: b595f7c83efd -->
Routing and Spectrum Assignment (RSA) is the problem of deciding, for each connection request, which route to use and which spectral resources to allocate along it. It arises from the evolution of wavelength switched optical networks (WSON), which route lightpaths on a fixed ITU-T grid (50 GHz spacing, limiting rates above 100 Gb/s), toward elastic optical networks (EONs). EONs use a flexible grid with slots in multiples of 12.5 GHz and configurable widths matched to actual bandwidth requirements, enabled by bandwidth-variable wavelength selective switches (BV-WSS) and bandwidth-variable transponders supporting multiple rates and modulation formats; this elasticity can reduce fiber spectrum usage by approximately 30%. The problem appears in several variants: Routing and Wavelength Assignment (RWA) for fixed-grid networks, RSA for flex-grid networks, and Routing, Modulation and Spectrum Assignment (RMSA), which adds modulation format selection as a decision variable. Dynamic operation is the focus of much recent work: requests arrive and expire according to probability distributions (typically exponential), unlike static or incremental traffic models, which lets the network react to shifting demand and reduces the margins otherwise needed to absorb uncertainty. Allocation is governed by three main constraints: spectrum continuity (the same frequency slots on every link of a lightpath), spectrum contiguity (allocated slots must be adjacent) and no reconfiguration (established lightpaths cannot be moved without disruption). Integer Linear Programming (ILP) gives exact solutions for static traffic but is computationally infeasible for dynamic traffic, so heuristics or learned policies are used instead. In multi-band networks the decision extends to a tuple of (path, band, channel), and in multiband over SDM networks it further includes the spatial resource (core), so that the process consists of finding a topological path and assigning band, core and frequency slot. The options are typically restricted to a set of precomputed candidate paths. Path computation is increasingly decoupled from path validation: candidate routes are checked with analytical or AI/ML Quality of Transmission (QoT) estimators, such as GNPy, rather than embedding physical-layer models in the path computation itself. The traditional approach uses heuristics such as k-Shortest Path (k-SP) routing combined with First-Fit (FF) spectrum assignment, which picks the first available block of slots. When properly tuned these are strong baselines: ordering candidate paths by hop count rather than kilometers and using K=50 candidate paths with KSP-FF or FF-KSP lowers blocking by large factors compared with the K=5, distance-ordered versions often used as benchmarks. They are simple and slightly more efficient at very low traffic loads, but they struggle with the non-linear physical-layer constraints and the larger channel counts of multi-band systems, and they do not adapt their path assignment to congestion. A learned policy, in contrast, is trained through rewards and penalties on interactions with the network and can adjust its choices dynamically. In adaptive EONs, where RSA is solved on-line per request, RL-based provisioning outperforms conventional dynamic heuristics in spectrum utilization and blocking probability by learning the long-term impact of each decision. To keep the problem tractable, many frameworks use the RL agent only for routing (for example choosing among candidate paths) while a heuristic such as first-fit performs spectrum allocation. Prediction-based schemes take a different route: short-term forecasts of arrival and departure times and spectrum utilization are used to update link weights, so that routing favors resources that raise the likelihood of accepting future requests and reduce blocking, although they can underperform non-predictive routing if the forecasts are inaccurate. Evaluation metrics include blocking probability (BP), which grows with traffic load; network throughput; and the average transmitter/receiver (TX/RX) interface usage per demand. In a reported evaluation on the 14-node, 44-link Japanese topology with Poisson traffic from 200 to 450 Erlang and 400 Gb/s requests, a PPO-based DRL agent reached a BP of 3.1×10⁻² at 450 Erlang versus 7.7×10⁻² for a standard RL-based RSA and 16.5×10⁻² for k-SP FF, i.e., about 80% lower BP than k-SP FF and 50% lower than RL-RSA, with throughput gains of 50% and 20%. The agent also used fewer TX/RX interfaces per demand than RL-RSA, because it favored shorter paths with higher GSNR that allow a single channel rather than several lower-order ones. In a multigranular MBoSDM proof of concept, a two-step algorithm first tries to find a path over existing virtual flexigrid links and, if that fails, instantiates a new core service from source to destination to create a new virtual DWDM link. In optical-computing-enabled networks the RWA/RSA problem is extended to the Routing, Wavelength and Network Coding Assignment (RWNCA) problem. Beyond route and wavelength selection, RWNCA must also determine the encoding pairs of lightpaths, the computing nodes where encoding occurs (e.g., node 7 or 8 in an illustrative NSFNET instance), and the routing of the resulting computed lightpaths. This added layer makes RWNCA roughly one order of magnitude harder computationally than RWA, and this enlarged problem defines 'optical network design and planning 2.0'.

### Heuristic Baselines for Dynamic RMSA
<!-- sources: reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope | gen: 69d95275044f -->
Heuristic baselines for dynamic routing, modulation and spectrum assignment combine a candidate-path routine with a spectrum-assignment rule. KSP-FF computes K shortest paths and, for each in order, tries first-fit spectrum allocation, taking the first path that succeeds; FF-KSP instead scans the spectrum slot by slot and selects the first slot index that is available on any of the K paths. Other evaluated variants include KSP-BF and BF-KSP (best-fit), KME-FF and KCA-FF. The criterion used to sort candidate paths strongly affects performance: ordering by number of hops rather than by physical length in kilometers significantly reduces service blocking probability (SBP), because a path with fewer hops consumes fewer total spectral resources across the network even when it is slightly longer. Experiments on four topologies (NSFNET, COST239, USNET, JPN48) show that SBP decreases as the number of candidate paths K increases, generally stabilizing at around K=50. The best heuristic depends on network size: in smaller networks (NSFNET, COST239) KSP-FF and KME-FF perform best, while in larger networks (USNET, JPN48) FF-KSP is significantly superior. The strongest benchmarks identified are therefore KSP-FF or FF-KSP with K=50 and hop-count ordering. These tuned heuristics are deterministic and fast, and they outperform or match most published RL-based RMSA and RSA results, which typically used K=5 and distance ordering.

### Blocking Probability, Traffic Models, and Benchmarking Rigor
<!-- sources: reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope | gen: 9f85a731be88 -->
Evaluation of reinforcement learning (RL) for dynamic resource allocation (DRA) in optical networks suffers from inconsistent benchmarking, poor reproducibility and limited generalizability. A manual review of 97 peer-reviewed papers on RWA, RSA, RMSA and related problems found that DeepRMSA established a de facto standard for problem definitions, but that many later works compare their RL solutions against weak versions of heuristics such as K-Shortest Paths First-Fit (KSP-FF), for example with few candidate paths and distance-based ordering. Simulation environments are also fragmented across incompatible toolkits, which prevents fair comparison. A further reproducibility flaw is an undocumented detail in the DeepRMSA codebase: service holding times are resampled when they exceed twice the mean. This truncation lowers the actual offered traffic load by approximately 31%, so several landmark papers that reused the code reported results under considerably lighter loads than they claimed. Recommended practice includes selecting and tuning the strongest possible baselines (optimizing the number of candidate paths and the path sort criteria), using deterministic algorithms for reproducibility, ensuring statistical significance with at least 100 blocking events per trial across multiple random seeds, and publishing open-source code built on established simulation frameworks. When five influential RL works (DeepRMSA, Reward-RMSA, GCN-RMSA, MaskRSA and PtrNet-RSA) were recreated and compared with both a basic KSP-FF (K=5) and an optimized heuristic (KSP-FF or FF-KSP with K=50 and hop-count ordering), simple heuristics outperformed or matched the published RL results in nearly all cases. Changing the path sort criterion to hop count often beat the RL results, and raising K to 50 improved heuristic performance by over an order of magnitude relative to most RL implementations. The conclusion drawn is that the success of RL in optical DRA had been overestimated because of deficient baselines and undocumented implementation details. Determining whether a meaningful optimality gap remains requires a lower bound on blocking probability, which exact methods cannot supply for dynamic traffic. One empirical approach relaxes the no-reconfiguration constraint through resource-prioritized defragmentation: all active requests are sorted in descending order of required resources (spectral slots multiplied by hops) and re-allocated sequentially using a strong heuristic. This yields an omniscient baseline that approximates a physical lower bound on blocking, since the network is effectively repacked near-optimally at each decision rather than constrained by past placements. Comparing the best heuristics with these empirical bounds indicates that, at a fixed service blocking probability of 0.1%, the offered traffic load in flex-grid networks could be increased by 19% to 36%. Thus, although current RL solutions fail to beat well-tuned basic heuristics, a substantial optimality gap exists and is a legitimate target for future work, either by closing the 19%-36% capacity gap or by addressing multi-objective optimizations in which simple heuristics are less effective.

### Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dynamic Resource Allocation (DeepRMSA and Successors)
<!-- sources: deep_reinforcement_learning_for_resource_allocation_in_multi-band_optical_networks, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope | gen: b9d2231fbeb5 -->
Optical RL-Gym is an open-source toolkit that provides reinforcement-learning environments for optical-network problems such as routing and spectrum assignment, in which an agent interacts with a simulated network and receives rewards for its allocation decisions. For multi-band (MB) networks, it has been extended in two ways. First, the GNPy library is integrated for physical-layer impairment modeling: GNPy implements the Generalized Gaussian Noise (GGN) model and accounts for wide-band effects such as Stimulated Raman Scattering, giving the environment an accurate per-channel GSNR instead of a simplified reach or distance rule. Second, a custom reward function is added, giving 1 + 1/(α×β) for a successful establishment (α = allocated channels, β = hops) and -1 for a blocked request, so that shorter paths and fewer spectral resources are rewarded. The agent trained in this environment uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as its policy optimizer. Training is run offline on a digital twin of the network under various traffic loads, which lets the agent adapt to dynamic requests before deployment in a real network. The accuracy of the physical-layer model matters because earlier RL attempts in MB networks, lacking it, failed to outperform heuristics such as k-Shortest Path First-Fit; with the GNPy-based environment, the trained agent reduced blocking probability by up to 80% versus k-SP FF on a 14-node, 44-link topology. The simulation landscape for RL-based resource allocation is otherwise fragmented across incompatible toolkits, which makes fair comparison between published results difficult. Following DeepRMSA, many studies share its problem definitions and codebase, but differences in environment implementation can silently change the effective load. Benchmarking studies therefore recommend open-source code and established simulation frameworks. One such framework, XLRON, was used to run experiments across four topologies (NSFNET, COST239, USNET, JPN48) to tune the number of candidate paths and compare heuristics such as KSP-FF, FF-KSP, KSP-BF, BF-KSP, KME-FF and KCA-FF.

### Multi-Band (MB) Transmission and Stimulated Raman Scattering (SRS)
<!-- sources: deep_reinforcement_learning_for_resource_allocation_in_multi-band_optical_networks, forthcoming_optical_x-haul_infrastructure_supporting_6g_mobile_network_requirements, next-generation_optical_networks_to_sustain_connectivity_of_the_future_all_roads_lead_to_optical-computing-enabled_network, overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 1d31abee4b38 -->
Multi-band (MB) transmission expands the capacity of optical networks without deploying new fiber by using bands beyond C, such as L, S, E, and in the longer term O and U, to answer continued global traffic growth; exploiting this additional spectrum maximizes the existing fiber infrastructure and reduces CapEx. Experimental results show aggregate capacities above 100 Tbps by exploiting the O, E, S and U bands together with C and L, using parametric optical band conversion. It comes at the cost of heterogeneity: performance differs across frequency bands, band-specific components (amplifiers, lasers) are required, and the much larger number of channels increases the complexity of resource allocation; developing amplifiers for the O, E, S and U bands is therefore a stated priority. Wide-band operation also brings nonlinear physical-layer impairments (PLIs), notably Stimulated Raman Scattering (SRS), which transfers power from higher-frequency to lower-frequency channels across the transmitted spectrum and so makes per-channel quality depend on the occupancy of the whole band. MB systems therefore cannot be treated as linear media with quasi-uniform channel behavior, as was assumed for C-band, and SRS-induced interband power transfer calls for precise optical power management. Classical heuristics such as k-Shortest Path (k-SP) and First-Fit (FF) do not account for these constraints well. Accurate performance estimation in MB networks relies on models that capture wide-band effects, such as the Generalized Gaussian Noise (GGN) model implemented in GNPy, which yields a generalized SNR (GSNR) per channel; the Optical Signal-to-Noise and Interference Ratio (OSNIR) likewise accounts for amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) noise and both intra- and inter-band nonlinear effects. On the control-plane side, the modeling shift is from 'implicit band' assumptions to 'per-band' parameter grouping, using profiles that group invariant data to keep the model scalable. In a DRL-based routing and spectrum assignment study on a 14-node, 44-link topology, the agent was never given a band hierarchy but learned to prioritize bands in the order C > L > S > E, with usage of 43%, 27%, 16% and 14% respectively. This ordering emerged from the agent's optimization of path length and GSNR during training, since the bands differ in achievable GSNR and thus in how many channels and which modulation format a request needs. For ultra-wideband (UWB) backhaul spanning about 21 THz, transmission quality across a national topology (about 1000 km, 7 hops) has been evaluated with OSNIR, accounting for ASE noise, nonlinear interference (NLI) and SRS. To counter the SRS-induced power tilt, pre-compensating filters are applied at node ingress. Node switch state also matters: the bar state of the Mach-Zehnder switches gives about 0.8 dB higher OSNIR than the cross state. PM-QPSK proves reliable across the entire national scale, whereas higher-order formats such as PM-16QAM are viable only between adjacent nodes or in specific bands (C and L) under a 2z-OSNIR power allocation strategy. MB and space-division multiplexing (SDM) are also framed as remedies for the capacity crunch of C-band fiber, yielding order-of-magnitude capacity increases but often requiring expensive new hardware (amplifiers, transceivers, fibers). This cost motivates architectural alternatives such as optical computing in the transport layer, which seek to improve spectral efficiency on the existing infrastructure rather than add spectrum or spatial resources.

### Multi-Granular Optical Nodes (MG-ON) and Waveband Selective Switches (WBSS)
<!-- sources: forthcoming_optical_x-haul_infrastructure_supporting_6g_mobile_network_requirements, optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited | gen: 2e528e03df5a -->
The multi-granular optical node (MG-ON) is a backhaul node designed to scale with ultra-wideband (UWB, about 21 THz) and space division multiplexing (SDM), moving beyond traditional wavelength selective switches (WSS) by switching at band and fiber granularity. It uses a three-layer hierarchy. Layer 1 (flex-band route and select) operates at fiber/band level with flex-WBSS modules; it enables Full Fiber Switching (FFS) by bypassing delay stages and supports Spatial Lane Changes (SLC) that relocate bands between spatial rails during link failures. Layer 2 (flex-band add/drop) uses an inter-band optical cross connect (OXC) to provide colorless, directionless and contentionless (C/D/C) access to wideband transceivers based on integrated comb sources. Layer 3 (legacy wavelength access for routing and add/drop) keeps compatibility with legacy C-band equipment through conventional WSS and intra-band OXCs, which can be decommissioned as the network evolves toward full band switching. The photonic-integrated-circuit (PIC) waveband selective switch (WBSS) combines an adaptive filtering stage of cascaded FIR lattice filters with a non-blocking spatial crossbar switch (CS), allowing the UWB spectrum to be carved into up to four flexible bands. An 'inverted logic' sets most Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI) switches to the bar state rather than the cross state, since the bar state has better crosstalk across wide spectral ranges. Insertion loss is modeled as the sum of FIR filter losses (0.1 dB per tap) and crossbar junction losses (0.05 dB per junction), and remains manageable for UWB operation despite waveguide crossings added to reduce crosstalk. Capacity is modeled from node degree D, spatial lanes S and spectral efficiency of 10 b/s/Hz, with formulas for the number of WBSS modules, inter- and intra-band OXC port counts and band transceivers. Assuming two bands stay in Layer 1 and two are passed to Layers 2 or 3, and FFS is enabled when FIR filters run in pass-through mode, throughput reaches about 3.76 Pb/s for D=4, about 9.4 Pb/s for D=5 and S=8 via FFS, and more than 10 Pb/s for D=6 and S>=8; Layer 1 contributes the largest capacity gain, supporting the move from waveband to full fiber switching. Cascadability was assessed with OSNIR including ASE, NLI and SRS, using ingress pre-compensating filters against SRS tilt. Over a national topology of about 1000 km and 7 hops, the bar state gives about 0.8 dB higher OSNIR than the cross state, PM-QPSK is reliable over the whole scale, and PM-16QAM is limited to adjacent nodes or specific bands (C and L) under 2z-OSNIR power allocation. Related work in the Octopus project explores multi-Tbit/s photonic switching controlled by SDN controllers to support time-sensitive networking, and optical bypass of the OLT has been proposed to save power and lower latency in converged access.

### Optical Digital-to-Analog Converter (oDAC)-Based Transceivers
<!-- sources: forthcoming_optical_x-haul_infrastructure_supporting_6g_mobile_network_requirements | gen: 8fa348faaafa -->
Optical digital-to-analog converters (oDACs) are proposed to replace power-intensive electronic DACs (eDACs) and part of the associated DSP in x-haul transceivers. An oDAC uses parallel Mach-Zehnder modulators (MZMs) to synthesize high-order multi-level optical signals directly in the optical domain from low-resolution drivers, so that the resolution requirement shifts from electronics to photonics. A first advantage is noise squelching: the non-linear transfer function of the MZM suppresses electronic driver noise, giving a 6.6 dB improvement in error vector magnitude (EVM) over conventional modulators. A second is scalability: a hybrid serial-parallel oDAC architecture can generate up to 256QAM constellations using only eight NRZ drivers, reaching 3.2 Tbps per wavelength, and transceivers based on it exceed 1 Tb/s per channel. A third is efficiency, with power consumption per bit reduced by 30%-40% compared with traditional eDAC-based solutions. These transceivers complete the proposed x-haul infrastructure alongside the PIC-based fronthaul filter and the multi-granular backhaul node, addressing the energy cost of the very high symbol rates and modulation orders needed for 6G.

### Traffic-Driven Service Provisioning and Predictive Provisioning
<!-- sources: survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: c46f00a26290 -->
Traffic-driven service provisioning adjusts optical network configuration to real-time demand and current network state, instead of the static worst-case design that causes over-provisioning and high operating cost. The motivation is the spatio-temporal fluctuation of Internet traffic (tidal traffic), driven by 5G/6G services such as IoT and autonomous vehicles. Machine learning (ML) is seen as the enabler for automating these data-heavy reconfiguration processes at the optical layer, which is increasingly programmable through software-defined networking (SDN), flexible grids and variable bit-rate coherent optics. A taxonomy maps strategies across static, reactive, proactive, dynamic and adaptive networks. Reactive networks have static infrastructure and use descriptive analytics, reprovisioning only when a threshold such as link utilization is violated, leaving high over-provisioning and low automation. Proactive networks have more dynamic infrastructure with external software control and use predictive or prescriptive analytics to reserve resources a priori for short-term future needs (hours to weeks). Adaptive networks have highly programmable infrastructure with embedded software control and use real-time monitoring and ML to provision on the fly, accounting for current demand and expected future behavior to limit congestion and fragmentation. ML-aided approaches split into two frameworks. Predictive provisioning uses supervised learning (SL) to forecast traffic, and the forecast feeds conventional resource allocation algorithms. Prescriptive provisioning uses reinforcement learning (RL) to learn allocation policies by trial and error without labeled data, giving direct actionable advice and avoiding the error accumulation of two-step predict-then-optimize pipelines, since it optimizes QoS targets and congestion directly. Unsupervised learning helps by clustering traffic profiles or detecting anomalies but does not predict future trends. ML-aided provisioning outperforms rule-based approaches in all network types by better capturing time-varying behavior. Open directions include field trials at scale, Tiny ML for edge deployment, defenses against adversarial ML attacks on traffic data, and combining bio-inspired optimization, federated learning (FL) and explainable AI.

Predictive provisioning is a two-step pipeline: a traffic forecast is produced first and then used as input to a conventional resource allocation algorithm, with off-line optimization in proactive networks and on-line optimization in adaptive networks. Short-term traffic prediction is treated as time-series forecasting. Non-linear ML models are preferred over linear statistical models such as ARIMA, which struggle with the burstiness and self-similarity of traffic. Supervised learning maps past values to future values, and regression is generally favored over classification because continuous outputs suit flexible infrastructure such as elastic optical networks (EONs). Performance is measured with mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE). Traffic can be monitored at base-station level, link level, or as a traffic demand (TD) matrix covering all source-destination pairs. Preprocessing includes time-slot discretization, sliding windows to limit computational complexity, and scaling to improve training convergence. Datasets are synthetic (simulators, Poisson or log-normal distributions) or real traces such as Abilene and GEANT. Feedforward NNs and DNNs map features effectively but often serve as benchmarks. Among recurrent networks, LSTMs solve the vanishing-gradient problem of plain RNNs, and GRUs have fewer parameters and lower cost. Encoder-decoders enable multi-step-ahead prediction, which reduces service disruption during reconfiguration. CNNs capture spatial dependencies in TD matrices, and graph neural networks treat the network as vertices and edges to capture spatio-temporal relationships. Bayesian learning, notably Gaussian process regression (GPR), gives probabilistic outputs and uncertainty measures and is robust to outliers and sparse data. Transformers (parallel sequence processing, faster training) and Bayesian deep learning (deep-learning scale with principled uncertainty estimation, to mitigate over- and under-provisioning) are named as models to exploit. For bit-rate prediction, aggregated traffic is easier to predict than base-station-level traffic, DNNs generally beat simple ANNs, and LSTMs work for short-term forecasts but lose accuracy beyond thresholds such as 60 s; quantile regression and GPR ensembles handle uncertainty better and reduce under-provisioning. For connection arrival and holding-time prediction, LSTMs support on-line adaptive provisioning, though unexpected traffic events degrade accuracy and training on diverse profiles improves robustness. For link load prediction, graph learning (GCN-GAN) and hybrid diffusion convolutional recurrent networks (DCRNN) outperform LSTMs by capturing topological spatial dependence together with temporal trends. In the 6G optical fronthaul, prediction is used to adjust the functional splitting option dynamically and feeds AI-based dynamic bandwidth allocation (DBA) and sleep modes, in which ONUs enter low-power states during low traffic to save energy.

In proactive networks, hourly-scale TD matrix predictions drive multi-period re-optimization through the sequence prediction, reconfiguration decision, resource allocation algorithm and physical reconfiguration. Objectives include minimizing energy consumption, connection blocking and reconfiguration disruptions, and solutions come from integer linear programming (ILP) or heuristics. Shutting down idle resources in low-traffic hours gives significant energy savings at the cost of higher blocking probability. Constrained reconfiguration, which limits the number of changed lightpaths, reduces service disruption and OPEX but may increase unserved traffic. Coordinated optimization across virtual and physical layers is more effective than optimizing a single layer. Quantile margins from the ML model instead of fixed empirical margins cut spectrum waste while avoiding under-provisioning, and α-fairness algorithms prevent greedy allocation from starving some services in congested networks. In adaptive networks, short-term predictions (seconds to minutes) of arrival and departure times and spectrum utilization are used on-line to update link weights, steering routing toward resources that raise the likelihood of accepting future requests and lowering blocking. This consistently reduces blocking relative to conventional dynamic provisioning when predictions are accurate, but with low accuracy it can perform worse than non-predictive schemes. Chaining prediction and optimization also introduces intermediate forecast errors, which motivates prescriptive RL approaches. Open issues are flexible representation of uncertainty, multi-step prediction (most work is single-step), prediction disaggregated per service SLA (needed for VNF service chain provisioning), an automated ML lifecycle with monitoring and adaptation for non-stationary traffic, edge training with FL for privacy and lower communication overhead, explainable AI with human-in-the-loop (HITL) approaches to build operator trust, and adapting optimizations to emerging technologies such as spatio-spectral multiplexing and to complex problems such as virtual network function service chain (VNF-SC) provisioning.

## New Optical Transport Technologies (SDM, HCF, Optical Computing)

### Space Division Multiplexing (SDM)
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, forthcoming_optical_x-haul_infrastructure_supporting_6g_mobile_network_requirements, overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 15ef1994e536 -->
Space division multiplexing (SDM), which uses multicore fibers (MCF), fiber bundles or few-mode fibers (FMF) to carry parallel spatial channels, is proposed as a scalable alternative to traditional single-core, single-channel fibers for raising fronthaul capacity toward the Tbps scale demanded by 6G, avoiding the 'capacity crunch' of single-mode fiber. MCF multiplies capacity by the number of cores, with inter-core crosstalk as the main challenge; FMF uses spatial modes as separate channels, which requires optical MIMO and complex DSP. SDM complements wavelength-domain scaling (WDM/TWDM-PON and coherent transmission) by adding a spatial dimension when spectral resources become scarce, and in transport networks it is regarded as the long-term capacity step beyond multiband (MB) transmission, giving the combined Multiband over SDM (MBoSDM) scenario. In a synergistic deployment it is suited to the core backbone, while other technologies such as hollow-core fiber serve critical low-latency links, since the cost of new fibers is a significant barrier. In the backhaul, SDM is combined with ultra-wideband (UWB) transmission in a multi-granular optical node, where throughput scales with node degree D and the number of spatial lanes S, assuming spectral efficiency of 10 b/s/Hz over about 21 THz and linear growth of WBSS ports with spatial lanes. Reported results are about 3.76 Pb/s for a small node (D=4), about 9.4 Pb/s for a medium node (D=5, S=8) through full fiber switching, and more than 10 Pb/s for large nodes (D=6, S>=8). Resilience is added by Spatial Lane Changes (SLC), which relocate bands between spatial rails during link failures. A standardization gap remains in the SDM data plane: without standard ITU-T layering for core characterization and switching, full control-plane interoperability is elusive, and experimental extensions such as a PHOTONIC_MEDIA/SDM_CORE layer qualifier in TAPI have been used instead. Capacity enhancement is paired with software-defined networking (SDN), which enables dynamic reconfiguration of the fronthaul and bandwidth allocation in response to traffic. AI-driven traffic forecasting of spatiotemporal demand feeds proactive dynamic bandwidth allocation (DBA) on top of this programmable infrastructure. Related uses of SDM in the optical wireless literature include power allocation in SDM-FSO systems with Q-learning, and multi-core-fiber elastic optical networks where LSTMs predict traffic.

### Hollow-Core Fiber (HCF) for Latency and Capacity
<!-- sources: optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 2931118948e0 -->
Hollow-core fiber (HCF) guides light in air or vacuum rather than glass, which reduces propagation delay and also gives lower optical nonlinearity than silica fiber. Standard single-mode fiber has a latency of about 5 μs/km, whereas new hollow-core fibers can bring this down to about 3.5 μs/km, a reduction of roughly 30% (about 1.54 μs saved per kilometer). Transmission losses have dropped to about 0.11 dB/km. This is relevant to 6G x-haul and fixed-mobile convergence, where hyper-reliable low-latency communication (HRLLC) targets of 0.1-1 ms (10-100 μs end to end in the optical transport budget) and tight fronthaul budgets limit the reachable distance between RUs and DUs/CUs, so lower per-kilometer delay can extend the feasible distance or relax placement of processing units. Manufacturing hurdles remain, however: high manufacturing cost and difficult fusion splicing hinder mass deployment and must be overcome before large-scale use in access and transport networks. Because no single technology satisfies all 6G needs, HCF is positioned for critical low-latency links within a mix that includes SDM for the core backbone and coherent PON for access, and reducing HCF manufacturing cost is listed among the strategic recommendations.

### Optical Bypass vs. Optical-Computing-Enabled Networking
<!-- sources: next-generation_optical_networks_to_sustain_connectivity_of_the_future_all_roads_lead_to_optical-computing-enabled_network | gen: 660c2d240bf3 -->
An optical-computing-enabled network departs from the optical-bypass principle that has governed optical networks for about two decades. Optical-bypass avoids costly optical-electrical-optical (OEO) conversions by keeping lightpaths transparent, but it rests on strictly avoiding signal interference in the time, frequency or spatial domains; in such a network, demands sharing links need separate wavelengths because overlapping signals must not interfere. The optical-computing-enabled paradigm instead uses controlled interference between transitional lightpaths for computing purposes rather than treating it as destructive, and can consolidate such demands into a single wavelength. The motivation is the capacity crunch of C-band fiber infrastructure under traffic growth driven by IoT, Big Data and AI: multi-band (MB) and space-division multiplexing (SDM) raise capacity by orders of magnitude but typically require expensive new hardware, whereas computing in the optical layer aims to improve spectral efficiency on existing infrastructure. The transport layer thereby evolves from a static role of moving data into an integrated communication-computing infrastructure in which transmission and processing occur simultaneously in the optical domain, with interest also fueled by the end of predictable transistor scaling (Moore's Law) and the speed and energy efficiency of silicon photonics and light-based computing for AI acceleration. This is also meant to support large-scale AI training directly within the optical layer. Realization is described as requiring multidisciplinary advances in devices, systems and network management algorithms.

Two representative computing primitives are proposed for integrating computation into the transport layer: optical aggregation/de-aggregation, based on nonlinear effects, and optical XOR encoding/decoding with photonic logic gates, the latter used for dedicated protection. Channel aggregation is traditionally performed electronically, which does not scale to very high bit rates. Optical aggregation instead exploits nonlinear effects, such as four-wave mixing (FWM) and cross-phase modulation (XPM), in media such as semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs) to combine several lower-speed channels into a single higher-speed channel with a higher-order modulation format; for example, two QPSK signals can be aggregated into one 16-QAM signal, and de-aggregation performs the inverse operation, yielding a gain in spectral efficiency. The added degree of freedom, the interaction of lightpaths, turns the classical Routing and Wavelength Assignment (RWA) problem into one that also includes computing assignment: designers must decide which pairs of lightpaths to compute, where the computing nodes are, and how to route the resulting computed lightpaths. This framework is termed 'optical network design and planning 2.0'.

### Optical XOR Network Coding and Planning
<!-- sources: next-generation_optical_networks_to_sustain_connectivity_of_the_future_all_roads_lead_to_optical-computing-enabled_network | gen: c644b74e12c8 -->
Photonic logic gates can perform bit-wise exclusive-or (XOR) operations on high-bit-rate optical signals, enabling network coding in the optical domain, which is particularly useful for dedicated protection. In an optical-bypass network, the backup paths of different demands often overlap on shared links and need distinct wavelengths to avoid interference. In a computing-enabled network, the backup signals of two demands that share a destination can be XOR-encoded at an intermediate node into a single computed lightpath, reducing wavelength consumption on the shared links by up to 50% while preserving near-immediate recovery, since decoding after a single link failure is itself an XOR operation. Resilience requires that the working routes of the two encoded demands be disjoint from each other and from the corresponding backup routes, so that any single link failure leaves enough information to recover both demands. All-optical coding is more restricted by wavelength constraints than electronic OEO coding, for which gains of up to 20% are reported.

Planning a network with optical XOR encoding can be formulated as an Integer Linear Programming (ILP) model that minimizes the total number of wavelengths used across all links (wavelength link cost). The model assumes that encoding is applied to the backup signals of demands sharing a common destination, using the same wavelength, and that working and backup paths are link-disjoint. Constraints include flow conservation (continuous working and backup paths from source to destination), wavelength uniqueness on each link, coding logic (each demand limited to one encoding operation, with a common destination for encoded pairs), and resilience (working routes of encoded demands disjoint from each other and from the corresponding backup routes, so recovery is possible after any single link failure). The problem is NP-hard and more computationally intensive than traditional RWA because of the additional variables tracking encoding nodes and computed paths, so a scalable heuristic is also proposed for larger networks. Evaluation against traditional optical-bypass designs without network coding (w-NC) on NSFNET and COST239 used wavelength link cost as the metric. In small-scale tests, the ILP optimum showed significant gains from network coding (NC), and the heuristic performed close to the ILP. On the realistic topologies, NC improved capacity efficiency by up to 8% in NSFNET and 5% in COST239, lower than the gains of up to 20% reported for electronic OEO coding because of the stricter wavelength constraints of all-optical coding.

### Plasmonic Fiber Sensors and Photonic Device Design
<!-- sources: leveraging_federated_learning_for_optimizing_fiber_optic_sensor_design_for_biodetection | gen: 5bdcfdc67aa3 -->
High-performance optical biosensors, needed for clinical diagnostics, drug discovery and environmental monitoring, are judged by sensitivity, accuracy, specificity, response time and reusability. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) in fiber optic sensors (FOS) is attractive for its flexibility and remote-sensing capability, and two-dimensional materials such as molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) are added to the plasmonic structure to improve sensitivity through optimization of radiation damping. The central metric is the Figure of Merit (FOM), the product of sensitivity and accuracy, whose maximization depends on the precise choice of incident light wavelength and metal layer thickness. Traditional design relies on systematic simulation-based exploration of these parameters, which is computationally expensive at small step sizes and, when data are aggregated, raises privacy risks. Machine learning (ML) has therefore been used as a surrogate for predicting optimal design parameters, with examples including ML for tilted fiber Bragg grating (TFBG)-assisted sensors, photonic crystal fiber (PCF) designs optimized by genetic algorithms or the Taguchi approach, 'SmartSPR' adaptive sensors and LSPR sensors for SARS-CoV-2 detection. The general workflow combines parameter optimization (varying wavelength and materials), simulation of sensor behavior across environmental factors and analyte concentrations, and supervised learning on the simulation data to build predictive performance models. Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), a supervised, non-parametric model, serves as such a surrogate, predicting FOM from wavelength and metal layer thickness in place of repeated fine-step simulations; its predictions have been shown to match actual FOM trends on unseen data. In one GPR-based optimization of a MoS2-assisted SPR fiber sensor, FOM improved by 11%, peaking at 6526.23 RIU^-1 at 1099.343 nm, and a fine wavelength step of 0.001 nm proved crucial for enhancing FOM through manipulation of the optical path difference. Validation with larger datasets improved the mean absolute error (MAE) to 65.69 RIU^-1 with an R2 near 0.9 for a centralized model.

Centralized GPR requires pooling all data, which conflicts with privacy constraints on sensitive medical data. This motivates federated learning (FL), in which distributed sensor nodes refine a shared model by exchanging trained parameters instead of raw data, with GPR as the local learner; this suits medical biosensing, where regulations restrict data sharing. A five-step workflow has been described. (1) Data partitioning: data from different environments or sources remain in decentralized datasets (e.g., FOS dataset 1 and 2) for regulatory compliance. (2) Local model training: each node trains a local GPR and evaluates it with R2 and root mean squared error (RMSE), tuning hyperparameters where accuracy is low so that a weak local model does not degrade the eventual global model. (3) Collaborative aggregation: local parameters are combined into a refined global model by weighted averaging, secure multiparty computation or differential privacy; to handle data heterogeneity (different data distributions between nodes), personalized FL (PFL) can tailor models to client-specific attributes. (4) Parameter updates: parameters are updated according to the reliability or quality of each local contribution. (5) Global model refinement: the global GPR is updated to reflect collective knowledge while staying adaptable to heterogeneous data. Because only parameters are shared, the approach resolves the trade-off between the large, diverse datasets needed for generalization and strict privacy requirements, and can yield generalized sensor designs across geographical locations. Open challenges include FL algorithms designed specifically for sensor optimization (better secure aggregation and differential privacy), robust handling of varying data quality and distribution to ensure convergence, validation in real-world biosensing deployments, and reduced communication overhead. Future directions include integrating FL with evolutionary algorithms and reinforcement learning.

## SDN, Disaggregation, and the Optical Control Plane

### GMPLS and Path Computation Element (PCE)
<!-- sources: optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges | gen: 5382c8ec5da2 -->
In distributed optical network control, each node hosts a control agent and the control plane runs on a separate network, so that data-plane failures do not disable the ability to recover the network. Generalized Multiprotocol Label Switching (GMPLS) automates provisioning of Generalized Label Switched Paths (G-LSPs) in wavelength switched optical networks (WSON) and rests on three protocols: OSPF-TE advertises topology and traffic engineering information, RSVP-TE reserves resources through forward Path messages and backward Resv messages, and the Link Management Protocol (LMP) monitors point-to-point links. A characteristic problem of this distributed model is backward blocking, in which a connection fails during the Resv phase because of resource contention, requiring multiple signaling attempts or protocol extensions. Fully distributed control also yields sub-optimal routing, and QoT-aware setup through GMPLS extensions becomes computationally heavy. The Path Computation Element (PCE) addresses these limits by centralizing path computation; it communicates with node agents through the PCEP protocol, improves resource utilization and can account for physical impairments more effectively, although communication overhead and queuing may add activation delay during restoration. Scalability limits prevent full resource advertisement across domains, so multidomain networks use several strategies: per-domain computation, where path segments are computed sequentially as signaling traverses each domain; inter-PCE computation, where end-to-end paths are computed before signaling, often with backward recursive PCE-based computation (BRPC); and hierarchical PCE (H-PCE), where a parent PCE (pPCE) coordinates the domain sequence and end-to-end path while child PCEs (cPCE) compute intra-domain segments.

### Software-Defined Networking (SDN) Applied to Optical Networks
<!-- sources: optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial, shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: bccd7d7fd511 -->
Software Defined Networking (SDN) decouples the data plane (forwarding hardware) from the control plane (software logic) managed by a centralized SDN controller, which maintains topology and configuration and allows wavelengths and topology to be configured programmatically through APIs. In optical fronthaul, this centralized control allows bandwidth to be reconfigured dynamically, traffic to be groomed and resilience to be improved. Compared with GMPLS, SDN's centralized path computation and parallelized signaling significantly reduce both provisioning and restoration times. Network Function Virtualization (NFV) complements SDN by virtualizing network functions (VNFs/CNFs) on commercial off-the-shelf hardware, enabling flexible deployment across the Cloud-Edge continuum. Management and orchestration (MANO) systems integrate the two, orchestrating end-to-end services by simultaneously requesting optical connectivity through SDN and compute resources through NFV, which supports 6G services that need joint communication and computation. OpenFlow was an early southbound interface, but its packet-centric nature limits its use in optical layers; NETCONF with YANG data models has become the de facto standard because of its model-based approach. Modern optical control relies on Model Driven Development (MDD), in which standardized YANG data models abstract optical node capabilities and are exchanged over transport protocols such as NETCONF, RESTCONF and gNMI. The architectural trend is moving from monolithic controllers toward modular, service-based architectures that apply 'separation of concerns': configuration and control are separated from monitoring and telemetry, and functions such as path computation are externalized. One current trend is delegating Routing and Spectrum Assignment (RSA) to digital twins, which improve Quality of Transmission (QoT) assessment by evaluating physical impairments before a lightpath is activated. Disaggregation separates hardware (white boxes) from software through open interfaces such as YANG. Two forms exist: partial disaggregation, in which only the transponders are vendor-neutral while the rest of the line system remains proprietary, and full disaggregation, which also turns ROADMs into white boxes (separating transponders, ROADMs and amplifiers). Open ROADM targets interoperability and disaggregated architectures to prevent vendor lock-in, while OpenConfig provides vendor-neutral YANG models and supports gNMI for streaming telemetry; together they aim to let components from different vendors be controlled uniformly by the SDN controller. A gap nevertheless remains: isolated data models and proprietary SDN controllers are still required to reach advanced device functionalities. For multiband networks, which introduce heterogeneous devices and per-band impairments, these models must be extended so that device capabilities and penalties are described per band rather than assumed uniform. Three open-source controller frameworks are prominent. OpenDaylight (with its TransportPCE application) is known for stability and OpenROADM compatibility. ONOS is designed for scalability and reliability and supports an intent-based approach to controlling packet and optical layers. TeraFlowSDN is a cloud-native, microservices-oriented controller that is extending into optical device management via OpenConfig. In multi-layer environments a hierarchical architecture is used, in which a parent controller coordinates specialized child controllers for the IP and optical domains.

### Multi-Layer Packet-Optical Control and Multi-Domain Orchestration
<!-- sources: optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial | gen: f1ae73d78edd -->
SDN allows joint configuration of packet and optical layers. An orchestrator such as Open Source MANO coordinates computational resources (managed with OpenStack) and networking resources (controlled with ONOS). In an illustrated video streaming service, a failure triggers the simultaneous activation of a new optical lightpath and the reconfiguration of IP/MAC flow rules at the packet switch, redirecting users to a backup server. For multilevel multiband-over-SDM networks, in which switching can occur at WDM, waveband or fiber/core granularity, control is organized hierarchically: single-switching nodes are grouped into technology domains (for example one region switching at WDM level and another at core level) managed by hierarchical controllers and orchestrators, while hybrid nodes that switch at both levels require Virtual Network Topologies (VNTs) spanning protocol layers. In multi-domain orchestration for future B5G telco clouds, services span Core, Metro and Edge data centers (DCs), and the central challenge is balancing two opposing latency components: network latency, which is higher for distant Core DCs, and processing latency, which is higher for resource-constrained Edge DCs. Orchestration must therefore trade resource utilization against the risk of SLA violations. To avoid the inefficiency of segregated Traffic Engineering Databases (TEDs) per layer or domain, an Open Network Database (ONDB) has been proposed: an independent, shared, distributed database that stores protocol-independent topology and TE information from different layers and domains. Experimental results show that ONDB can perform inter-layer correlation, for example for link maintenance, in a few milliseconds across 100 nodes.

### Multiband-over-SDM (MBoSDM) Control and Multigranular Nodes
<!-- sources: overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial | gen: 97a213578165 -->
In multiband over SDM (MBoSDM) networks, the design of the optical nodes determines how the control plane must treat the spatial dimension. In single-level (flat) SDM networks, switching occurs only at the DWDM level and the spatial layer is terminated at every node. This is a straightforward SDN upgrade, but it causes a 'port explosion' in WSS-based ROADMs (for example, 8 fibers with a node degree of 9 may require about 70 WSS ports) and significantly increases path computation complexity and latency. Multilevel networks instead allow switching at different granularities (WDM, waveband, or fiber/core). They can be built from single-switching nodes organized into technology domains managed by hierarchical controllers and orchestrators, or from hybrid (multigranular) nodes that switch at both the DWDM and SDM levels. Hybrid nodes require control-plane extensions for Virtual Network Topologies (VNTs) across protocol layers, for spatial characterization through core or mode identifiers, and for core-continuity constraints, which require a spatial path to remain within a single core, analogous to spectrum continuity in WDM. Optical bypass at lower layers can be more cost-efficient for specific traffic patterns, even though switching at the highest WDM layer yields lower blocking rates. A proof of concept implemented four multigranular nodes interconnected by multicore fibers and extended the Transport API (TAPI) with an experimental protocol layer qualifier, `PHOTONIC_MEDIA/SDM_CORE`. This lets the controller allocate specific cores for optical bypass, reducing WDM switching at intermediate nodes. SDN agents support submatrix operations to set up and release 'add core', 'express core' and 'drop core' connections and to configure Wavelength Cross-Connect (WXC) ports. In the TAPI model, SDM_CORE NEPs report core availability and SDM_CORE CEPs report the cores assigned. Path computation follows two steps: it first attempts to use existing virtual flexigrid links (VNT) and, failing that, creates a new core service between source and destination that yields a new virtual DWDM link. The lack of standard ITU-T layering for SDM core characterization and switching remains a gap for interoperability, and the work stresses balancing detailed device modeling, which enables optimal routing, against generic abstractions that preserve scalability.

### Flexigrid Control Terminology
<!-- sources: overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial | gen: ebe5b6b7332c -->
Control and management of flexigrid optical networks rely on a reference vocabulary that separates the signal, the spectrum it occupies and the network sections it traverses. An Optical Tributary Signal (OTSi) is a unidirectional signal carried within a network media channel. An OTSi Group (OTSiG) is a set of OTSis that together support one digital client; its members require co-routing and a controlled differential delay. A Media Channel (MC) represents a frequency slot or range on a specific path, while a Network Media Channel (NMC) is its end-to-end equivalent between transceiver ports. A Media Channel Group (MCG) is a management abstraction for co-routed media channels and covers two kinds of sections: Optical Transmission Sections (OTS), which lie between amplifiers, and Optical Multiplex Sections (OMS), which lie between filter or coupler ports. In control-plane data models such as the Transport API (TAPI), the OTS and OMS layers are represented with Network Edge Points and Connection End Points that capture fiber characteristics like loss, PMD and length, usually referencing static fiber profiles. The same terminology underpins the description of multiband and SDM extensions, where media channels additionally have to be qualified by band and, for spatial switching, by core.

### IP-over-WDM (IPoWDM) Packet-Optical Integration
<!-- sources: shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited | gen: a1db4aed9ba6 -->
The availability of coherent pluggable transceivers at 800G and above has accelerated the adoption of IP over WDM (IPoWDM), in which coherent optics are plugged directly into routers. Router-to-transceiver communication is facilitated by the C-CMIS standard. Removing standalone transponders reduces CAPEX and power consumption, but it complicates management because the packet and optical layers now share equipment and responsibilities converge on the router. The MANTRA (Metaverse-Ready Architectures for Open Transport) initiative proposes two hierarchical SDN architectures that divide control between an IP controller and an optical controller. In the single architecture, the IP controller fully manages the IPoWDM nodes and their pluggables, while the optical controller manages only the Optical Line System (OLS). The dual architecture is similar, but the optical controller is additionally granted read access to the IPoWDM nodes, which improves its decision-making, for example for QoT assessment. Both rely on a parent controller coordinating specialized child controllers for the IP and optical domains, consistent with the hierarchical multi-layer designs used with open-source controllers such as ONOS and TeraFlowSDN.

### P4 and Programmable Data Planes
<!-- sources: optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited | gen: 18ec1655b6b5 -->
The P4 language enables a programmable data plane in which forwarding decisions are taken at wire speed and with state, without waiting for the SDN controller. Two key advantages are described. In-band telemetry (INT) embeds telemetry metadata directly into user-traffic headers through custom headers that carry measurement information, allowing real-time observation of the network; it requires programmable data planes implemented with P4, eBPF or DPDK. Because the metadata travels with the packets, INT allows near-instant detection of soft failures within the packet-optical node pipeline. INT also facilitates multi-layer optimization, since data from diverse layers can be encoded in a single packet header, which is particularly useful in edge-computing environments. Stateful objects such as counters and meters enable context-aware traffic engineering directly at the node level. P4 thus complements centralized SDN control by moving selected fast-path decisions into the devices, and it is cited among the techniques that push optical and packet networks toward greater autonomy and lower latency.

### Network Telemetry (In-Band and Out-of-Band, gNMI/gRPC)
<!-- sources: dynamic_network-aware_soft_failure_localization_using_machine_learning_in_optical_networks, optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, overview_of_sdn_control_of_multiband_over_sdm_optical_networks_with_physical_layer_impairments_invited_tutorial, shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited | gen: 6d57cf7097da -->
Traditional management protocols such as SNMP, NETCONF and RESTCONF are text-based, verbose and poll-driven; SNMP polling in particular is too slow for modern requirements, and vendor-specific proprietary YANG models hinder interoperability in disaggregated optical networks. An alternative is end-to-end streaming telemetry based on gNMI/gRPC, which runs over HTTP/2 with protocol buffers (binary serialization) and a subscription-based streaming model that delivers real-time transmission parameters from devices to collectors at sub-second intervals. Combined with OpenConfig models, it builds 'data lakes' of time-series samples from amplifiers, ROADMs and pluggables, which serve as training data for AI and enable proactive network management, proactive SDN routines and faster failure localization. Southbound, between data-plane and control-plane components, a telemetry agent (gRPC client) subscribes to PM data from devices (gRPC servers) using XPath definitions; subscriptions avoid repeated polling and produce smaller request and response payloads than NETCONF. Northbound, between the control plane and the management plane, a unified ONF-TAPI v2.5.1 data model gives multi-domain visibility: the control-plane telemetry agent acts as a `tapi-gnmi-streaming` server, and a TAPI gNMI/gRPC client in the management plane collects PM data into a time-series database, avoiding the overhead and latency of RESTCONF's verbose JSON. In a testbed using the TeraFlowSDN controller with two OLS domains in a ring, southbound traffic fell by 94.2% (uplink) and 82.3% (downlink) versus NETCONF, northbound traffic fell by 60.6% to 87.6% versus RESTCONF, and overall end-to-end telemetry traffic dropped by 78.4%. Out-of-band telemetry (ONT) transmits monitoring data such as SNR, BER and power levels over separate channels, so it does not interfere with user traffic. It integrates legacy tools such as Optical Channel Monitors (OCM) and Optical Time Domain Reflectometers (OTDR). Modern implementations use Kafka for distributed publish-subscribe sharing of telemetry, and can be deployed horizontally, as peer-to-peer exchange between device agents, so that decisions are taken locally and the load on the central controller is reduced. Beyond device monitoring, the notion of controller telemetry extends streaming to the control plane itself: controller components act as streaming data sources so that state can be synchronized across hierarchical controllers or Digital Twins (DT). Trends point toward a unified programmable model replacing rigid NETCONF/YANG systems, and toward Generative AI (for example LLM-based bots) that translates high-level semantic operator commands into specific telemetry streams and control operations. For multiband networks, the Linux Foundation Transport API (TAPI) has been extended along several lines. Service Interface Points (SIPs) expose service endpoints, Network Edge Points (NEPs) report resource availability and Connection End Points (CEPs) report active configurations. OTS and OMS layers are modeled with NEPs/CEPs to capture loss, PMD and length, often by reference to static fiber profiles. Transceiver profiles specify, for each operational mode (modulation format and FEC), the applicable frequency ranges. ROADM models must express internal connectivity restrictions (non-CDC architectures) and per-band signal penalties, since WSS technology varies across bands. Because multiband amplifiers are often heterogeneous (for example separate amplifiers per band), the control plane abstracts them into simplified amplification functions while managing gain and tilt through frequency-range-specific lists.

### Optical Packet Switching in Data Centers

The growth of intra-data center traffic and the power and bandwidth limits of electronic switching motivate optical interconnects, whose main challenge is implementing controllers and schedulers that ensure low packet latency and high throughput. ### Autonomous Optical Networks and Margin Reduction
<!-- sources: optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope | gen: 4406751f99e6 -->
Operators traditionally protect transmission with margins, pessimistic buffers added to OSNR estimations to cover worst-case conditions such as all channels being lit, which wastes capacity. Autonomous optical networks reduce this inefficiency with an observe-decide-act loop: real-time monitoring, enabled by digital signal processing in coherent systems, lets the network re-adapt parameters such as modulation format or FEC on the fly. If performance degrades, the system can switch from PM-16QAM to PM-QPSK, which lowers the bit rate but increases robustness, instead of provisioning for the worst case in advance. Because of the large volume of monitoring data and the risk of controller bottlenecks, hierarchical control architectures are proposed, distributing observation and decision functions across levels. Dynamic resource allocation (DRA) is the decision component of this loop: by automating service provisioning and responding in real time to shifting traffic, it reduces the operational margins otherwise required for transmission and traffic uncertainty, and thereby increases overall network throughput without proportional growth in capital expenditure. Learning-based DRA, notably reinforcement learning, is proposed to deliver near-optimal allocations at heuristic speed, although its benefit over well-tuned heuristics has to be established through rigorous benchmarking.

## Intent-Based Networking and Zero-Touch Automation

### Intent-Based Networking (IBN): Architecture and Lifecycle
<!-- sources: intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review, optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited, zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation | gen: 06bf87339541 -->
An intent is a set of operational goals and outcomes expressed declaratively (what is needed) rather than imperatively (how to achieve it), which lets stakeholders manage network functions without exposure to internal resource complexity and supports vertical-specific services such as ultra-low latency for industrial automation. Intents are high-level business objectives, written in domain-specific languages, that the system translates into technical configurations; they are hardware-independent, contextual and persistent, and their benefits include fewer human errors, efficiency through automation, scalability and easier regulatory compliance. A virtual reality example in a 5G network states requirements of 1 Gbps bandwidth and 5 ms latency together with a policy prioritizing that traffic, and the zero-touch system turns them into resource allocations without manual switch configuration. In optical networks this represents a shift from imperative to declarative management: users express high-level intents, in natural language or as business goals (for example, ensure high availability for gold users), and an Intent Layer translates them into actionable instructions for the SDN controller, which then programs the hardware. The system operates a closed-loop workflow of fulfillment (deployment) and assurance (continuous verification and tuning); telemetry provides continuous assurance, so that if performance degrades (for example through fiber degradation) the IBN layer automatically triggers rerouting or parameter adjustments to maintain the service level agreement. In the ZSM framework, intents are the main communication method between humans and the system and between management layers, and closed-loop frameworks such as CLARA monitor network state and adjust configurations to stay aligned with the stated intents. This contrasts with policy-based management, which relies on imperative if-then rules triggered by events, usually with centralized control and detailed implementation knowledge; intent-based management instead specifies a desired state (context, capabilities, constraints) and lets the network, treated as a single entity, choose the implementation path, moving processing toward the device level and improving scalability. Standardization is spread across several bodies: ONF (NorthBound Interface), 3GPP (TR 28.812 on intent-driven management), ETSI (policy-driven orchestration) and IETF/IRTF (autonomic networking). Intents are transient (active for a single operation) or persistent (active until decommissioned), and persistent intents follow a lifecycle across three domains. The user domain handles recognition and formulation: through human-machine interfaces, an intent recognition engine interacts with the user, resolves conflicts and outputs an actionable intent. The processing domain translates human-readable intents into machine-readable configurations via NLP or ontology mapping, resolves conflicts between intents, and runs closed-loop feedback so that configurations adhere to SLAs and adapt to dynamic conditions; translation techniques range from complex NLP and RNNs to controlled NLP with limited vocabularies, NLP models such as BERT have been used to translate human language into executable network commands, NLP is also used for intent classification, and supervised learning is applied to network assurance. The implementation domain deploys configurations, verifies compliance with the intent objectives and reports outcomes to the processing domain. By stakeholder goals (customers seek performance such as high throughput, operators seek resource efficiency), intents fall into four types: customer service intents mapping customer requests through SLAs, network service intents guiding the deployment of network functionalities, strategy intents governing QoS, fault resolution and general policies, and validation/customization intents providing verification and modification guidelines for other intents. Intents also introduce security exposure, since they can be intercepted (data exposure) or used to manipulate closed loops. Industry implementations include Cisco's Digital Network Architecture (SDN-based data-center automation), Huawei's Intent Engine (NETCONF/YANG models for closed-loop campus control) and Juniper Apstra (multi-vendor, context model plus telemetry for automated reconfiguration); no fully converged multi-domain IBN solution exists.

A converged framework for intent-based network and service management is organized in four layers: Application, Intent, Network Management & Orchestration, and Resources. The Application layer handles intent representation, balancing human readability against machine scalability through Constrained Natural Language (CNL) or Softgoal Inter-dependency Graphs (SIG); some implementations use RNN-based chatbots to extract performance goals from users. The Intent layer is built around the Intent Controller, which mediates between user desires and network reality. Declarative northbound interfaces (NBI) keep intent sources independent of provider implementations, and mapping tables and catalogs translate human terminology into machine-readable forms. The controller generates service models as Service Descriptors (SD) governed by finite-state machines (FSM), converts them into domain-specific requirements using TOSCA and YANG, and forwards them to orchestrators such as OSM or M-CORD. The Intent API is two-way: it exposes infrastructure capabilities (via YANG/TOSCA) to the controller and pushes processed service descriptors down to the network layer. Service assurance relies on closed-loop feedback that monitors deployed services and updates the service graph when resources change; Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been proposed to predict future resource states and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to select among Service Graphs (SG). The Network Management & Orchestration layer comprises domain controllers (SDN, NFV MANO), translates orchestrator models into device-specific configurations (XML, YAML), deploys VNF graphs and Service Function Chains (SFCs), and its monitoring modules supply data for reactive or proactive refinement. The Resources layer provides compute, storage and connectivity, and southbound interfaces (SBI) expose real-time resource status upward, which is what closes the loop and enables autonomy. Open issues include the readability/scalability tension in intent description, limits of YANG and TOSCA (reusability and inheritance in service models, semantics for imperative policies, dynamic dependency models allowing runtime modification of service graphs), and microservice-style decomposition of service design.

### SLA Decomposition and Network Slicing via GST/NEST
<!-- sources: intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review | gen: 27c8384b8d86 -->
Future networks must go beyond the 5G categories eMBB, uRLLC and mMTC to support use cases such as Holographic Type Communications (HTC), Multi-Sense Networks (MSN) and Time-Engineered Communications (TEC), requiring more management agility and collaboration among network operators, cloud providers and application providers via NFV, SDN and Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC). A central tradeoff lies between over-provisioning, which wastes resources, and under-provisioning, which degrades quality. SLA decomposition addresses it by breaking a high-level end-to-end SLA into flexible intent-based targets for individual network segments or domains, enabling differentiated QoS that matches actual vertical requirements. ### Zero-Touch Network and Service Management (ZSM)
<!-- sources: intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review, optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications, zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation | gen: 44ed689a2158 -->
Autonomous network management aims to reduce human intervention, since management by human experts and static scripts is becoming unsustainable given network complexity, deployment cost and the slow pace of administrator retraining; intents serve as the high-level interface to this autonomy. The need is driven by next-generation networks (5G and 6G) that must serve enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), ultra-reliable low-latency communication (uRLLC) and massive machine-type communication (mMTC) on SDN, NFV and multi-access edge computing (MEC) infrastructure. Beyond-5G targets include data rates up to 1 Tbps, latency of 10–100 μs and device density of 10^7 per km², with terahertz communication and network intelligence. Static management and orchestration (MANO) cannot cope with this softwarized, virtualized complexity, so zero-touch operation relies on 'Self-X' behavior (self-configuration, self-monitoring, self-healing and self-optimization) to preserve end-to-end quality of experience. Several standardization efforts drive the evolution. ETSI Zero-touch Network and Service Management (ZSM) targets end-to-end automation across multi-domain environments, covering the lifecycle from design and planning to monitoring and optimization, and provides an orchestration and analytics framework with closed management loops covering monitoring, root-cause analysis and automated reconfiguration according to defined levels of autonomy. Its architecture is modular and scalable and built on 'service composability': management domains (MDs) handle local physical and virtual resources, an end-to-end cross-domain service management domain orchestrates across MDs, and cross-domain data services support global optimization, with data collection, intelligence, analytics, control and orchestration services inside the domains. Closed-loop automation and intent-based interfaces let objectives be reached without external intervention. Requirements fall into three categories: non-functional (availability, energy efficiency, vendor independence, high data availability), functional (cross-domain management, closed-loop control, real-time data collection, automated overload handling) and security (data protection at rest and in transit, privacy by design, and automatic detection and mitigation of attacks on ML/AI components). Related initiatives include IETF ANIMA (an AI framework for end-to-end network management and autonomic networking), ITU-T SG13 (AI-based automation in future networks, Recommendation Y.3177), ETSI ENI (cognitive management and SLA management), TM Forum ZOOM and ODA (zero-touch orchestration through open APIs and cloud-native blueprints) and EU Horizon 2020 projects such as MonB5G (distributed slice management) and Hexa-X (architectural foundation for 6G). Zero-touch network operation (ZNO) applies ZSM to four areas: resource management (dynamic allocation via ML and MEC), traffic control (predictive classification and intelligent routing), energy efficiency (adapting power use to real-time demand) and security and privacy (automated threat detection and response). Traditional ML inside ZSM is hindered by the need for expertise, large volumes of uncleaned data, difficult model selection, intensive hyperparameter tuning and volatile wireless environments, which motivates AutoML and network digital twins. In optical networks, ZSM relies on AI/ML for predictive analytics, for example forecasting traffic to scale resources proactively, and its functions have been mapped onto the OODA loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act); the overall system response time then depends heavily on the delay of data exchange between these functional elements. Typical AI/ML applications in this setting are reinforcement learning (RL) and multi-agent systems (MAS) for real-time route and wavelength assignment; recurrent neural networks (RNNs) analyzing telemetry to predict component failure and so support 'seven to nine nines' reliability; intent-based networking (IBN), which translates high-level objectives into low-level configurations using AI and large language models; and unsupervised learning for anomaly detection in traffic patterns for security. ETSI Experiential Network Intelligence (ENI) integrates AI and context-aware policies for cognitive management, adding situational awareness and knowledge management; it defines a path toward full autonomicity (categories Cat-4/5) in which the network performs self-initiated optimization and intents act as the primary human-machine interface. IETF ANIMA works toward an integrated autonomic networking model. The ITU Networks-2030 vision identifies gaps in programmability and lifecycle agility; intent-based approaches address them through SLO-aware interfaces and support for coordinated services in which multiple interdependent requests are negotiated via closed-loop feedback. Together with SDN/NFV and AI/ML inference (for example, extracting service primitives from abstract text, optimizing orchestration paths and proactive resource monitoring, potentially delivered as Machine Learning as a Service, MLaaS), these standards form the enabling basis of intent-driven autonomy. Remaining challenges include intent security (malicious intents appropriating resources, intent ownership and audit trails) and the inability of centralized orchestrators to scale across domains, which calls for distributed orchestration. Further open issues are explainable zero-touch management (black-box AI is problematic for critical services such as remote surgery), trustworthy sharing of topology data between operators (blockchain, trusted execution environments, encryption) and the computational cost of real-time analysis of large data volumes, for which FPGA/GPU acceleration is suggested. AI also imposes its own burdens: it needs massive, low-latency telemetry from the optical layer, creating a circular dependency in which the network must be designed to support the AI that manages it, and 'black box' decisions require explainable AI (XAI).

### Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) for Network Management
<!-- sources: zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation | gen: a66db1b920bf -->
Automated machine learning (AutoML) automates the stages of the ML pipeline, reducing the data-science expertise needed to deploy models in zero-touch network and service management (ZSM), where manual feature engineering, model selection and hyperparameter tuning are otherwise bottlenecks. Automated data preprocessing covers transformation (for example target encoding of categorical features), imputation of missing values with model-based (K-NN, XGBoost) or model-free methods, class balancing by under- or over-sampling, and normalization by Z-score or Min-Max scaling. Automated feature engineering comprises feature generation, selection (filter, wrapper or embedded methods) and extraction (for example PCA). Automated model learning searches architectures and hyperparameters with grid search, random search, Bayesian optimization or Hyperband, then trains candidates with optimizers such as Adam or SGD and regularization against overfitting. Automated model updating handles data drift (a shift in the input distribution) and concept drift (a change in the relationship between inputs and the target task), which degrade network ML models over time. Detection is either distribution-based, for example the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test comparing recent data with the training distribution, or performance-based, tracking an error metric over a sliding window and raising an alarm when it exceeds a threshold. Adaptation options are incremental updates of existing weights, full retraining, transfer learning from the previous model, and ensemble methods. Applications include cybersecurity, where AutoML supports DDoS and intrusion detection (IDS) by dynamically selecting the best algorithm according to node power levels and traffic payloads in resource-constrained sensor networks, and it has been combined with network digital twins to keep IDS models synchronized efficiently.

A case study on application throughput prediction supports quality of experience for 5G+ services such as video streaming, since ZSM can use the forecasts to scale resources proactively. It used a production dataset from an Irish mobile operator with 4G and 5G traces for video streaming and file downloads, including KPIs such as RSRP, RSRQ and downlink bitrate. Throughput distributions were consistent across days but varied by hour (skewed toward low values in the early morning), calling for time-aware models. The pipeline used LightGBM importance and Pearson's rank for feature selection and a Seq2Seq LSTM encoder-decoder tuned by Bayesian optimization. Drift detection was windowed and performance-based: when the mean absolute error (MAE) exceeded a dynamic threshold, weights were updated incrementally instead of retraining from scratch. In the proposed 5G architecture, an Application Edge Slice (AES) in the RAN collects traffic data and runs the pipeline locally, and its predictions go to the NWDAF, which informs ZSM resource-allocation decisions through the NSSF and NSSMF. AutoML with Seq2Seq outperformed traditional LSTM and manually built Seq2Seq models on all datasets (4G/5G, streaming/downloads); for 5G file downloads it reached a MAPE of 3.58% versus 4.76% for the manual Seq2Seq model. A complexity-accuracy trade-off appears in the look-back window: more past data improves accuracy but raises computational cost, while longer look-ahead horizons increase MAE and require more history. With simulated congestion-induced drift, MAE spiked; the system detected it at the next periodic check, performed every 10 minutes, and updated the model, restoring accuracy, which illustrates a trade-off between monitoring frequency (shorter time with a degraded model) and computational overhead. Open challenges are interpretability (AutoML tends to favor accuracy over transparency, so causality constraints and XAI methods such as SHAP and LIME are needed for regulatory compliance), scalability (exhaustive model search is slow on large network datasets, calling for parallel or distributed search and data sampling), robustness (vulnerability to adversarial noise injection, addressed by defensive distillation and adversarial training) and cold start (inefficient initial search, mitigated by meta-learning, transfer learning from pre-trained models and domain knowledge).

## Network Digital Twins

### Hierarchical Network Digital Twins (User, Infrastructure, Slice)
<!-- sources: ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications, zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation | gen: ff4471dbb327 -->
A digital twin (DT) is a real-time digital replica of a physical system with bidirectional data flow, enabled by IoT sensors, cloud storage and high-performance connectivity to keep virtual and physical worlds synchronized. A network digital twin (NDT) is a virtual representation of a physical communication network's structure and dynamics, organized in a three-layer hierarchy. The User DT (bottom layer) captures fine-grained profile, behavior and networking information and is deployed at the edge so that local controllers can perceive user status in real time. The Infrastructure DT (middle layer) mirrors network hardware such as base stations and servers, integrating topology, configuration and performance metrics to analyze traffic patterns and balance workloads. The Slice DT (top layer) manages isolated logical networks; deployed at core nodes, it uses data from the User and Infrastructure DTs to estimate spatio-temporal resource demands and validate slicing policies. Data flows from the physical network up through User → Infrastructure → Slice DT and then to the network controller. The layers operate on different timescales: User and Infrastructure DT cycles run at sub-second scales, while Slice DT decisions operate on longer strategic timescales of minutes to hours. In zero-touch network and service management, NDTs provide a risk-free environment for what-if analysis, with applications in online optimization (load balancing), troubleshooting (replaying past failures), disaster-scenario testing and strategic planning (predicting resource exhaustion); DRL agents can be trained on them to avoid risky configurations on live networks. Standardization is under way in ISO/IEC (manufacturing DTs), IEEE (digital representation of objects), ITU-T (Y.3090, NDT requirements) and IETF (NDT reference architectures). Reported advances include DTs managing metasurface reflectors in 6G terahertz communications, AutoML combined with DTs for efficient IDS synchronization, and TwinNet, a graph neural network that estimates end-to-end path delays on topologies unseen in training. Applied to the optical fronthaul, digital twins are proposed for proactive capacity planning, for simulating configuration changes before they are deployed, and for early fault detection. Because AI-driven management must be trained on rare faults that cannot be provoked on live networks, digital twins are used to simulate such faults without risking outages, and mandating digital twins to test AI configurations before deployment is among the strategic recommendations for 6G optical transport; they also support zero-touch operation and the Zero Trust validation of automated decisions. Open issues include flexible, mobility-aware deployment, for example placing User DTs at medium-level nodes to keep continuity during frequent base station handovers, and efficient collaboration among DTs. Causal discovery is argued to strengthen DT modeling: learning the causal structure of the physical twin prevents bias from unknown confounding variables, improving traffic prediction and resource allocation compared with purely correlational models.

### AI-Native Network Management in 6G
<!-- sources: ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks, intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review, optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited, toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges | gen: 8116a4f0069b -->
In an AI-native 6G network, AI shifts from an add-on to a native element integrated into network design and operation, a concept described as integrated AI and communication (IAAC), in which AI is embedded in the protocol stack for predictive bandwidth management and spectrum optimization, is used at the edge for faster response in autonomous systems, and supports proactive cyber-threat detection. It is spread over three tiers: an intelligent core that moves from cloud-native to AI-empowered resource allocation and traffic management, an intelligent edge that combines edge computing and AI for local processing and reduces the core's burden, and an intelligent air interface in which learning-based solutions replace inflexible PHY and MAC algorithms. Pervasive intelligence across edge and cloud supports DT-based management through status prediction, pattern abstraction and automated decision-making. Three categories of AI are distinguished. Foundation AI (e.g., GPT, BERT) provides knowledge reasoning and multi-modal perception; these high-resource models are typically deployed in the cloud to extract high-level features from heterogeneous data. Decision-Making AI (e.g., RL, LSTM) performs time-series forecasting of traffic load and latency and uncertainty-aware automated decisions; its lower resource needs suit edge deployment. Analytical AI (e.g., autoencoders, GNNs) handles anomaly diagnosis, root-cause analysis and feature abstraction through structural awareness and explainability. Within the DT hierarchy, the User DT uses RNNs for status prediction and CNNs/autoencoders to distill high-dimensional user data into salient features (e.g., swipe probability in video streaming); the Infrastructure DT uses GNNs for topology analysis, CNNs for traffic flow recognition and DRL with simulators; the Slice DT uses RNNs, LLMs with attention to assess user satisfaction from text and traffic data, and DRL to refine slicing policies. The workflow has four steps: (1) prediction and adaptation, where RNN predictions are compared with actual data and collection frequency is raised if the gap exceeds a threshold; (2) feature extraction and policy creation, where the User DT extracts features, the Infrastructure DT analyzes patterns via CNNs and DRL generates policies validated in an internal simulator; (3) strategic slicing, where abstracted data reaches the Slice DT and LLMs and DRL inform slicing decisions validated in a slice-level simulator; and (4) implementation, where validated decisions are sent to the network controller. Open issues include hybrid data-model-driven decision-making, using convex optimization for simple sub-problems and data-driven AI for complex non-convex ones. At the physical and transport layers, AI is envisioned both as an enhancer of communication and as a beneficiary of it, and is applied to dynamic resource allocation, anomaly detection and predictive maintenance based on sensing data such as fiber strain, traffic load and user mobility; the target is Level 4 network autonomy, managed with AI and SDN across converged fixed and mobile access. AI-driven virtualization is also applied to network function management (NFV). The integration of AI across protocol-stack layers raises standardization difficulties, and AI-driven networks are exposed to data poisoning and adversarial attacks. In intent-based management, AI/ML supplies the inference part of the loop: NLP, RNNs (including chatbots) and controlled-vocabulary NLP translate intents, GANs predict future resource states, DRL selects service graphs, and attention models such as BERT are proposed for semantic retrieval of intents; Machine Learning as a Service (MLaaS) is suggested as a deployment model, though its integration into the IBN control loop needs dedicated study. Intent-based approaches also enlarge the attack surface by giving users more influence over network design, so verification policies against malicious intents and distributed orchestration for multi-domain intents are open issues. A complementary view frames causality as the bedrock of AI-native management rather than mimicking functions: causal reasoning is integrated into sampling, QoS management, encoding, scheduling and communication across the stack, with layer-wise SCMs tailored to specific OSI layers and aligned with Open RAN. Resilience is handled as a detect-remediate-recover loop using counterfactual optimization over the causal graph. Shifting from purely data-driven foundation models, which are energy-inefficient with billions of parameters and prone to bias, toward causality-based models yields lighter, interpretable models that need less retraining.

### Adaptive Inference and Semantic Communication for Digital Twin Maintenance
<!-- sources: ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks | gen: a318db1e713c -->
Building and synchronizing digital twins (DTs) requires collecting massive multimodal data (video, audio, logs) from IoT devices, which can congest the network. Semantic communication addresses this by transmitting the meaning or contextual significance of information rather than raw data, using AI encoders and decoders at the transmitter and receiver, which reduces bandwidth requirements while maintaining DT fidelity. It is proposed as one of three solutions in an AI-native DT framework, alongside adaptive inference and online model updating, and complements adaptive collection, in which sampling frequency is raised only when predicted and observed data diverge beyond a threshold. A causal view defines semantics as the inherent causal structure of the data: using causal representation learning, the network can form a unique representation for states with similar cause-and-effect repertoires and transmit only these causal states, further reducing transmission overhead. Proposed extensions integrate this with integrated information theory and hypergame theory.

Two linked problems arise in DT-based management: high-fidelity real-time simulation is computationally intensive and energy-hungry, and shifts in user behavior, service demand and device interoperability cause data drift that makes static AI models obsolete. Adaptive network DT model inference scales computational effort dynamically: lightweight models handle common tasks such as bandwidth allocation, while complex models are triggered for critical tasks such as anomaly detection, with context-aware scheduling that considers energy budgets and prediction uncertainty. To counter drift, a dual error-based model update mechanism uses mean squared error (MSE) when labeled data are available and the entropy of the output probability distribution when data are unlabeled, with updates performed by incremental learning to avoid full retraining. In a multicast short video streaming case study, real-time QoE fluctuated when user behavior changed and recovered as DT models were updated online; the adaptive User DT approach achieved an average QoE increase of 10.3% over a hierarchical DRL scheme at a bandwidth of 2.2 MHz.

## Edge, Fog, and Cloud Computing Foundations

### Edge Computing, Fog Computing, MEC, and Cloudlets — Differentiation
<!-- sources: edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond | gen: e07752a785bb -->
Edge computing is motivated by the projected growth of connected devices to about 500 billion by 2030, which makes centralized cloud systems insufficient because of network congestion and latency, and by real-time applications such as autonomous vehicles, telemedicine and AR/VR. Mobile broadband and IoT create demand for latency, throughput and dependability roughly ten times beyond current levels, and moving intelligence to the edge reduces backbone traffic and cost. Edge-based solutions minimize bandwidth demand and reliance on connectivity by processing data at the gateway or device level, and include cloudlets, cyber foraging, fog computing and mobile edge computing. Cloudlets are small-scale data centers at the network edge that act as intermediaries between mobile devices and the cloud, reducing latency for time-sensitive tasks such as AR/VR. Mobile edge computing was originally defined by ETSI and is now called Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC); it extends cloud features to the RAN by deploying servers, using virtualization, at base stations (eNodeB) or RNC sites. Fog computing, pioneered by Cisco, extends the cloud to incorporate both central and peripheral infrastructure; unlike MEC's fixed locations, fog nodes can be deployed flexibly, for example on power poles or in vehicles. Related paradigms include content delivery networks (CDNs), developed to address the 'flash crowd' problem by distributing content across multiple edge servers, reducing load on central servers and improving user experience through localized storage of web data; ubiquitous (pervasive) computing, which embeds computing seamlessly in the everyday environment so that smart devices collaborate invisibly; peer-to-peer (P2P) architectures, which let equal nodes share resources directly without a central server, increasing resilience and scalability for applications such as file sharing and cryptocurrencies; and cloud computing, which delivers on-demand services (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS) over the internet on a pay-as-you-go basis, removing the need for local physical infrastructure. A current limitation is that many edge architectures rely on fixed infrastructure at specific sites, which restricts scalability, motivating long-term strategies that integrate terrestrial and space networks. Edge computing also involves four market actors (clients, ISPs, cloud providers and Edge Service Providers), whose interrelations determine service cost and pricing.

CROWN (Contractual, Reputational and Opportunistic Wireless Networking) is proposed for decentralized edge environments to address the 'entropy problem', the difficulty of discovering resources in a dynamic, heterogeneous edge. It dynamically maps link (l), storage (s) and computation (c) capacity of nodes; because this resource mapping is NP-hard, it is transformed into a convex optimization problem called Orthogonal Entropy-Division Multiple Access (OEDMA). Node availability is projected onto three PASS metrics: Primacy (selection rank), Supremacy (suitability for a task) and Sway (duration of occupancy). The implementation framework has three components: Contractual and Opportunistic Broadcasting (COB), in which nodes proactively broadcast their context and available resources; Proof of Reputation (POR), which uses reinforcement learning to assign PASS values from each node's historical performance; and Proof of Consensus (POC), which uses blockchain to coordinate and reach consensus on PASS values among nodes. The stated goal is a fully decentralized, federated environment that democratizes computation resources, potentially leading to cost-free cloud services.

### Cloud-Edge-Terminal Collaborative Network (CETCN) and Computing Continuum
<!-- sources: ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions, ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond, end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey, towards_6g_a_review_of_optical_transport_challenges_for_intelligent_and_autonomous_communications | gen: 5571e93f91bb -->
The Cloud-Edge-Terminal Collaborative Network (CETCN) is a three-layer architecture (Terminal → Edge → Cloud) intended to meet the low-latency and ultra-reliability needs of applications such as remote surgery and smart transportation, which centralized cloud computing cannot satisfy because of propagation delay and bandwidth bottlenecks. In the 6G optical transport view this appears as a Cloud-Edge-Device continuum in which multi-access edge computing (MEC) and Edge AI handle latency-sensitive tasks, and the optical network must support joint communication-computation optimisation (JCC), managing optical bandwidth and compute resources (CPU/GPU) as a single entity to optimize task placement; otherwise slow transport would neutralize Tbps radio capability. The terminal layer comprises sensors and devices that collect data or generate tasks; the edge layer comprises base stations, roadside units (RSUs) and edge servers that provide low-latency processing, using short-range links such as WiFi, Bluetooth and ZigBee; the cloud layer provides centralized data centers for heavy computation, long-term storage and global orchestration. Cloud computing (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS) suffers from transfer latency, outage risk, unpredictable pay-as-you-go costs and third-party privacy exposure, while serverless (FaaS) adds cold-start latency and stateless-execution limits. Multi-access edge computing (MEC) places servers at base stations or access points, reducing delay, saving IoT battery by offloading compute-intensive tasks, and improving privacy through local processing; it can run stand-alone. Fog computing sinks resources to fog nodes such as routers and gateways, is characterized by heterogeneity and distributed management, and is typically a tight extension of the cloud that requires cloud connectivity. Geographically, the cloud is centralized and oriented to big-data analysis and long-term storage, whereas fog/MEC are decentralized and oriented to real-time, short-term processing for local business execution. In cloud-edge collaboration the edge handles real-time control and data analysis while the cloud handles complex tasks and orchestration (e.g., industrial infrared service platforms, equipment defect detection); the cloud-edge-terminal variant also exploits terminal compute, such as parked vehicles or UAVs acting as auxiliary nodes, to relieve the edge in remote areas or at peak periods. Offloading in this continuum can proceed end-device to end-device, device to cloud, or vertically and horizontally across the mist-fog-cloud continuum, and neighboring user devices may form transient 'mobile clouds' that process tasks collaboratively. A complementary framing distinguishes 'AI for end-edge-cloud' (using AI to optimize the network) from 'end-edge-cloud for AI', in which the three tiers jointly serve the whole deep-learning lifecycle of training, inference and updating, motivated by the fact that large pre-trained foundation models (e.g., GPT-3, PaLM) exceed edge and end-device capacity while the cloud alone brings high latency, fragility under network interruptions during lifelong adaptation, and privacy risk. In this framing collaboration is organized along three dimensions. The data dimension is data-based (raw observations uploaded), information-based (gradients or feature maps shared to cut bandwidth and improve privacy) or knowledge-based (instance, representation or relational knowledge, often via distillation from a cloud 'teacher' to an edge 'student'). The model dimension treats training, inference and updating as a continuous process, with updating emphasizing lifelong learning and avoidance of catastrophic forgetting. The computing-power dimension spans the cloud (massive resources, high latency, privacy risk), the edge (location-aware, lower latency, limited capacity) and end devices (immediate response, severe constraints). Collaboration can be vertical (cross-layer), horizontal (between nodes of the same layer) or integrated (a hybrid of both). The conclusion drawn in the surveyed work is that future networks depend on hybrid edge-cloud architectures capable of balancing small-scale real-time intelligence at the edge with large-scale centralized processing, with remaining gaps in security, server positioning and hybrid system optimization. 'Endogenous intelligence' (cloud-edge-terminal intelligent collaboration) denotes low-cost, high-efficiency collaborative optimization across layers, with four stated advantages over pure cloud or terminal intelligence: lower bandwidth cost, higher real-time performance, stronger privacy protection and a higher overall system intelligence through DRL. AI can act at every stage of the data life cycle: preprocessing at collection, adaptive modulation in transmission, pattern recognition in processing, de-duplication in storage and predictive maintenance in utilization. Network DTs map naturally onto this layering: User DTs are deployed at the edge for real-time perception of user status, Slice DTs at core nodes for strategic slicing, and resource-heavy foundation models (e.g., GPT, BERT) in the cloud, while lighter decision-making models such as RL and LSTM run at the edge. Placement can be mobility-aware, for instance hosting User DTs at medium-level nodes to preserve continuity during frequent base station handovers. Core challenges are heterogeneous device capacity, NP-hard multi-objective optimization, and mixed-integer programming that couples discrete caching decisions with continuous offloading variables; the decentralization also multiplies management complexity across wavelengths, compute and storage, which makes AI orchestration indispensable. Further system-level challenges include unified scheduling across computing power networks (CPN), dynamic neural networks that adapt their structure to input or environment, and real-time monitoring to avoid disruptions in industrial settings. Communication is a bottleneck because internal memory speeds far exceed network bandwidth (a 'bucket effect'); proposed remedies are lossless or lossy data compression and 6G, which integrates sensing, computing and communication.

### Task Offloading, Task Scheduling, and Joint Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions, convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities, edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond, joint_service_migration_and_resource_allocation_in_edge_iot_system_based_on_deep_reinforcement_learning | gen: 4d23deb4ae5b -->
ETSI Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) integrates decentralized cloud capabilities at the network edge through a three-level structure: networks, MEC hosts and the MEC system. A common virtualization layer for compute and network resources provides a basis for convergence, and integration with NFV allows MEC applications and VNFs to share infrastructure and management tools. Within such edge platforms, task offloading decides what to offload and where computation runs among the terminal, edge servers and the cloud; directions include device-to-device, device-to-cloud, and vertical/horizontal transfers across the mist-fog-cloud continuum. Binary offloading treats tasks as indivisible, executed entirely at one location, while partial offloading splits a task across several computing entities. Offloading is formalized with models of computation capacity, data-size constraints, energy consumption and total latency, expressed as the sum of local execution time and offloading time (l = ΣT_local + T_offload). Task scheduling assigns tasks to specific edge devices to minimize the overall makespan (completion time) and balance load across heterogeneous hardware. Joint resource allocation coordinates CPU, memory, bandwidth and storage across both mobile devices and servers, typically with adaptive algorithms based on game theory or AI to cope with dynamic edge environments, with the goal of ensuring Quality of Experience (QoE). Resource allocation is modeled along three dimensions: the computing model (CPU cycle frequency and energy consumption, which scales quadratically with frequency), the communication model (multipath fading, interference and spectrum scarcity, with 6G targeting millisecond-level air-interface delay and higher energy efficiency) and the caching model (placement of popular content to cut backhaul traffic and latency). The joint problem trades off energy and delay under limited edge and terminal resources, and is a multi-objective, NP-hard mixed-integer program combining discrete decisions (offload or not, what to cache) with continuous variables (power, frequency, bandwidth). Surveyed optimization work also uses VM migration, UAV-assisted connectivity and reinforcement learning, and stresses application-aware offloading and microservice-based deployment for resource-constrained edges. A latency-minimization formulation for mobile IoT users models each task by its data size and the CPU cycles required per bit, so that edge-server (ES) computing power is expressed in CPU cycles rather than simplified as the number of users served. Total delay combines communication delay, based on the Shannon transmission rate between user and ES, and computation delay at the serving ES. The objective minimizes the average sum of processing delays of all users over an infinite horizon of time slots, with CPU and bandwidth allocated jointly at the target ES. Constraints include per-ES CPU capacity, per-ES bandwidth capacity, binary migrate/non-migrate decisions and the requirement that each user is served by exactly one ES. Because of the binary variables and dynamic environment, the problem is nonconvex, which motivates DRL. Simulation shows that when the upper limits of requested CPU and bandwidth grow, target ESs increasingly cannot satisfy demands, so the migration ratio falls and average task latency rises; adding ESs lowers latency because total available resources increase. DRL approaches avoid solving such non-convex problems explicitly. Single-agent methods (DQN, DDQN, actor-critic) minimize long-term energy and delay in terminal-edge or edge-cloud setups; POMDP and Dec-POMDP formulations handle incomplete knowledge of channel states or neighbor workloads; and MARL (MADDPG, mean-field RL) coordinates large populations to avoid congestion. Application examples include DDPG for balancing energy and latency in vehicular networks, MADDPG with blockchain for secure low-latency offloading of medical data, DQN/DDQN for mapping service function chains onto IIoT edge resources, and DQN-based migration of deep learning models in UAV-assisted agriculture. Two research gaps are identified: scalability and complexity under unpredictable resource patterns and heterogeneous devices and applications, and global resource orchestration, since optimal offloading mechanisms for nodes spread over wide geographic areas under mobility and strict resource limits are lacking. The literature is noted to rely mostly on simplified simulations rather than real traces; future directions include adapting to dynamic workloads and user mobility, federated learning to keep data local, green offloading that prioritizes carbon footprint, and optimizing quality of experience rather than only latency and energy.

### Service Migration and Mobility Prediction
<!-- sources: joint_service_migration_and_resource_allocation_in_edge_iot_system_based_on_deep_reinforcement_learning, ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions | gen: 30132c76e744 -->
Service migration in multi-access edge computing keeps latency-sensitive services close to mobile users by moving a service instance (SI) from a source edge server (ES) to a target ES. It addresses a conflict created by user mobility and limited ES resources: remaining connected to a distant source ES increases communication delay, whereas migrating to a closer target ES incurs migration latency. The total delay of a migrated service has three parts: migration delay (the sum of SI suspending time, state context data synchronization time between ESs and SI resuming time), communication delay from the user to the target ES, and computation delay at the target ES. Without migration, delay consists only of communication delay (computed from the Shannon transmission rate) and computation delay at the source ES. The benefit of migrating depends on the resources the target ES can allocate, since a nearby ES that cannot satisfy a user's CPU and bandwidth demand yields little gain; migration and resource allocation must therefore be optimized jointly. Prior work is characterized as using Lyapunov optimization or specific placement schemes, assuming uniform resource allocation across users, ignoring the effect of target-ES resource policies, ignoring mobility patterns, or lacking the predictive handover needed for seamless service; network-slice migration studies focus mainly on bandwidth rather than combined computing and communication resources. The migration decision is binary, each user is served by exactly one ES, and the problem of minimizing average processing delay over an infinite horizon is nonconvex. Simulation against always-migrate (AMS), never-migrate (NMS) and random-migrate (RMS) policies shows the trade-off: AMS incurs unnecessary migrations, NMS suffers long-distance communication, and a learned joint policy outperforms both as the number of users grows. When the upper limits of requested resources rise, the migration ratio decreases because target ESs cannot meet the demands.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks predict a mobile user's near-future location from its historical trajectory so that service placement and migration can be decided proactively. In a DRL framework for edge IoT, the predicted location is part of the MDP state and determines the target ES, namely the nearest ES to the predicted position. This removes the need for a global search over all ESs and reduces the action space, leaving the DRL agent to decide only whether to migrate and how much CPU and bandwidth to allocate; the state also includes resources currently allocated by the source ES and those available at the target ES. The predictor was trained on the Microsoft Research Asia Beijing cab trajectory dataset (10,357 cabs) and evaluated with 70 mobile users and 60 ESs spaced 5 km apart. Prediction supports seamless handover and service continuity under high mobility. The LSTM adds a cost depending on sequence length and number of hidden units, giving overall complexity O(z·w_full + z·w_lstm) over z iterations. Combined with a Parameterized DQN, the approach achieved lower average task processing latency than DDPG-, DQN-, random-, always- and never-migrate baselines. More broadly, mobility management must preserve continuity despite changing channel conditions; 5G 3GPP mechanisms cover intra-RAT and inter-RAT handover, idle-mode mobility, Xn/NG-based handover, dual connectivity and network-slicing mobility. Mobility causes handover disruption, packet loss, jitter and signal fluctuation, and traditional Markov-chain models are considered too simple for heterogeneous cloud-edge-terminal networks. DRL is applied to trajectory prediction (Q-learning combined with memory networks/RNNs), handover optimization (DQN/DDQN to minimize handover frequency and radio link failure probability), and UAV-assisted mobility (real-time path planning for seamless coverage). The main open issue is real-time decision-making, because the computational overhead of DRL training and inference may conflict with the millisecond timescale of a handover event.

### Collaborative Caching and Cache Replacement
<!-- sources: ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions, edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond | gen: 5c9a4d58e2ea -->
Collaborative caching stores popular content at the network edge to cut core traffic and latency. Retrieval is tiered: a request goes from the user to the local edge server, then to a cooperative edge domain, and finally to the cloud data center, with the main constraint being the finite caching capacity C_i of each edge node. Enabling technologies include VMs and containers on edge servers for agility, and SDN/NFV, whose programmable data plane routes requests dynamically to the most efficient cache location. Related precedents include CDNs, which localize web content on distributed edge servers, and Named Data Networking, which provides in-network caching. Surveyed strategies range from popularity-based caching at the edge to UE-side pre-fetching using social recommendations, while collaborative pre-fetching remains under-explored. Classical replacement policies (LRU, LFU, FIFO) are being replaced by AI-driven popularity prediction, since DRL can learn content popularity without prior knowledge. Q-learning and DQN are used for content replacement policies; D2D communication lets users share cached content and offload edge servers; and federated caching lets base stations collaboratively train prediction models without sharing raw user data. Open directions are privacy-preserving caching that does not require users to disclose content preferences, data replication that places copies to avoid redundancy while ensuring availability, and economic models for fair benefit-sharing among hardware providers, content owners and operators.

### Information-Centric Networking (ICN) / Named Data Networking (NDN) for Edge Computing
<!-- sources: edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond | gen: f460fb3d5b22 -->
Information-Centric Networking (ICN), and in particular Named Data Networking (NDN), is highlighted in edge computing research for its ability to prioritize critical traffic and to enable remote computation without IP address mapping, since content and computations are addressed by name. Its drawback is higher overhead than TCP/IP. To secure NDN, reputation schemes based on the historical behavior of nodes are proposed as an alternative to traditional cryptographic certificates. The same reputation idea underlies decentralized edge frameworks such as CROWN, where reinforcement learning assigns trust-related scores to nodes from past performance and blockchain consensus coordinates them. Related literature also points to collaborative 'mobile clouds' or 'transient clouds', in which neighboring user devices pool resources to process tasks, a setting where trust among unfamiliar peers is a central concern.

### Space-Based MEC and Non-Terrestrial Edge Computing

## Distributed and Collaborative Machine Learning for Networks

### End-Edge-Cloud Collaboration Dimensions
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: c2b880f58922 -->
The 'end-edge-cloud for AI' perspective, as distinct from 'AI for end-edge-cloud' (using AI to optimize the network), studies how end devices, edge nodes and cloud centers jointly support the deep learning (DL) lifecycle. Motivation: DL performance grows with model size, but large pre-trained foundation models exceed the compute and storage of edge and end devices, while cloud-only deployment brings high communication latency, instability of lifelong learning and adaptation under network interruptions, and data privacy risks. Collaboration is analyzed along three dimensions. In the data dimension it is data-based (uploading raw observations, as in cloud DL), information-based (sharing processed data or intermediate results such as gradients in training or feature maps in inference, reducing bandwidth and improving privacy) or knowledge-based (sharing instance, representation or relational knowledge, often through knowledge distillation from a cloud teacher to an edge student). In the model dimension, DL is a continuous cycle of training, inference and updating, with updating distinguished by lifelong learning and prevention of catastrophic forgetting. In the computing power dimension, the cloud offers massive resources but high latency and privacy risk, the edge offers location-aware, lower-latency processing with limited capacity, and end devices respond immediately under severe constraints. Collaboration can be vertical (across layers), horizontal (between nodes of the same layer) or integrated (a hybrid). The resulting framework of co-training, co-inference and co-updating is supported by model compression, partitioning and knowledge transfer; open system issues include unified scheduling across computing power networks (CPN), adaptability and reliability monitoring for industrial use.

### Model/Data Parallelism and Distributed Co-Training
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey, leveraging_federated_learning_for_optimizing_fiber_optic_sensor_design_for_biodetection, toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity | gen: 0e12cec6f227 -->
Collaborative training across end devices, edge nodes and the cloud is organized into two parallelism strategies. In data parallelism each node holds a copy of the model and processes a subset of the data. The centralized form relies on a parameter server that aggregates gradients or model updates, as in FedAvg-style federated learning (FL); the decentralized form has nodes exchange updates directly with peers, removing the central server and its single point of failure. Personalized FL addresses Non-IID (non-independently and identically distributed) client data, which degrades a single global model, by fine-tuning the global model locally or by approaches such as virtual homogeneity learning. In model parallelism the model itself is split across nodes, which is needed when it is too large for any single device. Layer-level (inter-layer) parallelism assigns sequential groups of layers to different nodes, and pipeline parallelism such as GPipe splits each batch into micro-batches so that nodes are idle less of the time. Neuron-level (intra-layer) parallelism partitions tensors or weight matrices along chosen dimensions, as in Megatron-LM, so that each node computes part of every layer. Horizontal collaboration (between peers of the same tier), vertical collaboration (across end, edge and cloud) and integrated schemes combining both can be used to place these workloads. In the 6G edge-AI view, edge learning models comprise federated learning (privacy-preserving distributed training through a central aggregator), decentralized learning (peer-to-peer model exchange without a central server, increasing resilience), model split learning (a DNN divided between devices and servers to balance compute load), distributed reinforcement learning (collaborative decision-making in dynamic environments such as autonomous vehicles) and trustworthy learning, which targets security and fairness using blockchain or Byzantine-resilient aggregation. Decentralizing computation to the network edge lowers latency and bandwidth use while improving privacy. Sharing gradients or other intermediate information instead of raw data lowers bandwidth use and improves privacy, though communication overhead remains the main limit: the gap between internal memory speed and network bandwidth (a 'bucket effect') makes synchronization costly, motivating lossless or lossy data compression and, prospectively, 6G networks that integrate sensing, computing and communication. FL has also been applied outside the network domain, for example to plasmonic fiber optic biosensor design, where each distributed sensing node trains a local Gaussian Process Regressor on its own simulation or measurement data and the locally trained regression models' parameters are aggregated into a global model, so that raw medical data never leave the node. In that setting, communication overhead and data heterogeneity across nodes (differing data quality and distributions, partly addressed by personalized FL) remain open issues, with improved aggregation mechanisms and reduced communication cost identified as future work.

### Progressive Co-Inference: Early Exiting and Model Segment Partitioning
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: b7277fc68801 -->
End-edge-cloud collaborative inference uses two mechanisms. Progressive co-inference via early exiting adds side branches to the network (e.g., BranchyNet): if a sample's confidence at an early branch on the end or edge device exceeds a threshold, inference terminates there, and otherwise the intermediate data is passed to deeper layers on the edge or cloud. Exit criteria are either rule-based, using entropy or confidence thresholds, or learning-based, with a trained module predicting the best exit point. A security concern specific to this design is the "early exit attack", which targets the progressive inference mechanism. Distributed co-inference via model segments physically partitions the model across nodes at chosen split points, minimizing communication overhead. Layer-level partitioning divides the model into sequential segments (e.g., JointDNN) and exploits the fact that intermediate feature maps are often smaller than the raw input, so executing early layers locally and offloading the rest can reduce transmitted data. Neurosurgeon selects a split point to minimize latency or energy, and DADS represents the model as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and formulates partitioning as a min-cut problem that minimizes latency or energy, which also handles non-chain topologies. Static prediction models of layer latency and bandwidth cope poorly with dynamic network conditions, and structures with residual connections require more advanced graph analysis.

Neuron-level partitioning splits computation within layers, either by channel (input channels, partitioning kernels, or output channels, partitioning filters) or spatially (grid or vertical splits of the input). Spatial splitting requires a full model copy on every node, which is problematic for very large models. Future work points toward self-adaptive partitioning that selects partition points and compression ratios from real-time hardware availability and task demands instead of static thresholds, and toward dynamic neural networks that adjust their structure to input or environment.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Lightweight Architecture Search
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey, shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited | gen: 2fcb01ae1fab -->
Model compression makes deep networks deployable on resource-constrained end and edge nodes, and it also addresses the sustainability concern that AI/ML significantly increases power consumption in optical networks. Analysis of standard hyperparameter optimization (e.g., Bayesian) shows that the resulting networks often leave many neurons inactive, adding unnecessary complexity. Pruning and sparsification remove parameters: unstructured pruning deletes individual weights and biases and yields sparse matrices, while structured pruning removes whole filters or rows and so stays compatible with standard hardware. Pruning can be applied after training, through iterative prune-and-fine-tune cycles, or at initialization, where the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis holds that a dense network contains a sparse 'winning' sub-network that can be located with masks and trained to comparable accuracy. Knowledge distillation transfers knowledge from a complex 'teacher' model to a leaner 'student' model; applied together with pruning to ML models for optical networking, it can reduce complexity by approximately 90% without significant accuracy loss. Parameter sharing maps multiple parameters to one shared value using k-means clustering or hash functions. Quantization lowers numeric precision (e.g., 32-bit floating point to 8-bit integer or binary), cutting model size substantially at the possible cost of accuracy. Manual design relies on experts to build lightweight modules, such as the depthwise separable convolutions in MobileNet. Neural architecture search (NAS) automates the discovery of architectures, either indirectly by tuning an existing framework or directly by exploring the search space from scratch, and remains computationally expensive. Compression is typically combined with partitioning, and future directions include self-adaptive schemes that choose compression ratios from real-time hardware and task conditions rather than static thresholds.

### Transfer Learning and Knowledge Distillation
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: 23a88c60c894 -->
Knowledge transfer lets models at different tiers update one another without exchanging raw data. The transferable knowledge types include instance, data representation, relational (logical rules), feature (output or intermediate), model relational, and structured knowledge such as parameters and hyperparameters. Transfer learning moves knowledge from a source domain to a target domain, either data-based (instance or representation transfer) or model-based (transfer of structured parameters), with variants such as meta-learning and multimodal transfer. Knowledge distillation (KD) has a teacher model guide a student, which in the end-edge-cloud setting typically means a large cloud teacher supervising a compact edge or end student. Extensions include multi-teacher learning, knowledge amalgamation for combining several tasks, and 'teacher assistants', medium-sized models that bridge the capacity gap between cloud teachers and small end-device students. Federated KD replaces the exchange of heavy parameter sets with soft labels or logits, improving communication efficiency. A recognized open problem is the lack of a unified theoretical framework for knowledge migration, along with the risk of negative transfer, in which transferred knowledge degrades the target model's performance.

### Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: 4b95757eeea8 -->
Adapting massive foundation models (e.g., GPT-3, PaLM) without full retraining is necessary because their size exceeds edge and end-device resources. Parameter-efficient tuning updates only a small subset of parameters and takes three main forms: Adapters (small inserted modules), Prompt-tuning (learned prompts or soft tokens) and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which trains low-rank update matrices. LoRA is highlighted as advantageous because its updates can be merged into the base weights, so it adds no inference latency, unlike adapters. Resource-efficient tuning targets memory footprint during fine-tuning through quantization, as in QLoRA, which combines LoRA with a quantized base model, and through gradient-related optimizations. Together these methods make it feasible to personalize foundation models at the edge and to reduce the volume of parameters exchanged between tiers during collaborative updating.

### Federated Continual Learning
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: 56b31ff4a56b -->
In the end-edge-cloud setting, model updating is distinguished from ordinary training by its emphasis on lifelong learning: models must adapt to new data and domains over time without suffering catastrophic forgetting, where new data causes the loss of previously acquired knowledge. Updating relies on multi-model collaboration through knowledge transfer, using transfer learning and knowledge distillation to achieve domain adaptation. Federated continual learning (FCL) combines FL with continual learning so that multiple clients learn tasks sequentially over time while retaining earlier ones. Approaches rely on parameter isolation, which dedicates or protects parameter subsets for particular tasks, or on distillation, which preserves earlier behavior by having the previous model or a teacher supervise the updated one. Because edge nodes can act on locally collected data and the cloud holds more capacity, updating can be split across tiers, easing the instability of cloud-only lifelong adaptation during network interruptions.

### Security, Privacy, and IP Protection in Collaborative Learning
<!-- sources: end-edge-cloud_collaborative_computing_for_deep_learning_a_comprehensive_survey, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges, zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation, ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions, ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond, explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research, leveraging_federated_learning_for_optimizing_fiber_optic_sensor_design_for_biodetection | gen: 17ce1154e5c7 -->
The distributed nature of end-edge-cloud deep learning enlarges the attack surface relative to a single-site deployment, since data, gradients, intermediate features and model parts move across devices and tiers, and it creates a trade-off between data availability for learning and data protection, addressed with techniques such as encryption and blockchain. Sharing gradients, feature maps or knowledge instead of raw data reduces exposure but does not eliminate it. Model intellectual property deployed or partitioned across edge and end nodes risks theft, with backdoor watermarking proposed to establish ownership, and collaborative mechanisms bring new attack vectors such as 'early exit attacks', in which inputs are crafted to defeat the confidence-based exit logic of branched models and disrupt progressive inference. Edge nodes are more exposed to physical attacks (for example on cooling systems) than centralized data centers, yet keeping sensitive data local enhances privacy relative to cloud processing; future work calls for robust encryption and real-time threat detection across the distributed edge perimeter. In the wider 6G context, AI-driven networks are vulnerable to data poisoning and adversarial attacks, ISAC introduces privacy risks by exposing environmental and user data through sensing, and network digital twins add privacy risks through the continuous synchronization of massive amounts of user data into the twin, while their AI models are likewise exposed to adversarial attacks and data poisoning. Cryptography faces a double-sided quantum issue: quantum key distribution (QKD) offers security, while quantum computing threatens existing cryptographic standards. A proposed direction combines physical layer security via reconfigurable intelligent surfaces with federated learning, which trains AI models on devices without sharing raw data and so preserves privacy, and which is also suggested for sharing insights across network segments and complementing techniques such as differential privacy. In zero-touch 5G+ networks, the attack surface spans five domains: user equipment (botnets), the RAN (rogue base stations, man-in-the-middle), the core (SDN/VNF hijacking), slicing (isolation breaches) and SDN (topology poisoning). Automation adds threats of its own: open APIs are exposed to SQL injection and DoS, intents can be intercepted (data exposure), closed loops can be manipulated through deception attacks, and ML models face adversarial, model-extraction and model-inversion attacks. Trust enablers include blockchain, which keeps tamper-evident logs to ensure data integrity in ML pipelines; Manufacturer Usage Description (MUD), which defines normal device behavior, together with Trust and Reputation Managers (TRM) that audit infrastructure; federated learning, which allows anomaly detection without centralizing sensitive raw data; and trusted execution environments with robust encryption for sharing topology data between operators. AutoML models are additionally vulnerable to adversarial noise injection, with defensive distillation and adversarial training proposed as defenses.

Differential privacy (DP) adds controlled noise during data collection, aggregation at the edge or computation in the cloud so that individual records cannot be re-identified. In AI-enabled edge networks, local differential privacy (LDP) with Laplace noise is applied in A3C-based RL training to prevent strategy leakage; DP-DQN masks user usage patterns in offloading from 'honest but curious' MEC servers; LDP protects content preferences in caching; and synthetic trajectory generation protects user movement in mobility management. Future work targets dynamic privacy-budget management, adjusting noise levels to the requirements of the analysis, and extending DP to complex data types such as graphs and images. In multi-vendor O-RAN, where data sharing is limited, XAI models can themselves leak private information through their explanations, so differential privacy or homomorphic encryption is needed alongside federated learning when distributing explainable models. Outside the network domain, DP is one of the options (alongside weighted averaging and secure multiparty computation) for aggregating locally trained Gaussian Process Regressor parameters across distributed medical biosensor nodes, protecting sensitive patient-related data while a global sensor-design model is built. Security and privacy are therefore listed among the main open challenges alongside communication bottlenecks, self-adaptive compression and partitioning, and effective knowledge transfer.

## Reinforcement Learning for Network Control

### Deep Reinforcement Learning for Network and System Optimization
<!-- sources: a_comprehensive_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications, ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions, ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, deep_reinforcement_learning_for_resource_allocation_in_multi-band_optical_networks, enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research, joint_service_migration_and_resource_allocation_in_edge_iot_system_based_on_deep_reinforcement_learning, leveraging_federated_learning_for_optimizing_fiber_optic_sensor_design_for_biodetection, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks, zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation | gen: 095f2c5dd500 -->
Reinforcement learning (RL) optimizes decisions by trial and error against a reward function, and deep RL (DRL) uses neural networks to handle large state spaces in dynamic environments. In optical networks, policy-based RL optimizes routing and wavelength assignment (RWA), reducing packet loss by 88% during failures in transparent networks and blocking probability in OBS networks by up to 90%, and drives iterative indoor positioning (5 cm mean absolute error) and adaptive bandwidth/power allocation in multi-user VLC. Value-based RL (Q-learning) selects paths and wavelengths in OBS with O(n) complexity versus shortest-path or dynamic routing, and allocates power in SDM-FSO and hybrid LiFi-WiFi networks, lowering delay and blocking relative to static heuristics. DRL optimizes survivable routing, modulation and spectrum assignment (RMSA) in elastic optical networks, with blocking probability reduced by up to 77%; schedules flows in hybrid optical-electrical networks; selects relays in cooperative FSO; allocates power in hybrid RF/VLC networks (outperforming genetic algorithms); and manages handover in 6G hybrid architectures. In optical-fibre network planning, DRL supports proactive resource reallocation in response to real-time traffic spikes, alongside spectrum defragmentation, which consolidates fragmented wavelength slots to free contiguous spectrum. At the physical layer, DRL finds optimal Volterra equalizer structures more efficiently than exhaustive or greedy search, lowering complexity with no BER penalty. RL's potential for fully autonomous network control and real-time adaptation of modulation and power is noted as under-explored. For dynamic resource allocation (DRA), RL is attractive because the problems involve large combinatorial search spaces and objectives that can be optimized through simulation, promising near-optimal results at heuristic speed. Method families differ: action-value methods such as Q-learning estimate the value of actions in given states and are used mainly for route selection, while policy-gradient methods (A2C, PPO) optimize policy parameters directly and can handle continuous action spaces, with actor-critic architectures using a learned value function (the critic) to reduce variance. In traffic-driven (prescriptive) service provisioning, RL learns a policy rather than forecasting traffic; it is model-free and adapts as the environment changes. The problem is modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP) with states S, actions A, transition dynamics T, reward R, starting states and discount factor γ, and the agent maximizes cumulative discounted reward. Value-based, policy-based and actor-critic algorithms are used, with DRL actor-critic models preferred for the complex states of optical networks. Because of the curse of dimensionality, state and action spaces must be abstracted (for example path-level rather than link-level representations), and rewards must be scaled so that the agent converges toward the intended objective, such as minimizing blocking probability. Success is judged by convergence of cumulative reward over episodes, blocking reduction against baselines and training time. Given the dimensionality of optical networks, many frameworks apply RL only to sub-problems such as routing and leave spectrum allocation to heuristics. In proactive networks, RL agents suggest off-line re-optimization actions, and limited evidence indicates that they balance over- and under-provisioning better than predictive models because they observe the actual QoS impact of their actions. In adaptive single-domain networks, DeepRMSA improves blocking probability but needs millions of episodes to converge, and transfer learning (TL) can cut this to thousands. In multi-layer networks, agents working on logical topologies or auxiliary graphs (ADMIRE) converge faster and perform better than link-level agents. In multi-domain networks, information sharing between DRL agents (DeepCoop) is essential for convergence. For VNF-SC, hierarchical GNN-based DRL optimizes resource utilization and blocking jointly better than non-hierarchical DNNs. Imitation learning from baseline heuristics to seed agents is a promising way to speed up training, and offline-to-online pipelines with HITL mechanisms aim to avoid disastrous trial-and-error actions in live networks. A survey of 97 peer-reviewed papers, grouped into RWA, RSA, RMSA and other topics (traffic grooming, survivability, multicast provisioning), found that DeepRMSA set a de facto standard for problem definitions. Five influential works built on it: DeepRMSA (a neural network picks among K-shortest paths with first-fit spectrum allocation), Reward-RMSA (adds fragmentation information to the reward), GCN-RMSA (graph convolutional networks and RNNs for network-state features), MaskRSA (invalid action masking to choose among all available slots on K paths) and PtrNet-RSA (pointer networks select path nodes, removing the precomputed K-shortest-path restriction). When their problem settings were recreated and compared with tuned heuristics (KSP-FF or FF-KSP with K=50 and hop-count ordering), simple heuristics matched or outperformed the published RL results in nearly all cases, in many cases by more than an order of magnitude in blocking, indicating that earlier claims of RL superiority rested on weak baselines and, in some codebases, on undocumented lighter traffic loads. A remaining gap of 19% to 36% in supportable load relative to empirical lower bounds suggests room for RL, notably in multi-objective settings where simple heuristics are less effective. In multi-band (MB) optical networks, earlier RL attempts for routing and spectrum assignment (RSA) failed to outperform heuristics; a PPO-based DRL-RSA agent addresses this by observing the available spectrum across all precomputed paths and bands for each connection request and choosing an action tuple (path, band, channel). Its resource-aware reward gives 1 + 1/(α×β) for a successful establishment (α = allocated channels, β = hops), favoring short paths and few spectral resources, and -1 for a blocked request. Training is offline on a digital twin of the network across various traffic loads, so the agent adapts to dynamic requests before deployment in a real network. On the 14-node, 44-link Japanese topology at 200-450 Erlang, DRL-RSA reduces blocking probability by about 80% versus k-SP First-Fit and about 50% versus a standard RL-RSA at medium to high loads (3.1×10⁻² versus 16.5×10⁻² and 7.7×10⁻² at 450 Erlang), raising throughput by 50% and 20% respectively; k-SP First-Fit is slightly more efficient only at very low loads. The same toolbox extends to edge/cloud settings, where DRL is favored over traditional optimization because it needs no accurate environment model, handles high-dimensional dynamic states through iterative interaction, and optimizes long-term rather than one-shot performance. Single-agent problems are formalized as a Markov decision process (MDP), the tuple ⟨S, A, R, T, γ⟩, in which the agent maximizes discounted cumulative reward under its policy; when the full state is not visible (e.g., an edge server sees only its connected users) a partially observable MDP (POMDP) adds observations O and observation probabilities Z. Algorithms used include DQN (replay buffer and target network for stability), DDQN (decouples action selection from evaluation to reduce Q-value overestimation), actor-critic (jointly learns a policy and a value function), DDPG (extends DQN to continuous action spaces) and PPO (a policy-gradient optimizer). These are applied to offloading, caching and mobility management, where they typically address mixed discrete-continuous, non-convex problems that convex solvers handle too slowly. A concrete hybrid-action case is joint service migration and resource allocation in edge IoT, cast as an MDP whose state holds the LSTM-predicted user location, the nearest target ES, resources currently allocated by the source ES and resources available at the target ES; the action combines a discrete migration decision with continuous CPU and bandwidth allocations; and the reward is the negative expected sum of task processing delays of all users. Parameterized DQN (PDQN) is used because DQN cannot output continuous actions and DDPG handles discrete ones poorly; baselines relying on DQN with discretization or DDPG with rounding of continuous outputs suffer performance degradation, and PDQN converged to a lower minimum average delay than both. Discount factors of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 and 0.9 all converged but differed in speed and quality of convergence. In AI-native network DTs, DRL is coupled with simulators such as NS-3: in the Infrastructure DT, agents interact with the simulated environment to optimize operations and generate policies that are validated in an internal simulator before deployment, and in the Slice DT, DRL refines slicing policies via simulation and validates them in a slice-level simulator. In a multicast short video streaming case study, an autoencoder-based Double Deep Q Network (DDQN) combined with K-means++ clustering abstracts user behavior patterns and recommended video lists for multicast groups, while an LSTM predicts user status. In RAN control, most functions (user access control, slicing, function placement, energy scaling) are likewise formulated as MDPs solved by RL/DRL, and they face an accuracy-interpretability trade-off: as the number of features and states grows, the models become opaque, for example leaving unexplained why a DQN gives certain UEs specific radio blocks despite different service requirements. XAI for RL is divided into reactive XAI, which gives immediate explanations through policy simplification (decision trees), reward decomposition or feature-contribution methods (LIME/SHAP), and proactive XAI, which offers longer-term explainability through structural causal models, consequence-based explanations, hierarchical policies and relational RL. Examples include EXPLORA, which uses attributed graphs to explain DRL agent behavior, and STEP, which applies an information bottleneck for post-hoc explainability in slicing. In zero-touch 5G+ management, DRL is applied to radio resource allocation (for example in WLANs) and VNF placement, and agents can be trained on network digital twins to avoid risky configurations on live networks; for uRLLC services, DRL reduced end-to-end latency and SLA violations compared with baseline rejection-based methods. In intelligent routing, the EARS architecture uses DDPG to optimize throughput and delay in SDN, and DQN is used for vertical routing in FANETs (drone networks) to improve energy efficiency and reduce link breakages. For energy-aware deployment, SCHE2MA applies distributed RL to service function chain deployment, lowering latency while improving energy efficiency by 17.1% over centralized solutions, and KB5G uses actor-critic methods to minimize VNF instantiation cost and energy consumption. Beyond networking, integrating federated learning with reinforcement learning and evolutionary algorithms has been named as a future direction for further refining the performance of plasmonic fiber optic biosensors designed with federated Gaussian Process Regressor models.

### Multi-Agent Deep RL
<!-- sources: ai-enhanced_cloud-edge-terminal_collaborative_network_survey_applications_and_future_directions, ai-native_network_digital_twin_for_intelligent_network_management_in_6g, joint_service_migration_and_resource_allocation_in_edge_iot_system_based_on_deep_reinforcement_learning, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: 0d1f8ae0d762 -->
Multi-agent DRL (MADRL) extends single-agent RL to settings where several decision makers, such as edge servers, vehicles, UAVs or satellites, act concurrently in a shared environment. The Markov game generalizes the MDP to multiple players whose rewards depend on joint actions, with the solution concept often being a Nash equilibrium, in which no agent can improve its reward by unilaterally changing its policy. The Dec-POMDP models cooperative agents that act on local observations to maximize a shared long-term reward. The dominant training scheme is centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE), which lets different vendors or nodes decide optimally from local observations at run time while using global information during training. MADDPG extends DDPG with centralized critics to stabilize the otherwise non-stationary environment; COMA uses a counterfactual baseline to address credit assignment among cooperating agents; and mean-field RL (MF-Q, MF-DDPG) improves scalability by approximating the interaction among many agents as that between one agent and the average effect of its neighbors. In edge networks, MARL is used to manage large user or satellite populations where coordination avoids congestion, in offloading, caching and V2V collaboration. In multi-domain optical networks, information sharing between cooperating DRL agents (DeepCoop) is reported as essential for convergence, since each domain agent sees only part of the network. Future work points toward truly cooperative multi-agent RL grounded in game theory, in place of loosely coupled agents that merely exchange information. A single-agent, controller-based alternative serves as a contrast: in edge IoT service migration and resource allocation, a cloud server acts as the centralized controller and decision maker, observing the state of users and base-station-integrated edge servers and issuing migration and CPU/bandwidth allocation decisions for all users through one DRL agent (LSTM plus PDQN). This avoids inter-agent coordination and non-stationarity, with time complexity O(z·w_full + z·w_lstm) in the number of iterations z, but it relies on global state visibility at the controller. In network DT research, multi-agent systems (MAS) are listed as an open direction in which individual DTs (User, Infrastructure, Slice) negotiate and coordinate their decisions, and are paired with federated learning to share insights across network segments. Open limitations include dimensionality explosion of joint action spaces in ultra-dense networks (addressed with MARL and game theory), unreliable wireless links that prevent agents from synchronizing global state in real time, conflicting objectives that call for mixed reward functions (e.g., user QoS versus provider revenue), the mean-field assumption of isomorphic players that fails for devices of very different capacity, and a lack of formal convergence proofs, with most evidence coming from simulation. Quantum machine learning is mentioned as a possible accelerator for the heavy learning phase.

### Parameterized Deep Q-Network (PDQN) for Hybrid Discrete-Continuous Actions
<!-- sources: joint_service_migration_and_resource_allocation_in_edge_iot_system_based_on_deep_reinforcement_learning | gen: 7863897871b7 -->
Parameterized Deep Q-Networks (PDQN) address reinforcement learning problems whose action space is hybrid, combining a discrete choice with continuous parameters attached to each choice. Standard DQN handles only discrete actions, so continuous variables must be discretized, while DDPG handles continuous actions but struggles with discrete ones and typically requires rounding continuous outputs into discrete decisions, which can degrade performance. PDQN bridges the two with two networks: a Q-network that approximates the value function over discrete actions, and an x-network, a deterministic policy network that maps the state to continuous action parameters for every discrete action. In joint service migration and resource allocation for edge IoT, the discrete action is the migrate/non-migrate decision and the continuous parameters are the CPU and bandwidth allocated at the target edge server. Training follows a DQN-like loop: the agent observes the state, selects an action with an ε-greedy strategy over the PDQN outputs, and stores experience tuples in a replay buffer. The Q-network is updated by minimizing a least-mean-squares loss against the target value, and the x-network is updated to maximize the Q-value. Compared with DDPG with rounding and DQN with discretization, PDQN converged to a lower minimum average task delay and its latency grew more slowly as the number of users increased, while remaining consistently best across varying ES densities. The overall time complexity combines the fully connected layer cost and the LSTM cost, O(z·w_full + z·w_lstm), where z is the number of iterations.

### Machine Learning Paradigms and Deep Learning Architectures
<!-- sources: a_comprehensive_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications, ai-driven_converged_metro-access_optical_network-as-a-service_with_point-to-multipoint_coherent_optics_for_6g_x-hauling, toward_6g_an_overview_of_the_next_generation_of_intelligent_network_connectivity, dynamic_network-aware_soft_failure_localization_using_machine_learning_in_optical_networks | gen: ad175d93db3e -->
Machine learning (ML) in optical communications is organized by learning paradigm, with each classical algorithm mapped to tasks across optical fiber communication (OFC), optical wireless communication (OWC) and optical communication networking (OCN). The same paradigms apply in the 6G wireless context: supervised learning for tasks such as traffic prediction, unsupervised learning for clustering user or network behaviors, and reinforcement learning (RL) for dynamic resource allocation. Training may be offline (batch processing of collected data) or online (continuous incremental updates). Three methods are singled out for 6G: deep learning (DL), which extracts high-level features from raw data for complex routing and signal classification; federated learning (FL), in which only model updates rather than raw data are shared, enhancing privacy; and transfer learning (TL), which reuses knowledge from one task to another to reduce data and compute needs. Supervised learning uses labeled data for prediction and classification. Support vector machines (SVM) find margin-maximizing hyperplanes, improve 16-QAM detector sensitivity by 1.2-1.8 dB and enable QoT estimation with up to 99.95% accuracy. Artificial neural networks (ANN, including RBFNN and MLP variants) model nonlinear relationships for equalization, OSNR/CD/PMD estimation from asynchronous amplitude histograms and amplifier gain optimization. k-nearest neighbors (kNN) handles nonlinear phase noise, I/Q imbalance, modulation format identification (MFI, 100% accuracy in OFDM) and VLC fingerprint positioning (~2.7 cm). Ensemble methods (Random Forest, XGBoost) predict RSSI in hybrid FSO/RF links and perform MFI/QoT prediction with 4.6x lower complexity than DNNs. Regression (linear, ridge, Lasso, kernel ridge) supports impairment compensation, VLC positioning (linear regression cuts errors ~90%, KRR a further ~48%) and fault localization. In a supervised-regression comparison for BER prediction in optical transport, Linear Regression, Decision Trees and Random Forest are contrasted using the coefficient of determination (R^2); Random Forest is selected because its ensemble structure captures non-linear relationships between path features and BER while reducing the overfitting of single trees, yielding the highest accuracy. Evaluation covers scalability (training on 1 DU, testing on 5 DUs), cross-topology generalization (train on one network, test on another), training-set size (10% to 100%) and robustness to additive Gaussian noise.

Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabeled data. K-means (minimizing within-cluster variance) tracks signal variation in Nyquist-WDM, recovers RF phase in radio-over-fiber, cuts PON deployment cost by 50% and flags malicious nodes in OBS; hierarchical clustering groups signals with similar distortion and builds FSO MANET routing; expectation maximization (EM) models data as probability mixtures for laser phase/amplitude noise (MSE up to 20 dB better than conventional methods, ten times faster than Wiener filtering), pilotless channel estimation in FSO/VLC and OCDMA multi-user detection within 2 dB of optimal; independent component analysis (ICA) performs blind equalization that outperforms the constant modulus algorithm (CMA) for 16-QAM and is robust to PMD/PDL; principal component analysis (PCA) reduces dimensionality for OSNR/CD monitoring and fiber intrusion detection (99% detection, 0% false positives). RL learns decisions through environment interaction: policy-based methods optimize RWA (packet loss down 88% during failures; OBS blocking down up to 90%) and indoor positioning (5 cm MAE), while value-based Q-learning handles power allocation in SDM-FSO and LiFi-WiFi networks and OBS path selection at O(n) complexity. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) complements these for decision-making. DL uses multi-layer networks suited to different data structures. Deep neural networks (DNNs) learn general mappings to compensate linear and nonlinear fiber impairments and mitigate nonlinear interference (NLI) at lower complexity than digital backpropagation (DBP); in mode-division multiplexing (MDM), DNN detectors outperform MIMO zero-forcing; in networks they serve MFI, OSNR monitoring and QoT estimation, and DNN-based RSA in elastic optical networks (EONs) reduces spectrum fragmentation by 20% and latency by 15%; in FSO and underwater VLC they act as detectors, constellation shapers and post-equalizers that beat LMS and Volterra equalizers. Recurrent networks (RNN, LSTM, GRU, bidirectional variants) exploit temporal dependencies: as nonlinear equalizers in PAM4 they reduce BER by 20% and complexity by 50%, cascade DNN-RNN structures cut BER by up to 100x relative to non-cascade models, GRUs predict FSO channel state from weather data with <6.9% error, and LSTMs mitigate ISI and flicker in VLC (attention improves BER over long delays) and monitor OSNR with 0.18 dB MAE or forecast traffic in multi-core-fiber EONs. Convolutional networks (CNN) extract spatial features: they reconstruct OAM modal coefficients faster than iterative Gerchberg-Saxton, demodulate OAM modes under strong turbulence with >99% accuracy, perform wavefront correction in FSO, improve VLC positioning by 50% via RSS pre-processing, estimate EVM in <100 microseconds, run joint MFI/OSNR monitoring at >99% accuracy and fingerprint ONUs for security (99.25%). Graph neural networks (GNNs) handle graph-structured network data, where nodes are devices and edges are links: a graph attention layer weights neighbor contributions by learned importance, and stacking it with convolution layers for spatial features and LSTM layers for temporal patterns yields a model that localizes soft failures across the topology (97.6% accuracy, about 15 ms processing time in one reported design). The general trade-off is lower complexity and data needs for classical ML versus higher accuracy, at greater computational and data cost, for DL.

### Failure Detection, Identification, and Localization (Hard vs. Soft Failures)
<!-- sources: dynamic_network-aware_soft_failure_localization_using_machine_learning_in_optical_networks, shaping_the_future_of_optical_networks_by_integrating_sdn_telemetry_and_ai_invited | gen: 3fb665eb4479 -->
Soft failures (SFs) are gradual degradations of quality of transmission (QoT) that do not trigger hard alarms, so they are hard to catch with vendor-defined global thresholds. Typical causes include launch power degradation at terminals, booster amplifier attenuation, amplifier gain degradation and WSS attenuation. In partially disaggregated optical transport networks, where OLS domain controllers are managed by a hierarchical SDN controller, multi-vendor equipment and dynamic reconfigurations (spectrum defragmentation, node additions) complicate monitoring, and the management plane has limited visibility into data-plane performance monitoring (PM) data. Detection is complicated further by KPIs with differing data distributions and by the absence of labeled training sets in dynamic networks; real-world data are also strongly imbalanced, since failures are rare, and data augmentation has been suggested to compensate. Because a fault in one component produces sequential deviations across downstream nodes, finding the root cause requires modeling relationships between nodes, which network dynamicity makes harder. A common design is a two-step pipeline: first, distributed per-device anomaly detection on PM data yields a one-hot vector describing the status of each device's KPIs; second, a network-wide model uses these vectors together with the topology to classify the failure type and localize its origin. Many existing GNN-based localization methods assume a fixed topology or react to alarms rather than proactively monitoring performance. In a testbed with two OLS domains in a ring topology, this pipeline detected gradual KPI deviations of about 0.3 dBm/dB and localized failures with 97.6% accuracy. In-band telemetry in programmable packet-optical nodes can additionally expose soft failures almost instantly, and ML-based alarm clustering is used to suppress redundant alarms so that operators handle fewer correlated events. Telemetry-driven prediction and localization of soft failures feeds the closed-loop assurance of intent-based systems, which can trigger rerouting before service levels are violated.

### Graph Neural Networks for Fault Localization
<!-- sources: dynamic_network-aware_soft_failure_localization_using_machine_learning_in_optical_networks, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: e88ea2c44211 -->
Graph neural networks (GNNs) model the network as a graph so that failure localization can exploit relationships between nodes, since a fault at one component causes sequential deviations at others. Two variants are compared for soft-failure localization. GNN-GAT (Graph Attention Network) uses an attention mechanism to aggregate data from neighboring nodes, weighting each neighbor by a learned importance coefficient (alpha_ij); node features are then max-pooled and passed to a fully connected neural network (FCNN). Its limitation is reliance on a fixed adjacency matrix, so topology changes require expensive retraining. GNN-SAT (Sample, Aggregate, and Temporal) is inductive: a node-centric attention mechanism samples only nodes exhibiting anomalies and their immediate neighbors, building a standard-size input from the current adjacency matrix, which makes the model topology-aware in real time. Convolution layers extract spatial features and LSTM layers process temporal patterns preceding a failure, and the LSTM output feeds an FCNN that produces the SF label distribution. In evaluation, GNN-SAT reached 97.6% accuracy versus about 84% for GNN-GAT, stayed accurate when virtual nodes were added without retraining, and could anticipate SFs before they fully manifested thanks to the temporal module. Because it processes only affected regions rather than the whole graph, its processing time was about 15.28 ms versus about 57 ms for GNN-GAT. The same topology awareness is used in traffic-driven provisioning. For link load prediction, graph-based models such as GCN-GAN and hybrid diffusion convolutional recurrent networks (DCRNN) are more effective than LSTMs, because they capture the spatial dependencies imposed by the network topology alongside temporal trends, which a purely sequential model cannot represent. In prescriptive provisioning of virtual network function service chains (VNF-SC), hierarchical GNN-based DRL optimizes resource utilization and blocking probability jointly more effectively than non-hierarchical DNN agents.

### Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Hybrid DSP-AI

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and hybrid DSP-plus-AI designs are proposed as future directions for optical communications. These directions sit alongside other proposed priorities: reinforcement learning for real-time adaptation of modulation and power, transfer and semi-supervised learning for data efficiency, end-to-end learning via deep autoencoders, and real-world case studies with standardized benchmarks.

### Challenges of Deploying AI in Optical Communications
<!-- sources: a_comprehensive_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications, causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks, enabling_optical_fronthaul_and_technologies_for_6g, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: 6c060ea085a2 -->
Challenges for ML/DL in optical communications fall into four categories. Data: scarcity of high-quality labeled data, especially for rare failure events, leads to overfitting. Modeling: difficulty selecting optimal models and the lack of interpretability of deep networks as black boxes, together with vulnerability to adversarial data poisoning. Resources: high computational and memory demands for training and deploying deep models, with added energy overhead and a persistent trade-off between accuracy (highest for DL) and cost that matters for real-time operation. Integration: incompatibility with legacy optical hardware architectures. Identified research gaps include a shortage of large-scale real-world implementations relative to laboratory studies, little work on the long-term stability of AI models in live networks, limited exploitation of RL for autonomous network control, and the absence of standardized benchmarks. Proposed responses are physics-informed neural networks, hybrid DSP-AI models, transfer and semi-supervised learning for data efficiency, real-world case studies and benchmarks, and end-to-end learning. For fronthaul specifically, explainable AI (XAI) is proposed for transparency, federated learning for privacy and on-device distributed training, and digital twins to validate AI decisions in simulation before physical deployment; future work is directed toward explainable models and cross-layer optimization spanning the physical and orchestration layers. Similar limits are described for statistical AI in 6G more broadly, where six challenges are listed: dynamic adaptability, time criticality, intent management, resilience, nonlinear signal dynamics, and human-level reasoning. They are compounded by large dataset requirements, high retraining overhead in volatile environments, black-box opacity, and the energy cost of large foundation models; causal approaches are proposed as a response. For ML-aided traffic-driven provisioning, further challenges are listed. Field trials at scale are lacking, since most results come from synthetic simulations rather than real, large, non-stationary networks. An automated ML lifecycle (monitoring, model adaptation) is needed because traffic is non-stationary. RL agents converge slowly (DeepRMSA needs millions of episodes), which is mitigated by imitation learning and transfer learning, and their trial-and-error exploration risks disastrous actions in live networks, motivating offline-to-online pipelines and human-in-the-loop (HITL) mechanisms. Tiny ML is proposed for edge deployment, federated learning for privacy and reduced communication overhead, and explainable AI for operator trust. Adversarial ML attacks that manipulate traffic data could cause outages or QoS violations, so defenses are needed. Uncertainty representation (Bayesian deep learning), multi-step and disaggregated traffic prediction remain open, and low prediction accuracy can make predictive schemes perform worse than non-predictive ones.

## Explainable AI (XAI) for Network Systems

### XAI Fundamentals and Taxonomy
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research | gen: d9f6a4cacb47 -->
eXplainable AI (XAI) comprises tools that let humans interpret and trust AI results through objective metrics, turning closed-box models into 'glass-box' ones that reveal how input features contribute to decisions; it is also motivated by regulation such as the EU AI Act, which mandates transparency. Explainability refers to understanding a model's internal logic and mechanisms, whereas interpretability is the ability of a human to consistently predict the output for given inputs. XAI techniques are categorized along three axes. By model transparency, interpretable models such as decision trees are inherently transparent, while complex models such as deep neural networks (DNNs) require post-hoc explainability, for example through surrogate models. By model agnosticity, model-agnostic methods treat the AI as an opaque entity and analyze input-output relationships without knowledge of its internal structure. By explanation method, the families are simplification (approximating a complex model), feature relevance (quantifying input impact), local explanations (focused on a single prediction), visual explanations (heatmaps, graphs) and text explanations (natural language). Representative algorithms include SHAP, which applies Shapley values from game theory to obtain additive feature importance; DeepLIFT, which compares neuron activations for an input against a reference baseline; LIME, which approximates a closed-box model with local linear surrogates; RuleFit, which combines decision trees and sparse linear models; Integrated Gradients (IG), which accumulates gradients along a path from a baseline to the input; and GNN Explainer, which identifies critical nodes and edges in graph neural networks. For reinforcement learning (RL), specific approaches are reward shaping, attention mechanisms, structural causal models (SCM) built on directed acyclic graphs (DAG) and machine reasoning. Explanation needs depend on the user profile: data scientists need debugging tools, regulators need compliance evidence and auditors look for fairness and accountability. In telecom, bias denotes systematic errors caused by unbalanced training data (for example, over-representing SLA violations) or inappropriate feature selection (for example, proxies for sensitive attributes), which XAI helps expose.

### XAI Evaluation Metrics
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research | gen: c57c58113d7a -->
To move beyond subjective assessment, XAI methods are compared with objective metrics. Confidence or faithfulness is measured by feature mutation, replacing features with baseline values to check whether predictions remain stable. Log-odds (LO) is the average difference in negative log probabilities before and after masking the top-attributed features. Comprehensiveness measures the drop in predicted probability when the top features are removed, while sufficiency measures whether those features alone are adequate to maintain the prediction. Robustness or sensitivity is quantified with a Lipschitz constant λ, ensuring that small input perturbations do not radically change the explanation. Ambiguity is measured via entropy or KL divergence: importance concentrated on few features means low ambiguity, while importance spread uniformly means high ambiguity. Fidelity and soundness are expressed through recall (how well an explanation captures the truly relevant features) and precision (how well it excludes irrelevant ones). R-squared (R²) is used specifically for LIME to assess how well the local surrogate fits the global model. Relative consistency (ReCo) checks whether different predictors yield similar explanations when their predictions agree. Applying these metrics to a neuro-symbolic (NeSy) model for CPU usage prediction in O-RAN, SHAP provides high accuracy and confidence but is computationally expensive because of its combinatorial complexity, whereas gradient-based methods are significantly faster and therefore better suited to real-time constraints.

### XAI Deployment Patterns in O-RAN
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research | gen: e7c1a3c67efe -->
Three deployment scenarios integrate XAI into AI-enabled O-RAN. In local interpretable AI deployment, raw data at the O-CU is processed by local dApps and corresponding XAI dApps; local models are transferred to the Near-RT RIC over E2 to derive generalized models, which feed back to the O-CU through the O1 interface. Techniques such as RuleFit generate decision rules that are transparent to different user profiles, such as developers and regulators. In explanation-guided deep reinforcement learning (DRL), SHAP importance values taken from the replay buffer are converted into a probability distribution with a softmax, and its entropy is computed. The inverse of this entropy acts as an "XAI reward" that is combined with the standard SLA reward, pushing the agent toward actions that are both high-performing and highly explainable (low uncertainty). In explanation-aided confident federated learning (FL), local training at O-CUs includes a run-time explanation check: an XAI xApp produces feature attributions and a "confidence mapper" converts them into a metric used as a constraint in the local optimizer. To preserve global model quality, the federation layer at the Non-RT RIC selects only the K O-CUs with the highest confidence scores for aggregation, filtering out erroneous local models, so that only insightful information reaches the federation layer.

XAI also turns closed-box O-RAN applications into diagnosable ones across several use cases. In quality of experience (QoE) optimization, attributions identify the network environment factors that lead to under-provisioning or "stall events" in data transmission, letting operators refine QoE assurance xApps more effectively than with closed-box ML. In traffic steering, attribution methods (SHAP, IG) give context for offloading decisions, and LIME helps engineers understand the consequences of DRL choices when steering traffic across radio technologies. In RAN slice SLA assurance, XAI provides root-cause analysis of violations by pointing out problematic network factors, and trustworthiness can itself become a measurable part of the SLA. In multi-vendor slices, where vO-DUs and vO-CUs from different vendors coexist under loose, moderate or tight coordination, XAI improves the transparency of the negotiation agents that manage shared resources. In resource allocation optimization, XAI guides RL agents toward "safe" decision processes. In user access control, XAI makes AI-based user association transparent, helping operators move beyond simple RSS-based schemes, which cause ping-pong effects, while keeping a clear understanding of why users are assigned to particular base stations. Related literature also uses SHAP to identify key KPIs for traffic classification, reducing monitoring overhead (33% data-rate reduction), and AIChronoLens to link temporal input properties to explanations for vBS traffic forecasting.

### MLOps/RLOps Maturity and Transparency Checks
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research | gen: 91ef9334157f -->
The proposal is to augment MLOps pipelines in O-RAN with a 'model transparency check' closed loop, mapped onto the three O-RAN control loops (O-DU at TTI scale, Near-RT RIC at 10 ms to 1 s, Non-RT RIC above 1 s). Three maturity levels are described. In the manual pipeline (no MLOps), data collection, training at the Non-RT RIC and deployment to the Near-RT RIC over the A1 interface are manual, which is fragile and fails to adapt to dynamic radio environments. In training pipeline automation, continuous training is triggered by new data profiles; a feature store centralizes feature management, ML metadata supports pipeline tracking, and updated AI and XAI models are deployed through the A1-P interface. In continuous integration and delivery (CI/CD) automation, the highest level, building, validating and deploying the training pipeline itself is fully automated. RLOps is mentioned as a lifecycle specific to RL agents, covering specification through production monitoring and safety.

### Security of O-RAN and Attacks on XAI
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research | gen: 00912317aaf3 -->
O-RAN's openness expands the attack surface through the O-Cloud, open-source code and poisoning attacks, and its distributed architecture complicates trust; zero-trust principles and mutual authentication are proposed, and XAI can be used to validate that security protocols operate as intended. The O-RAN Alliance WG11 has assessed the Non-RT RIC, O-Cloud and Near-RT RIC with respect to confidentiality, integrity and availability, finding a lack of 'security by design' in current specifications and increased exposure to multi-vendor risks. XAI helps detect malicious xApps and rApps by making their decision processes interpretable, and can reveal the root causes of attacks flagged by xApps; proposed solutions include PCA and clustering for data filtering in the AI/ML workflow module and post-hoc models that monitor the behavior of deployed components in the RICs. XcARet is an example of a transparent security architecture. XAI itself can be targeted: adversarial ML can corrupt both a model and its explanation; evasion or model-inversion attacks can exploit explanations to infer sensitive private data; and social engineering can manipulate human interpreters with persuasive but misleading explanations. Interpretable security monitoring and human-in-the-loop enforcement protocols are identified as needed for robust oversight.

### Open Challenges of XAI for 6G O-RAN
<!-- sources: explainable_ai_in_6g_o-ran_a_tutorial_and_survey_on_architecture_use_cases_challenges_and_future_research | gen: 89d7cde50cd2 -->
Several challenges remain. The explainability-performance trade-off pits high-performing complex models (DNNs) against simple interpretable ones; 'in-hoc' constrained optimization during training, rather than post-hoc explanation, is suggested to maintain confidence without sacrificing performance. Large language models (LLMs) could help tasks such as beamforming or power allocation, but their billion-parameter architectures make explainability extremely hard. Standardization is lacking: there are no common APIs or data formats for XAI tools across O-RAN components, which hinders interoperability, and building a global XAI model from vendor-specific local models over interfaces such as F1, Xn and X2 is difficult. Privacy is a concern in multi-vendor environments where data sharing is limited; federated learning helps, but XAI models may still leak private information, so differential privacy or homomorphic encryption is needed. The depth of the O-RAN software/hardware stack makes concise interpretation difficult, and model-agnostic approaches are proposed to cope. Real-time constraints make perturbation-based methods such as SHAP too slow for RAN control, whereas gradient-based methods are more viable because of lower latency. XAI must be human-centered, with different interfaces and levels of detail for data scientists, managers and regulators. Finally, security requires interpretable monitoring and human-in-the-loop enforcement.

## Causal Reasoning for AI-Native Networks

### Causal Reasoning Framework: Discovery, CRL, Inference
<!-- sources: causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks | gen: 34fb0798613b -->
Statistical AI in 6G faces six challenges: dynamic adaptability, time criticality, intent management, resilience, nonlinear signal dynamics, and human-level cognition/reasoning. Correlation-based models ($P(X,Y)$) need massive datasets, incur high retraining overhead in volatile conditions (e.g., sudden THz blockages that curve-fitting networks cannot track), behave as black boxes, and cannot combine experiences into novel deductions. A causal framework addresses these by modeling the system's 'physics' with directed graphs and comprises three building blocks. Causal discovery learns the causal structure from observed data, giving directionality and explanation rather than mere correlation. Causal representation learning (CRL) maps high-dimensional raw data into lower-dimensional causal variables. Causal inference uses the learned structure to predict the effects of interventions and counterfactuals. Structural causal models (SCMs) formalize this as endogenous variables (cause/effect), exogenous variables (noise), and structural functions by which a node's parents determine it. Three reasoning levels are distinguished: associational ($\mathcal{L}_1$, observing $X$ to update beliefs about $Y$, as in supervised learning); interventional ($\mathcal{L}_2$, the do-operator, e.g., 'what happens if the number of antennas is increased?'); and counterfactual ($\mathcal{L}_3$, imagined alternative pasts for root-cause analysis, e.g., 'would QoS have been better if an RIS were placed here?'). For explainability, causal graphs define the explanandum (the phenomenon explained) and the explanans (the sequence of causal relationships and intervened values), with Causal Bayesian Optimization (CBO) proposed as a computationally feasible way to find optimal intervention sets for resource allocation. For generalization, distribution generalization is defined as finding an approximate solution close to the minimax solution across all possible interventions on the wireless environment; causal ML is argued to reach this more efficiently than transfer or meta-learning by exploiting a single causal graph rather than massive random datasets. Energy efficiency follows from lighter models focused on essential causal factors, reduced retraining because causal relations persist across contexts, and causality-based semantic communication that sends only causal states. Applications of causal graphical models (CGMs) include THz beamforming (variational causal networks and Granger causality to model time-varying channels with a reduced state space), digital twins, training-data generation through causal generative models (interventions and counterfactuals to synthesize scenarios such as disasters or hardware faults, argued to be structurally more accurate than GANs), and resilience, framed as a detection-remediation-recovery loop in which a base station monitors the causal graph $\mathcal{G}$, predicts QoE violations, and solves a counterfactual optimization over observational and interventional data to select a remediation policy.

### Causal Reinforcement Learning and Causal Bandits
<!-- sources: causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks | gen: 33c70357a858 -->
Conventional reinforcement learning is passive and struggles in Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs), which characterize many wireless control problems. Causal RL instead treats actions as interventions on an SCM of the environment. By distilling learning histories from multiple environments into a sequence model through behavioral cloning, it is proposed to build causal foundation models that remain invariant to environment changes, reducing the need for retraining. For real-time decisions that must meet 6G synchronization demands, causal multi-armed bandits (MAB) extend traditional MABs by using an SCM to acquire knowledge about non-intervened variables, so that information from one intervention informs others. This yields distribution invariance and sample efficiency without extensive new training data.

### Intent-Based Wireless Networking with Causal Inference
<!-- sources: causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks, intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review | gen: 497b3d1b2c62 -->
Intent-based networks translate business goals into network configurations through a closed loop that spans three domains of the intent lifecycle: a user domain that recognizes and formulates intents, a processing domain that translates them into machine-readable configurations, and an implementation domain that deploys them and reports outcomes back. In the intent assurance step, measured KPIs are compared against the stated objectives; in the corrective-action step, deviations trigger adjustments, and the results returned to the processing domain close the loop so that configurations remain consistent with SLAs under changing network conditions. Intent translation itself ranges from NLP and RNN-based approaches to controlled NLP with limited vocabularies that scale better, and conflict resolution prevents contention for resources among concurrent intents. Causal inference supports this loop through SCM decomposition, which breaks a high-level intent into manageable subgoals distributed across different OSI layers. Layer-wise SCMs keep complexity tractable and are consistent with Open RAN architectures. The explicit causal structure enables deductive reasoning about which layer or variable to intervene on and supports autonomous self-reflection on whether actions achieved the intended outcome.

### Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) with Causal Methods
<!-- sources: causal_reasoning_charting_a_revolutionary_course_for_next-generation_ai-native_wireless_networks, edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond, toward_6g_evolution_three_enhancements_three_innovations_and_three_major_challenges | gen: 28abbeb59a6c -->
Integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) lets wireless signals simultaneously carry data and sense the environment; it is one of the three new 6G capabilities, fusing sensing and data transmission so that the network performs localization and channel estimation at the same time as communication, which improves beamforming and resource efficiency. One use is beam prediction through angle-of-arrival (AoA) estimation. In edge computing it supplies real-time context such as user location and mobility, which allows computational resources to be adjusted dynamically and saves energy by putting servers into low-power states during sensed inactivity. Purely data-driven AI performs poorly in ISAC because scatterers are mobile and sensing data volumes are large. Causal discovery addresses this in several ways. For target tracking, Granger causality models time-varying objects and improves beam alignment through nonlinear MMSE methods. For distributed sensing, causal reasoning games let nodes use SCMs to estimate other users' strategies and optimize resource usage with game-theoretic tools. For the nonlinearity of self-interference (SI) caused by hardware imperfections, causal discovery models the underlying physics of the SI, avoiding recurrent retraining. For waveform design, causal multi-objective RL (MO-RL) identifies the minimal interventions needed to balance conflicting goals such as radar sensing quality versus communication QoE, reaching near-Pareto-optimal solutions that standard MO-RL may miss. Sensing also carries a privacy risk, since it can expose environmental and user data.

## Network-Cloud/Edge Convergence and Virtualization

### Network Cloudification and Four-Layer Architectural Framework
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities | gen: f5c72dbef56a -->
Network-cloud/edge convergence is the merging of networking, traditionally focused on data communication, with cloud/edge computing, focused on processing and storage. In the converged state, dedicated network appliances are replaced by commodity servers hosting virtual functions, and cloud servers are used to host network functions. The motivation is a holistic view in which resource management is integrated across network-compute systems and service provisioning is unified, improving resource utilization, flexibility and overall service performance. A four-layer architectural framework has been proposed for such systems. The Infrastructure Layer comprises multiple administrative domains (admin-domains), each containing technical domains (tech-domains) for networking, computing or storage. The Virtualization Layer offers a common abstraction of heterogeneous resources through the IaaS model. The Virtual Function Layer hosts Virtual Network Functions (VNFs) and Virtual Compute Functions (VCFs). The Service Layer orchestrates VNFs and VCFs as components of composite services for multi-tenant users. Three capabilities are required for the framework to work: unified resource management across tech-domains, federated service management across admin-domains, and holistic life-cycle management of virtual functions and services.

### Network Function Virtualization (NFV) and MANO
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities, edge_computing_in_future_wireless_networks_a_comprehensive_evaluation_and_vision_for_6g_and_beyond, intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review | gen: cdc57d8cbfd9 -->
ETSI Network Function Virtualization (NFV) separates network functions from dedicated hardware and drives cloudification by abstracting the infrastructure through a virtualization layer, so that functions run as software (VNFs) on commodity servers. The Management and Orchestration (MANO) framework provides the control plane for this model: the Virtualized Infrastructure Manager (VIM) manages compute, storage and network resources of the infrastructure, while the NFV Orchestrator (NFVO) coordinates resources and VNFs to deliver end-to-end network services. Together they enable unified resource management and end-to-end service provisioning. Recent ETSI NFV updates add support for multi-admin-domain orchestration, container-based cloud-native VNFs and a micro-services architecture, moving NFV closer to cloud practice. NFV also serves as the shared basis for other convergence efforts: ETSI MEC integrates with it so that MEC applications and VNFs can share infrastructure and management tools, and IETF SFC builds on it as the service provisioning model that steers traffic through ordered functions. In edge network management, NFV is listed together with Software Defined Networking (SDN) and Cloud-RAN (C-RAN) as an enabling technology for monitoring and dynamically adjusting network states across scattered edge nodes. In intent-based management, SDN provides a programmable control plane for policy enforcement and NFV abstracts functions into VNFs, making service chaining and traffic steering more flexible than in physical-only networks. Orchestrators such as ONAP, OSM, ONOS and M-CORD receive service descriptors derived from intents, expressed in TOSCA or YANG, and the domain controllers (SDN, NFV MANO) translate them into device-specific configurations (XML, YAML) and deploy VNF graphs and Service Function Chains (SFCs). Current softwarized networks still rely largely on imperative policies, so mapping declarative parameters from multiple stakeholders into an autonomous VNF and SFC lifecycle remains an open challenge.

### XaaS Abstraction for Network Resources
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities | gen: 6e8ab83af66d -->
Cloudification applies the Everything-as-a-Service (XaaS) paradigm to networking, so that network resources become self-contained, abstracted components that can be offered on demand, as in VNFaaS (virtual network functions as a service) and VNaaS (virtual networks as a service). Across representative projects, the level of abstraction rises progressively: from low-level infrastructure abstraction (IaaS), to VNFaaS and Network Function-as-a-Service (NFaaS, introduced in T-NOVA, which uses a marketplace broker to select and compose VNF components into end-to-end services), to a generalized XaaS (adopted by CORD, where VNFs are deployed like any other cloud service), and finally to Slice-as-a-Service (SlaaS, introduced in 5GEx), which merges connectivity and compute into a single composite service. This progression turns networks into versatile platforms offering both computing and networking services, and supports a common abstraction layer over heterogeneous network-compute resources.

### Service Function Chaining and Multi-Domain Orchestration Standards
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities | gen: fec25ea46c0f -->
The IETF Service Function Chaining (SFC) architecture defines a service as an ordered sequence of service functions (SFs) through which traffic is steered. It complements NFV by supplying the service provisioning model, while NFV supplies the virtualized functions. Current developments include hierarchical SFC architectures for multi-domain orchestration and distributed discovery mechanisms suited to edge environments. The MEF Lifecycle Service Orchestration (LSO) framework addresses orchestration across different providers and tech-domains through a four-layer architecture: Business, Service Orchestration, Infrastructure Control and Element Control. Its inter-domain APIs specifically support the provisioning of composite network-cloud services spanning multiple administrative domains. Together, SFC and LSO cover the service-composition and cross-provider orchestration aspects that NFV MANO alone does not.

### 5G Network Slicing as End-to-End Multi-Domain Entities
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities, intent-driven_autonomous_network_and_service_management_in_future_cellular_networks_a_structured_literature_review, optical_access_networks_to_support_future_5g_and_6g_mobile_networks_invited, optical_networks_management_and_control_a_review_and_recent_challenges, zero-touch_networks_towards_next-generation_network_automation | gen: 5251b1716c43 -->
In 3GPP and 5G-PPP, 5G uses SDN and NFV to create network slices: multi-tenant virtual networks running on shared infrastructure. From a convergence perspective, slices are treated as end-to-end entities that span multiple tech-domains (network, compute, storage) and admin-domains, rather than being confined to the radio or core network. The goal is a common abstraction layer for all network-compute resources, which facilitates delivery of edge services within slices. Projects such as 5GEx (Slice-as-a-Service across providers) and 5G-Transformer (Vertical Slicer, Service Orchestrator and Mobile Transport and Computing Platform, with federation at resource and service levels to integrate MEC into 5G) build on this view. In the optical domain, slicing is enabled by disaggregation: a physical infrastructure is partitioned into multiple virtual networks through device hypervisors, which ensure performance and connectivity isolation by dedicating specific physical resources, such as media channels, to each slice. In fixed-mobile convergence, Open RAN and Cloud RAN allow a single physical access infrastructure to be partitioned into virtual segments tailored to bandwidth, latency and security requirements of residential, business or mobile services, with the OLT acting as a common aggregation point for fixed and mobile traffic and potentially evolving toward all-optical architectures. In intent-driven management, the GSMA Generic Slicing Template (GST) acts as a deployment-agnostic blueprint: the resource-facing communication service uses it to trigger instantiation of Network Slicing Instances (NSIs) in the network management layer regardless of the underlying infrastructure, with end-to-end SLAs decomposed into per-domain intent targets. Multi-domain slicing raises intent-specific difficulties: centralized orchestrators lack global context and may face conflicts between local domain permissions and overarching inter-domain intents, so distributed orchestration is required. In zero-touch management, further challenges are the growing orchestration complexity as slices scale and the need to guarantee isolation between them (isolation breaches are a listed security risk). Proposed solutions include RL-based demand prediction for dynamic slice resource allocation and HARNESS, a system that provides high availability for eMBB, uRLLC and mMTC slices by intelligently handling control-plane requests. In the 5G architecture used for throughput prediction, slice-related decisions flow from the Network Data Analytics Function (NWDAF) to the NSSF and NSSMF for resource allocation.

### Convergence Projects and Multi-Provider Federation
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities | gen: bfbb52b09e08 -->
Several research projects have explored network-cloud convergence. UNIFY unifies carrier networks and cloud systems with an Infrastructure Layer, Orchestration Layer and Service Layer, using hierarchical resource management through a global orchestrator, but is mostly limited to a single admin-domain. T-NOVA delivers composite services by managing VNFs over integrated infrastructures, introducing NFaaS and a marketplace broker for composing VNFs into end-to-end services. CORD re-architects central offices as data centers using SDN, NFV and cloud technologies (OpenStack, Kubernetes) under an XaaS paradigm; M-CORD extends it to cloud-native 5G RAN and core virtualization. 5G Exchange (5GEx) orchestrates resources across multiple providers with a decentralized cascade approach in which providers act as resellers, and introduces Slice-as-a-Service. 5G-Transformer turns mobile networks into SDN/NFV platforms for verticals and supports inter-provider federation at resource and service levels. Four trends emerge across them: (1) abstraction moves from IaaS toward VNFaaS, XaaS and SlaaS; (2) resource management relies consistently on hierarchical structures within single admin-domains; (3) federation shifts from centralized brokerage to decentralized service federation among providers; and (4) the scope of convergence expands from the network core and cloud data centers toward the edge and access networks.

### Core Challenges of Network-Cloud/Edge Convergence
<!-- sources: convergence_of_networking_and_cloud_edge_computing_status_challenges_and_opportunities | gen: cedef42ae3b3 -->
Four main challenges are identified. Resource and function heterogeneity: integrating diverse networking, compute and storage implementations into one abstraction layer is difficult, and VNFs have stricter latency and throughput requirements than ordinary cloud functions, complicating composite service provisioning across autonomous domains. System scalability: the shift to micro-services and containerization multiplies function components and communication overhead, straining architectures and control mechanisms. Provisioning agility and performance: highly dynamic infrastructure availability and user mobility demand sophisticated inter-domain federation, and end-to-end performance guarantees during resource mapping remain an open problem. 5G and edge integration: the complexity of 5G, with dense functions, ultra-low latency for vehicles and high positioning accuracy, creates hurdles for seamless edge integration. Research opportunities include architectural design that applies SDN principles to build programmable control platforms for infrastructure and service layers and compares federation structures (hierarchical vs. peer-to-peer); unified abstraction modeling, i.e., standardized multi-level virtualization models covering both functional and non-functional (performance) requirements while balancing detail against scalability; and composite service management through automated, intelligent inter-domain orchestration using game theory, control theory and machine learning. Cross-fertilization among network virtualization, cloud-native networking and micro-services architecture is regarded as essential, and the field is still considered to be in its infancy.
