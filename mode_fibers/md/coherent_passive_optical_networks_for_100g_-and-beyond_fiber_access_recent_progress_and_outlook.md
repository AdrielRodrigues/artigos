# Coherent Passive Optical Networks for 100G/-and-Beyond Fiber Access: Recent Progress and Outlook

Junwen Zhang and Zhensheng Jia

# Abstract

Coherent optics is considered a promising candidate for realizing single-wavelength passive optical networks (PONs) at 100 G/ and beyond. It has been a game changer for enabling ultra-highspeed data transmission in long-haul and metro networks. Interest in coherent optics is at an alltime high for application to short-reach access networks. The use of a local oscillator in coherent beating with received signals results in superior receiver sensitivity and thereby an extended power budget that supports high-capacity, longreach, and large-split-ratio access. It has also been demonstrated to be beneficial for flexible deployment and adaptive access by advanced modulation formats and powerful digital signal processing. In this article, we review use cases, challenges, and recent progress in high-speed coherent PONs for 100 G/ and beyond. We also provide technical insights into key enabling technologies in coherent PONs, including innovations for simplification and optimization of coherent optics and coherent upstream burst-mode detection. Finally, we discuss the outlook for coherent PONs toward realizing a rate-flexible and adaptive fiber access network. The architecture of a rate-flexible single-wavelength time-and-frequency-division multiplexing coherent PON based on digital subcarrier multiplexing technology is introduced. In addition, the adaptive fiber access network enabled by the adaptive modulation and coding scheme is also discussed.

# Introduction

The passive optical network (PON) is a point-to-multipoint (P2MP) broadband fiber access technology that has successfully been demonstrated as a low-cost and effective solution for the "last mile" connection between service providers and customers over all passive optical distribution networks (ODNs) [1, 2]. PONs have been deployed worldwide since the 1990s to support fiber-to-X (FTTX) technology, where X can represent home (FTTH), office (FTTO), building (FTTB), mobile cell (FTTC), and so on. So far, residential broadband access services have been the main PON applications. Advances in the high-speed optical access network have always been propelled by novel business and application drivers [2]. To meet rising bandwidth demands, several generations of PON systems have been standardized through the efforts of two major industry standardization organizations: the International Telecommunication Union Telecommunication Standardization Sector (ITU-T)/Full Service Access Network (FSAN) Group and the IEEE 802.3 Ethernet Working Group. Two major types of PON standards, namely, GPON and Ethernet PON (EPON), have been developed by ITU-T/FSAN and IEEE 802.3, respectively. Figure 1 illustrates the evolution in PON standards in terms of the maximum provided bandwidth from broadband PON (B-PON) to different generations of PON systems. The figure shows that over the last few decades, the PON access speed over a single wavelength has increased by almost 100 times from hundreds of megabits per seconds per wavelength to the current speed of 25/50 Gb/s per wavelength [3].

Driven by emerging services, such as 5G mobile Internet, cloud networking, and high-definition (HD) video streaming services, the optical access network industry is embracing a new wave of bandwidth acceleration. This change is further evidenced by recent progress by standards bodies in developing next-generation high-speed PON standards. To support the ever-growing bandwidth demand from these emerging services, the 25/50G next-generation Ethernet PON (NG-EPON) specification was approved by IEEE in 2020 based on 25 Gb/s per single wavelength, and the 25GS-PON multi-source agreement (MSA) is under testing. The ITU-T 50G PON standard (also referred to as G.9804), based on a 50G single wavelength, is now close to becoming a reality because consent was obtained for multiple parts of the series in 2021.

It is envisioned that 100 Gb/s per wavelength and beyond will be required in the future to meet continuously growing bandwidth demands. Intensity modulation and direct detection (IM/ DD) technologies have been used in the physical layer of previous generations of PON systems ranging from B-PON to the current 50G PON. IM/DD, which employs electro-absorption modulated laser (EML) or directly modulated laser (DML) at the transmitter to modulate signals on the optical intensity and pin photodiodes (PIN) or avalanche photodiode (APD) at the receiver to directly detect the optical intensity, is an attractive approach due to its cost effectiveness and simple configuration. For example, 100G could be aggregated by wavelength bonding of four 25G channels.

Alternatively, a high-speed PON based on a single wavelength with a time-division multiplex (TDM) mechanism can reduce the number of required optical components and the related cost for colorless modules and therefore remains of considerable interest to both vendors and operators. Will coherent optics be an answer for next-generation high-speed optical access networks? In this article, we attempt to answer this question through a comprehensive review of recent research on coherent PONs.

In the next section, we review the motivations for employing coherent optics for 100-Gb/s-andbeyond single-wavelength fiber access by considering three major application scenarios and the four most important advantages. We analyze the bandwidth requirements for these application scenarios and show how coherent optics can support 100G-and-beyond PONs with advantages beyond sensitivity improvement. Following that, we analyze two major challenges posed by coherent PONs, including the challenge of cost, complexity, and power consumption, and upstream burst-mode coherent detection. Then we provide technical insight into the two key enabling technologies for coherent PONs: the simplification and optimization of coherent optics, and burstmode coherent detection. Following that, we discuss the outlook for coherent PONs, including future research directions for flexible multiplexing beyond TDM and rate-adaptive coherent access. Finally, we conclude the article.

Note that a coherent WDM PON or an ultra DWDM (UDWDM) PON with additional capability of frequency selection from coherent detection has been widely discussed and studied [4]. However, the objective of this article is not to debate whether future coherent PONs should be TDM or WDM, but rather to explore how technologies need to evolve to keep pace with the continued growth of speed for a single wavelength. Therefore, as discussed above, this article focuses on the evolution of single-wavelength 100G-and-beyond PON systems.

Therefore, to keep pace with Nielsen's Law, a 100G PON will probably be needed by 2030.

### Advantages of Coherent PONs

One of the most significant motivations for introducing coherent detection into 100G-and-beyond single-wavelength PONs is that the single-wavelength IM/DD PON will reach its sensitivity limit above 50 Gb/s speeds. The typical PR-30 power budget defined in the IEEE and ITU-T PON standards is 29 dB; however, in practice, operators usually require a loss budget of more than 31 dB to coexist with legacy infrastructures and reserve an additional loss budget for field operations. The ITU-T standards define 32 dB and even 35 dB as requirements for long-reach PON systems for extended power budgets.

IEEE 802.3ca NG-EPON defines a receiver sensitivity of 25G per wavelength as approximately –24.1 to –24.3 dBm at a bit error rate (BER) level of 1 10–2. The full specifications of the ITU-T 50G PON are still in development, and early phase results show the sensitivity increases to –22.75 dBm at a BER level of 1 10–2. Better results have generally been obtained in lab tests. For example, a –22 dBm sensitivity was demonstrated for a single-wavelength 100G PON based on an O-band IM/DD with semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA), APD, and more than 189 nonlinear equal-

ization taps. In addition to complex digital signal processing (DSP), a high launch power is needed for 100G PON to stay within the required power budget. Achieving such a high launch power at the optical network unit (ONU) side is still too challenging in the upstream. In addition, fiber dispersion causes significant penalties for high-baud-rate signals. Coexistence with traditional PON services makes it challenging to find a suitable transmission wavelength window in the O-band. Therefore, for increasing the data rate for a single wavelength, limited sensitivity will become too challenging for the 100G TDM-PON to meet the PR-30 power budget (> 31 dB link budget in practice) by using direct detection in the O-band, not to mention extended power budgets.

Coherent optics has been widely deployed in long-haul and metro transmission systems and has now been introduced into access networks due to its superior receiver sensitivity and the maturity of the technology. Coherent optical detection can be simply described as beating between an incoming signal and a clean local oscillator (LO) signal, as shown Fig. 3a. Employing coherent optics in a PON offers four advantages, as described below.

Superior Receiver Sensitivity: Coherent beating between the signal and a clean LO significantly improves the receiver sensitivity. Figure 3b compares the sensitivity for coherent detection and the IM/DD for 100G-and-beyond access networks. The receiver sensitivity for coherent detection of a 100 Gb/s polarization-division multiplexing quadrature phase shift keying (25-GBaud PDM-QPSK) signal is more than 14 dB better at a BER of 1 10–3 than a 100 Gb/s IM/DD system that uses transceivers with a 100 GBaud non-return-to-zero (NRZ) signal with 100 GHz bandwidth. Note that 100 GHz optics is still very challenging. If we limit the transceiver optical bandwidth to 25 GHz, the sensitivity improvement by coherent detection is more than 19 dB, compared to 100 GBaud NRZ or 50 GBaud pulse amplitude modulation (PAM)-4 signals. Simulation results show that the sensitivity of coherent optics is far better than the IM/DD solutions for bandwidth limited cases. For higher speeds, the simulated sensitivity of the 200G single-wavelength PON based on 50 Gbaud PDM QPSK can be as low as –36 dBm at a BER of 1 10–3 and –38.5 dBm at a BER of 1 10–2. Therefore, a coherent PON can support a longer transmission distance and/or a larger number of end users than an IM/DD PON.

High Speed Realization by Using Advanced Modulation Formats: Multidimensional and more advanced modulation formats of coherent optics enable high-speed access [8]. Unlike a DD solution in which the signal is represented in only one dimension by the intensity or amplitude, coherent optics enables linear conversion of the optical field and can detect signals on the amplitude, phase, and polarization. Therefore, multidimensional modulation can be achieved to enable high spectral efficiency and consequently high-speed data transmission based on low-bandwidth optoelectronic devices. An example is shown in Fig. 3b: 100 Gb/s can be achieved using PDM-QPSK and 200 Gb/s can be achieved using PDM-16-ary quadrature amplitude modulation (16-QAM) for the same 25G class of optoelectronic devices.

The simulated sensitivity of the 200G single-wavelength PON based on 25 Gbaud PDM 16-QAM can be as low as –32 dBm at a BER of 1 10–3 and –34.5 dBm at a BER of 1 10–2, which is much better compared to IM/DD solutions.

Digital Dispersion Compensation and More: Another significant benefit of coherent detection is that advanced DSP is enabled by linear conversion of the optical field. Optical and electrical impairments (e.g., chromatic dispersion) can be completely handled in the digital domain [8, 9]. This property of coherent detection is very attractive and useful because high-speed access in the C-band is enabled. Compared to alternative IM/ DD PONs in which wavelengths are allocated to the O-band to prevent incurring a dispersion penalty, the wavelength window is considerably expanded using coherent detection because chromatic dispersion is almost eliminated. Consequently, more optical spectra become available for future use and network upgrades.

Channel Selection without an Optical Filter: Finally, coherent detection provides the capability of frequency selection without the need for an optical filter. The inherent frequency selectivity of coherent detection enables the detection of only frequencies close to the LO frequency for a receiver with limited bandwidth; or if a broadband coherent receiver is used, digital channel selection can be realized by using digital filters. Therefore, the wavelength of the LO in the OLT or ONU can be changed to select a specific channel or subchannel. Consequently, no optical filter is needed at the receiver side, and no frequency-selective components are required in the optical distribution network (ODN). This characteristic is useful for future WDM coherent PON extensions. Furthermore, coherent detection also enables the additional frequency-division multiplex (FDM) scheme, which is discussed later.

The analysis presented above shows that to fulfill a PR-30-and-beyond power budget, a 100G single-wavelength PON will probably require coherent detection. Therefore, it has attracted lots of research attention recently as a promising candidate for 100 Gb/s or higher PON systems. There have already been many experimental demonstrations of coherent PONs achieving 100 Gb/s for single wavelengths over 50 and 100 km by using adaptive DSP for chromatic dispersion compensation, clock recovery, frequency offset compensation, polarization demultiplexing, carrier phase recovery, and channel equalization. An 800-G (T) WDM coherent PON based on 100G/ has been used to demonstrate the considerable potential of coherent detection for future terabit-per-second PON super broadband access [10].

### Cost, Complexity, and Power Consumption

Cost is the first challenge that must be resolved to introduce coherent optics into access networks [8, 9]. A PON is generally directly connected to end users and is therefore highly sensitive to the cost of the optical and electrical components used in the network because of the enormous market involved. Coherent optics used in long-haul and metro optical transmission are all typically based on state-of-the-art photonic and electrical components to obtain best-in-class performance. Direct implementation of coherent optics from long hauls and metros in access networks is uneconomical because of high costs. In addition, compared to an IM/DD PON, coherent PON requires additional LO lasers, advanced modulators, additional photodetectors (PDs), digital-to-analog converters (DACs)/analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), and DSP application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs). These components increase the transceiver cost and complexity, as well as the power consumption. Therefore, the lasers, modulators, coherent receivers, and DSPs in an access network must be optimized to lower the complexity, cost, and power consumption.

### Upstream Burst-ModeDetection

Upstream burst-mode detection is the second major challenge to the implementation of a coherent single-wavelength TDM PON. In the PON downstream, signals are continuously broadcast to all users, whereas the signals in the upstream are received from different users, burst by burst. The upstream signals may have completely different power levels, clocks, frequencies, phases, and states of polarization (SOPs). Compared to the burst-mode receiver in IM/DD, coherent burst-mode detection is even more challenging due to the complexity of coherent optical signals. These challenges include burst-mode linear amplification and burst-mode DSP. As the upstream signals have different power levels, the first challenge of burst-mode coherent detection is burst signal amplification. For an IM/DD PON, burstmode amplification is typically realized by using a burst-mode limiting transimpedance amplifier (BM-LM-TIA) after the PD. However, for coherent burst-mode detection, multiple linear and identical burst-mode TIAs are required for two or four paths in polarization- and phase-diversity coherent detection. Burst-mode DSP is another issue that needs to be solved. Conventional DSP algorithms used in coherent detection are typically designed for point-to-point (P2P) continuous mode receivers and are not suitable for burst-mode detection because of an excessively long acquisition time for signal recovery. Burst-mode DSP requires rapid action by all subsystems to detect short bursts. Additionally, the frequency-offset compensation range in the upstream burst-mode coherent detection should be sufficiently large to cover possible wavelength drifts from different users.

# Enabling Technologies

### Simplification and Optimization of Coherent Optics

To adapt coherent optics to access networks, many methods are available to simplify the setup, optimize the DSP, and reduce power consumption. Simplification and optimization of coherent optics in the access network have currently become active research topics in both industry and academia [11]. The cost of coherent detection can be broken down into several parts: the laser source, transmitter, receiver, and DSP. The first three parts are mainly optical and electrical components that can be simplified in many ways.

In long-haul transmission, laser sources on both the transmitter and receiver sides are generally expensive, high-quality, narrow-linewidth (typically less than 100 kHz) external cavity lasers (ECLs). Typically, two laser sources are needed in one transceiver, including one signal carrier and one LO. One solution for cost reduction is to use fewer lasers in the ONU. Using a remote laser source from the centralized OLT for each ONU would reduce the overall cost by distributing the laser cost over the whole network [4]. Another way of lowering the cost would be to replace expensive ECLs with lower-cost lasers, such as distributed feedback (DFB) lasers ( 1 MHz linewidth), and allow an acceptable power penalty in the access network [8].

Another major simplification is related to the transceiver, especially on the receiver side. A fullfield optical coherent receiver is typically used in long-haul transmission systems and consists of four pairs of balanced PDs (8 PDs) with polarizationand phase-diversity homodyne coherent detection. Consequently, four ADCs are required for signal processing. This setup could be simplified by using fewer PDs. The use of heterodyne coherent detection can halve the number of required balanced PDs, ADCs, and associated TIAs. The system could be further simplified by removing the polarization-diversity receiving system and using polarization scrambling or a special polarization-time block coding method [11]. In addition to simplifying the receiver, coherent detection could also be applied using conventional intensity or amplitude modulation formats, for example, NRZ or PAM-4. This modification would also simplify the transmitter side without the use of complex in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) modulators.

DSP optimization in a coherent PON is also a very active research area in which system functionalities are optimized to reduce power consumption [8]. Compared to long-haul and metro coherent systems in which distance-dependent equalizations result in considerable computational complexity and power consumption, the access network generally requires much less DSP due to the shorter transmission distance. For example, fixed chromatic dispersion with huge computation complexity can be eliminated in the access network, as the dispersion accumulating in a typical access link can be compensated in the adaptive equalizers with a small number of taps. The lower number of digital taps represents lower power consumption from the DSP. In addition, polar-

ization mode dispersion (PMD) compensation is not necessary over a 20 km fiber link, which further reduces both the complexity and power consumption. Other functions, such as carrier phase recovery, can be simplified by using shared phase estimation information between two polarizations for which the phase noise is almost identical in short transmission links [10]. These modifications can significantly reduce the total energy consumption by optimizing the DSP for the access network environment [8].

### Coherent Burst-ModeDetection

As mentioned above, a key challenge for coherent PON is the realization of upstream coherent burst-mode detection. Many innovations have been made in this research area.

Burst-mode coherent detection generally includes burst-mode linear amplification and burstmode DSP. Burst-mode amplification is required to deal with different signal power levels and enlarge the dynamic range. As considerable challenges are still associated with coherent receivers with burst-mode linear TIAs, optical burst-mode preamplification is an effective method of leveling burst signals before these signals are sent to coherent receivers. In [12], a real-time burst-mode coherent receiver with an automatic gain control SOA (AGC-SOA) was demonstrated and a real-time digital signal processor was proposed. Most recently, a 25 Gbaud PAM-4 linear burstmode receiver designed in a 0.25 mm SiGe:C was demonstrated, showing the possibility of using burst-mode linear TIAs in the future.

Burst-mode DSP and an efficient preamble design at the receiver side are needed in addition to optical amplification. The acquisition time for signal recovery at the coherent burst-mode receiver must be sufficiently short to improve the upstream efficiency. Specially designed preambles and fast DSPs have been reported. In pilot-aided time-domain estimation, a low-complexity widely linear compensation method is used to compensate for the IQ imbalance, and burst-mode DSP based on precalculated finite impulse response (FIR) filter coefficients has also been demonstrated to shorten the acquisition time.

# Future Research Directions

Coherent optics provide many benefits to access networks beyond sensitivity improvement. Innovations will foreseeably continue to be made in this area that promote technology development and take full advantage of coherent detection. In addition to the above-mentioned key enabling technologies, there are some interesting research directions that have been attracting increasing interest.

### Flexible Multiplexing Beyond TDM

The development of coherent detection will enable the utilization of more flexible multiplexing, including TDM, frequency-division multiplexing (FDM), WDM, and multidimensional multiplexing, in optical access networks. Solutions such as XR optics-enabled P2MP coherent optical network architecture utilizing digital subcarrier multiplexing technology show the possibility of FDM in a fine frequency granularity.

TDM has been the dominant multiplexing technology in optical access networks for decades, and WDM has been proposed to considerably increase the fiber capacity. Both coherent TDM-PON and coherent WDM-PON have been reported recently for next-generation access, showing the significant advantages of coherent detection. A TDM-PON can achieve a high peak data rate because of a high statistical multiplexing gain for bandwidth sharing with colorless components. A WDM-PON can provide secure virtual P2P and low latency to dedicated users. More flexible bandwidth allocation can be achieved for the next-generation access network by taking advantage of both types of PONs mentioned above, as shown in Fig. 5, where a novel rate-flexible dynamic TFDM 100G/l coherent PON architecture based on DSCM technology [14] is used to combine the advantages of TDM and FDM. Multiple receivers and different wavelengths are generally necessary for different subcarriers from different ONUs to prevent optical beating inter-

ference in IM/DD systems. However, whether downstream or upstream, the OLT and ONU require only one wavelength and one transceiver in the proposed TFDM system due to coherent multiple subcarrier detection. The TFDM coherent PON enables multiplexing flexibility in both the time and frequency domains for P2MP coherent access networks.

In addition, combining TDM, FDM, WDM, and polarization-division multiplexing (PDM) into one coherent system could enable multidimensional coherent access. Novel multiplexing schemes, including time-division multiple access (TDMA), frequency-division multiple access (FDMA), code-division multiple access (CDMA) and non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA), and so on, are also worthy of further study in future coherent PON systems.

During this process, the modulation formats change from 64-QAM to QPSK, whereas the net data rate decreases from 255 Gb/s to 85 Gb/s.

# Conclusions

We have reviewed the use cases, challenges, and recent progress for using a high-speed coherent PON for 100G/-and -beyond fiber access networks. An analysis of the bandwidth requirements for three major services shows how 100G-and-beyond PONs will be needed. In addition, four major advantages of using coherent optics to obtain 100 Gb/s-and-beyond single-wavelength fiber access are presented. However, major challenges to the implementation of coherent PONs remain. Cost, complexity, and high power consumption are among the challenges that must be resolved to introduce coherent optics into access networks. Upstream burst detection is another problem that must be solved to implement coherent TDM-PONs. Many schemes have been developed to adapt coherent optics to access networks. To take full advantage of coherent detection, future directions, including flexible multiplexing beyond TDM and rate-adaptive access in coherent PON, are introduced and discussed.
