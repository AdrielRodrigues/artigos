# Key Concepts

Reference notes synthesizing the core concepts needed to read this collection of papers on space-division multiplexing, few-mode fibers, coherent PON, elastic optical networks, and 5G-oriented optical transport.

## Coherent PON Foundations and Motivation

### Drivers of 100G-and-Beyond Access Demand
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook | gen: f8494dabb30b -->
Three scenarios drive the need for 100 Gb/s per wavelength in access. In residential broadband, 8K/16K video and VR/AR services are expected to require 3-4 Gb/s of downlink per user, so with a 1:64 split ratio the total bandwidth could exceed 84 Gb/s by 2030. In mobile X-haul, the transition to B5G and 6G requires high-capacity links between distributed units (DU) and radio units (RU); depending on the functional split, peak downlink requirements range from about 3 Gb/s to over 86 Gb/s. In business services, Nielsen's Law of Internet Bandwidth, which predicts a 50% CAGR for high-end users, implies that 100G capacity will be needed around 2030 by enterprise and cloud service providers.

### IM/DD Limitations in High-Speed PON
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook, coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: 5c9e1f8025d4 -->
Intensity modulation/direct detection has been the standard in PONs because of its simplicity and low cost, but it faces severe physical limits above 50 Gb/s per wavelength. Direct detection only senses signal intensity, and its sensitivity drops as line rate grows because of increased noise bandwidth and chromatic dispersion penalties. This limited receiver sensitivity restricts the power budget, split ratio and reach, and prevents IM/DD from meeting the loss budgets of deployed optical distribution networks (ODNs), such as the PR-30 budget (>31 dB) at 100G. Because direct detection is a square-law process, linear impairments become nonlinear and are difficult to compensate with digital signal processing. For example, coherent 100 Gb/s PDM-QPSK shows a sensitivity improvement of more than 14 dB over NRZ. Chromatic dispersion also causes large penalties at higher symbol rates, which confines IM/DD to the O-band near the zero-dispersion wavelength and limits the spectral windows available. Multi-wavelength IM/DD solutions exist to reach 100G, but they increase the number of components and complicate wavelength and resource management. These constraints motivate the post-50G PON era, in which coherent optics is presented as an alternative that overcomes the physical-layer limits of direct detection.

### Coherent Passive Optical Networks (Coherent PON)
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook, coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: ee7dca7a40cb -->
Coherent PONs are proposed as the main route to single-wavelength passive optical networks at 100 Gb/s and beyond, following the evolution from B-PON to the current 25G/50G standards (IEEE 25 Gb/s, ITU-T 50 Gb/s). Some proposals set a 200 Gb/s per-wavelength target for the next generation to ensure long-term viability for operators. By mixing the received signal with a local oscillator (LO), coherent detection gives much higher receiver sensitivity than intensity modulation/direct detection (IM/DD), extending the power budget to meet the PR-30 class (>31 dB), where IM/DD fails. This supports larger split ratios, longer reach, more connected users and better support for 5G/6G xHaul. Beyond sensitivity, coherent receivers detect amplitude, phase and polarization (four degrees of freedom), which allows multidimensional modulation such as PDM-QPSK for 100G or PDM-16-QAM for 200G using relatively low-bandwidth (about 25 GHz) optoelectronics, and enables flexible line rates matched to user needs. Linear field detection also enables digital dispersion compensation and filter-less channel selection through the LO, easing future WDM and FDM extensions. Single-wavelength operation is preferred over multi-wavelength IM/DD because it reduces component counts and simplifies resource management. The reference architecture uses one wavelength per direction, with TDM downstream and TDMA upstream. The OLT employs a conventional dual-polarization (DP) coherent transceiver, while the ONU uses a simplified coherent receiver and a basic amplitude-modulated transmitter (e.g., an electro-absorption modulated laser) to limit cost. An optimistic timeline, extrapolated from historical gaps of five to ten years between standards and about eight years between deployments, places coherent TDM-PON standardization in 2026, initial deployment in 2028, adoption first in low-volume business services, then 6G xHaul (about 2029) and residential FTTH (about 2030). The main obstacles are the cost, complexity and power consumption of components inherited from long-haul systems (LO lasers, complex modulators, multiple photodetectors, ADCs/DACs, DSP ASICs) and the difficulty of upstream burst-mode detection. Coherent optics has already migrated from core networks to data center interconnects (e.g., 400G ZR), and advances in photonic integration and co-packaging of optics, RF and DSP ASICs are expected to ease these hurdles.

### Wavelength Plan and Coexistence in Coherent PON
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: bcd7241a33c4 -->
To be deployable, a coherent PON must reuse existing optical distribution networks, which requires keeping the legacy loss budgets (for example the ITU-T E2 class of 35 dB) and placing the coherent channels at wavelengths distinct from those of legacy services so that they can coexist with TDM-PONs by wavelength overlay. The reference design uses a single wavelength per direction, with candidate windows in the S, C and L bands. One specific plan places the downstream at 1510 ± 2 nm and the upstream at 1560 ± 2 nm. Operation outside the O-band is feasible because coherent detection compensates chromatic dispersion digitally, allowing use of the low-attenuation 1550 nm region, while the LO wavelength provides inherent frequency selectivity that avoids separate optical filters and eases a later extension to WDM-PON.

## Coherent PON Transceivers and DSP

### Digital Chromatic Dispersion Compensation in Coherent Systems
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook, coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how, high-capacity_free-space_optical_communications_using_wavelength-_and_mode-division-multiplexing_in_the_mid-infrared_region | gen: 6f4e3704dace -->
Coherent receivers convert the optical field linearly to the electrical domain, so chromatic dispersion, a linear impairment, can be removed entirely by digital signal processing rather than by dispersion-compensating fiber or limited by the dispersion penalty of IM/DD. In access networks this allows high-speed operation in the C-band, where dispersion is large but attenuation is lowest and amplifiers are available, expanding the usable spectral windows beyond the O-band. Full digital compensation also permits coexistence with legacy TDM-PONs through wavelength overlay, since the coherent channel can be placed in a different band from the legacy signals. A related advantage is filter-less channel selection: the inherent frequency selectivity of the LO lets the receiver pick a channel or subchannel without physical optical filters, which facilitates future WDM and FDM extensions. Over short links of about 20 km, the DSP can even omit fixed dispersion compensation to save complexity. The same DSP-based compensation extends beyond fiber links: in a coherent mid-IR free-space link with wavelength conversion, offline DSP handles phase noise, frequency offsets from independent pump lasers, and distortions from the hardware chain. At the transmitter, Nyquist pulse shaping uses a raised-cosine filter with a low roll-off factor of 0.05 to minimize bandwidth and guard bands. At the receiver, band-pass filtering suppresses adjacent-wavelength crosstalk, coarse and fine carrier phase recovery (fourth-power-based and Kalman filtering) tracks phase, and a 48-tap FIR feed-forward equalizer compensates linear distortions.

### Cost and Complexity Reduction for Coherent Access Transceivers
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook, coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: e7c0dc75c88e -->
Making coherent optics economical for access requires simplifying hardware and DSP relative to long-haul designs, largely through an asymmetric architecture: a conventional DP coherent transceiver at the OLT, whose cost is shared among users, and a simplified ONU. On the ONU transmit side, high-end DP-IQ modulators are cost-prohibitive because of complex bias control and insertion loss, so simple EML or DML lasers generating intensity-modulated signals (NRZ/PAM) are proposed instead. For laser sources, proposals include using a remote LO or laser source supplied from the OLT, or replacing costly external cavity lasers (ECL) with lower-cost distributed feedback (DFB) lasers. For receivers, heterodyne detection replaces the full-field intradyne receiver and halves the number of photodetectors and ADCs, while polarization scrambling or polarization coding (e.g., Alamouti coding) can remove the polarization-diversity hardware. For DSP, complexity is lowered by dropping fixed chromatic dispersion and PMD compensation, which is unnecessary over links of about 20 km or less, by sharing phase noise estimation between the two polarizations (since polarization mode dispersion is low at PON reaches, phase noise estimated in one polarization can be applied to the other), and by using sub-Nyquist sampling and frequency-domain equalization to reduce ONU DSP power. Together these measures target the extra cost and energy consumption of LO lasers, complex modulators, multiple photodetectors, ADCs/DACs and DSP ASICs compared with IM/DD transceivers.

### Low-Complexity Heterodyne Receivers for Coherent ONUs
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: e4487df69879 -->
Standard dual-polarization intradyne receivers are too complex and power-hungry for ONUs, so several levels of simplification have been proposed for the downstream receiver. In heterodyne detection, the 90° optical hybrid is replaced by a 3 dB coupler and the downconversion to baseband is performed in the DSP. This halves the number of optoelectronic components (photodetectors and ADCs), at the price of doubling the required receiver bandwidth. A further step is single-polarization (SP) heterodyne detection, which reduces the front end to one balanced photodiode and one ADC. Because a single-polarization receiver is sensitive to polarization fluctuations, they are mitigated at the OLT transmitter with Alamouti coding rather than by adding hardware at the ONU. Simulations for 200 Gb/s with 16-QAM over 40 km show that a simplified heterodyne receiver can achieve a power budget of 38.05 dB, exceeding the ITU-T E2 class loss budget of 35 dB. DSP power at the ONU can be reduced further with sub-Nyquist sampling and frequency-domain equalization, and by estimating phase noise in one polarization and reusing it for the other.

*Fontes: [Coherent Passive Optical Networks: Why, When, and How](md/coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how_summary.md)*

### Alamouti Coding for Polarization-Insensitive Reception
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: 1453feaf43c6 -->
Alamouti coding is an orthogonal space-time (here polarization-time) block code in which pairs of symbols are transmitted across the two polarizations in a conjugated and swapped arrangement over two symbol periods. In coherent PON it is applied at the OLT transmitter so that single-polarization ONU receivers, which have no polarization diversity, tolerate the random polarization rotation of the fiber without any additional ONU hardware or complexity. The receiver can recover the symbols regardless of the instantaneous state of polarization, so the processing burden is moved to the shared OLT. This is what allows the single-polarization heterodyne receiver, with one balanced photodiode and one ADC, to be used as a low-cost downstream ONU front end, and it is an instance of the polarization coding option cited as an alternative to polarization-diversity hardware or polarization scrambling.

### Upstream Burst-Mode Coherent Detection
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook, coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: 1b91d8d2b646 -->
Unlike the continuous broadcast downstream signal, upstream traffic in a coherent PON arrives as bursts from different ONUs, each with its own power level, clock, frequency offset, phase and state of polarization (SOP). Three main challenges are identified. First, dynamic range: the OLT must handle bursts with up to 20 dB power variation, which otherwise causes TIA saturation or ADC quantization noise. This calls for burst-mode linear amplification, with multiple identical linear TIAs serving the diversity paths; proposed solutions include burst-mode amplifiers (BM-EDFA or BM-SOA) with dual-stage automatic gain control (AGC), as well as AGC-SOAs and SiGe:C linear burst-mode receivers that level the burst power before the coherent receiver. Second, rapid adaptation: standard point-to-point DSP converges too slowly, so burst-mode DSP needs very fast acquisition within the short preamble of each burst, and a wide frequency-offset compensation range to tolerate wavelength drift. Solutions include predefined tap weights and variable step-size adaptation algorithms. A structured preamble can be used: SP-1 for settling, SP-2 for clock recovery, SP-3 for frame and frequency synchronization, and SP-4 for adaptive channel equalization. Conjugate symmetric symbols and Jones matrix inversion further shorten convergence time. Third, frequency locking: low-cost ONU lasers drift in wavelength, which is addressed by using a wavelength comb source as the LO at the OLT, so the signal beats with one comb line without fast LO tuning. To keep ONU cost low, the upstream signal is generated by simple EML or DML lasers as intensity-modulated NRZ/PAM, while the coherent receiver at the OLT provides the sensitivity gain.

*Fontes: [Coherent Passive Optical Networks for 100G/-and-Beyond Fiber Access: Recent Progress and Outlook](md/coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook_summary.md); [Coherent Passive Optical Networks: Why, When, and How](md/coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how_summary.md)*

### Comb-Source Local Oscillator for Upstream Frequency Locking
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_why_when_and_how | gen: 29011ec064ad -->
Low-cost lasers at the ONU drift in wavelength, so the upstream bursts arrive at the OLT with different and varying frequency offsets. Tracking them with a tunable LO would require fast and precise LO tuning for every burst. The proposed alternative is to use a wavelength comb source as the LO at the OLT: each incoming burst beats with whichever comb line lies closest to its wavelength, so the intermediate frequency stays within the receiver's compensation range and no fast LO retuning is needed. This simplifies frequency locking in the burst-mode coherent receiver and complements the burst-mode amplification and fast-converging DSP used to handle the 20 dB dynamic range and short preambles. It lets the ONU keep simple EML or DML intensity-modulated transmitters while the OLT retains coherent sensitivity.

## Multiple Access and Rate Adaptation in Coherent PON

### Digital Subcarrier Multiplexing (DSCM) and TFDM
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook | gen: 12d228db3423 -->
Coherent detection allows PONs to move beyond simple time-division multiplexing (TDM) toward more flexible multiple-access schemes. One proposed rate-flexible architecture uses digital subcarrier multiplexing (DSCM) to implement time-and-frequency-division multiplexing (TFDM), in which ONUs share resources in both the time and frequency domains using a single wavelength and a single transceiver. The frequency selectivity of the LO makes subcarrier selection possible without optical filters. This fits into a broader multidimensional access picture that integrates TDM, FDM, WDM and polarization-division multiplexing (PDM), with potential exploration of CDMA and NOMA.

### Rate-Adaptive Access with Probabilistic Shaping
<!-- sources: coherent_passive_optical_networks_for_100g_-and-beyond_fiber_access_recent_progress_and_outlook, few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems | gen: 0b3e0e50cf16 -->
In a PON where all ONUs run at a common rate, performance is limited by the worst-performing link. Rate-adaptive access avoids this bottleneck by letting each ONU's rate follow its own effective SNR. Proposed mechanisms are adaptive LDPC-FEC and probabilistic shaping (PS). PS gives fine-grained control of the information rate by reshaping the probability distribution of constellation points, rather than switching between discrete modulation formats. Simulations show that net data rates can vary continuously from 255 Gb/s (64-QAM) down to 85 Gb/s (QPSK) as the received optical power decreases, so channel capacity is exploited at each power level. The same principle applies outside access networks: in a weakly coupled few-mode fiber experiment, rate-adaptive probabilistically shaped dual-polarization 16QAM was used across the C and L bands to fill each of ten spatial modes according to its own quality, which, combined with 2 × 2 and 4 × 4 MIMO, yielded a total fiber capacity of 402.7 Tb/s over 48 km.

## Few-Mode Fiber Transmission and MDM

### Few-Mode Fiber and Mode-Group Division Multiplexing (MGDM)
<!-- sources: demand-aware_versus_fixed_mode-group_and_modulation-format_assignment_strategies_for_few-mode_fiber_optical_networks, few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems, forward_brillouin_scattering_in_few-mode_fibers, performance_evaluation_of_list-sorting-based_allocation_algorithms_in_few-mode_fiber_sdm_optical_networks | gen: 35d5256740e8 -->
Few-mode fiber (FMF) enables space-division multiplexing by carrying several spatial modes in one fiber core, raising capacity beyond the single-mode limit. A key attraction is that FMFs keep the standard 125 µm cladding diameter, so they remain compatible with existing mechanical reliability, connectivity, and cabling infrastructure, unlike many multi-core fibers that need larger or non-standard cladding. The main challenge is modal crosstalk, addressed by two strategies: a weakly coupled approach, in which crosstalk is minimized so modes or mode groups can be detected separately without full multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) processing, and a full-MIMO approach, in which differential mode group delay (DMGD) is minimized and MIMO digital signal processing (DSP) undoes the coupling at the receiver. Mode-group division multiplexing (MGDM) reduces the DSP burden by grouping linearly polarized (LP) modes with similar propagation constants (the same propagation velocity) into mode-groups, each treated as a 'superchannel'. Coupling is then largely confined within a group, so any MIMO DSP only needs to span the modes of one group rather than the whole fiber, and with direct detection it can be avoided altogether. Each mode-group can be routed and switched independently, which makes MGDM compatible with networking functions such as all-optical switching. MGDM is also framed as a way to overcome the capacity limits of traditional wavelength division multiplexing (WDM): in MGDM-WDM networks a channel is a wavelength carried on a specific mode-group, and the model accounts for attenuation, dispersion, the Kerr effect, crosstalk and differential mode delay. Mode-groups are ordered from lower-order to higher-order: lower-order groups suffer less intermodal interference and therefore reach farther but carry less capacity, whereas higher-order groups contain more LP modes and offer more capacity but a shorter optical reach. In the network model, spans are FMF and amplification uses few-mode erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (FM-EDFA). Weakly coupled FMFs favor step-index core profiles because of low-cost, large-scale manufacturability, but crosstalk worsens as modes are added, ranging from below -40 dB/km with two LP mode groups to about -25 dB/km with seven. Introducing a depressed-index zone at the core center raises the minimum effective index difference between groups without significantly harming attenuation or effective area, but the reduction is limited by mode overlap between groups. Weakly coupled FMFs are therefore best suited to short-reach use with MGDM and intensity-modulation direct detection (IM-DD), using multiplane light conversion (MPLC) for mode multiplexing. Reported results include 14.5 Tb/s over 2 km of standard multimode fiber and 200 Gb/s bidirectional transmission over 20 km of weakly coupled step-index FMF with four LP mode groups; discrete multitone (DMT) modulation was found more resilient than PAM4 to crosstalk fluctuations in IM-DD MGDM. In field deployments upgrading legacy local area networks without replacing installed multimode fiber, four mode groups carried 4 × 10 Gb/s over distances up to 3300 m in hospitals, universities, and ski-resort sites, a 400-fold increase over standard 100 Mb/s LAN rates. Different mode-groups combined with different modulation formats offer different trade-offs between optical reach and transmission capacity, and this heterogeneity is what creates the mode-group allocation problem in MGDM-WDM networks. Beyond communications, commercial step-index FMFs also serve as a platform for nonlinear opto-acoustic experiments: a fiber with a 4.8 µm core and 62.8 µm cladding supporting the LP01, LP02 and LP11 groups, combined with a mode division multiplexer that launches selected mode groups, was used to study forward Brillouin scattering, where the choice of optical mode group determines which acoustic symmetries and frequencies (up to 1.8 GHz) are stimulated.

### Full-MIMO Mode-Division Multiplexing over Few-Mode Fiber
<!-- sources: few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems | gen: 16e9b0a39c69 -->
In the full-MIMO (strongly coupled) approach, modes are allowed to couple during propagation and the receiver recovers all spatial channels with a MIMO equalizer, so the design goal shifts from low crosstalk to low differential mode group delay (DMGD), since DMGD sets the temporal spread the equalizer must cover and hence its complexity. Trench-assisted graded-index cores are used to minimize DMGD. Standard manufacturing leads to a steep DMGD increase with mode count (about 410 ps/km for 28 modes), whereas rescaled multimode preforms with tighter tolerances keep DMGD below 80 ps/km for 15-mode fibers, and concatenating fibers with opposite-sign DMGD can reduce the total link delay spread by factors of 2–4. To test scaling limits, a transmission over 26.5 km of 45-spatial-mode graded-index-core FMF, with 2.4 ns of accumulated DMGD, used MPLC multiplexers and a 90 × 90 MIMO equalizer; it reached a spectral efficiency of 202 b/s/Hz, with QPSK Q-factors above 9.8 dB on all modes. For capacity, a 15-mode fiber combined with an optical comb source and C+L-band operation carried 382 WDM channels for 1.01 Pb/s over 23 km. The impulse response duration grows at longer wavelengths because DMGD varies with wavelength, and total capacity in such systems is limited by mode-dependent loss (MDL). Full-MIMO systems are therefore considered the suitable route for long-haul transport, where DMGD accumulation and MDL become the dominant limits.

### Weakly Coupled FMF Transmission with Low-Order MIMO
<!-- sources: few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems | gen: bdd6b7c27f34 -->
Between direct-detection MGDM and full MIMO lies a partial-MIMO regime in which weakly coupled step-index few-mode fibers are used with only low-order MIMO equalizers, 2 × 2 and 4 × 4, to undo residual coupling inside small groups of modes. Because MIMO complexity scales roughly with the square of the number of jointly processed modes, restricting the equalizer size sharply reduces DSP cost while accepting some residual fiber crosstalk. A demonstration of this approach multiplexed ten spatial modes over 48 km of weakly coupled fiber, using rate-adaptive probabilistically shaped dual-polarization 16QAM in the C and L bands, and reached a total fiber capacity of 402.7 Tb/s using only 2 × 2 and 4 × 4 MIMO. The approach is considered attractive for short-reach and LAN-type applications, and it is the basis for real-time implementations on ASIC or FPGA hardware, where full-MIMO resource requirements would be prohibitive.

*Fontes: [Few-Mode Fiber Technology Deployments and Systems](md/few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems_summary.md)*

### Real-Time MIMO DSP Implementation for MDM
<!-- sources: few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems | gen: e10c9a65413f -->
Real-time mode-division multiplexing (MDM) receivers must implement MIMO equalization in ASIC or FPGA hardware, where complexity is the main obstacle: MIMO complexity grows quadratically with the number of modes (N²). Using CMOS roadmaps, an analysis shows that while 2 × 2 MIMO is standard for dual-polarization single-mode fiber, a full-MIMO system with many modes would need transistor densities only expected from next-generation (about 3 nm) technology, whereas partial MIMO on weakly coupled fibers is feasible with current hardware. On the algorithm side, blind equalizers such as the constant modulus algorithm (CMA) are considered unsuitable for large-scale MDM because of the singularity problem, in which the probability of converging to a wrong solution (several outputs locking onto the same channel) increases with the number of modes; training-based algorithms such as least mean squares (LMS) are preferred. FPGA parallelization introduces pipelined delay in the adaptation loop, which hurts phase tracking, and the Mori algorithm is used to compensate, improving phase tracking speed by a factor of five over conventional LMS. A prototype receiver built from FPGAs and MPLC multiplexers demonstrated real-time ten-spatial-mode transmission over 48 km of weakly coupled FMF using DP-QPSK with 18 subcarriers, keeping the BER below the 2.7 × 10⁻² threshold of a 20% overhead FEC.

*Fontes: [Few-Mode Fiber Technology Deployments and Systems](md/few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems_summary.md)*

### Differential Mode Group Delay and Mode-Dependent Loss in Long-Haul MDM
<!-- sources: few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems | gen: db3f9541e183 -->
Long-haul MDM is limited by two accumulating impairments: differential mode group delay (DMGD), which lengthens the channel impulse response and raises MIMO equalizer complexity, and mode-dependent loss (MDL), which destroys the orthogonality of the spatial channels and reduces capacity. Mitigation is pursued in both the optical and digital domains. Optical methods include low-DMGD fiber design with trench-assisted graded-index cores, concatenating fibers with opposite-sign DMGD to cut the total delay spread by factors of 2–4, zero-DMGD-slope fibers that keep delay flat across wavelength in wideband operation, and ring-core amplifiers that limit mode-dependent gain. Cyclic mode permutation (CMP) periodically permutes modes along the link so that each mode experiences averaged delay and loss, effectively converting weak coupling into 'quasi-strong' coupling; with CMP, a transoceanic-class transmission of 6300 km was achieved. Digital methods include frequency-domain MIMO equalization, which lowers the cost of equalizing long impulse responses, and digital interference cancelers that counter MDL.

*Fontes: [Few-Mode Fiber Technology Deployments and Systems](md/few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems_summary.md)*

### Multiplane Light Conversion (MPLC) Mode Multiplexers
<!-- sources: few-mode_fiber_technology_deployments_and_systems | gen: 67ae7c7b64bd -->
Multiplane light conversion (MPLC) is a mode multiplexing technology that transforms an array of input spots (for example from single-mode fibers) into the spatial modes or mode groups of a few-mode or multimode fiber through a sequence of phase masks separated by free-space propagation. It is used throughout FMF experiments and deployments. In MGDM field deployments it converts single-mode inputs into four mode groups to upgrade legacy multimode LANs, enabling 4 × 10 Gb/s transmission up to 3300 m and 14.5 Tb/s over 2 km of standard multimode fiber in laboratory experiments. ## Mode-Group Resource Allocation in MGDM-WDM Networks

### RMMWA: Routing, Modulation, Mode-Group and Wavelength Allocation
<!-- sources: demand-aware_versus_fixed_mode-group_and_modulation-format_assignment_strategies_for_few-mode_fiber_optical_networks, performance_evaluation_of_list-sorting-based_allocation_algorithms_in_few-mode_fiber_sdm_optical_networks | gen: 22b5bc53fe2f -->
RMMWA is the resource allocation problem of MGDM-WDM networks, extending the classical routing and wavelength assignment problem with mode-group and modulation-format choices. It is solved as four sequential steps: routing, typically using K-shortest paths (K=3 in the reported simulations); modulation-format allocation; mode-group allocation; and wavelength allocation using the First-Fit policy. The modulation-format and mode-group steps are often treated jointly as mode-group and modulation-format allocation (MMA). Feasible (mode-group, modulation format) pairs are described by a matrix of options, with companion matrices giving the optical reach R(m,g) and the capacity C(m,g) of each pair. The network is a graph of nodes and unidirectional links of equal capacity, and connection requests are triplets of source, destination and requested bitrate b drawn from a predefined set, with uniform node and bitrate selection, Poisson arrivals and exponential holding times; load is measured in Erlangs (λ/μ). A central constraint is the modal-spectral exclusivity constraint (MSEC): no two connections may occupy the same wavelength and mode-group combination on any single link, which prevents collisions and crosstalk. Requests that cannot satisfy these constraints are blocked, and blocking probability is the main performance metric. A common two-stage solution separates offline and online work. Offline, all mode-group/modulation pairs are sorted into a list (by reach or by capacity) and the K-shortest paths are precomputed for all node pairs. Online, for each request the algorithm iterates over the candidate paths; for each path and bitrate b it filters the sorted list into an auxiliary subset keeping only pairs with C(m,g) ≥ b and R(m,g) ≥ route length, takes the first available pair, and assigns a wavelength for that mode-group on all links of the path with First Fit. The resulting complexity is O(K·M·G·W·L), with K candidate paths, M modulation formats, G mode-groups, W wavelengths and L links.

### Multidimensional Overprovisioning in SDM Networks
<!-- sources: demand-aware_versus_fixed_mode-group_and_modulation-format_assignment_strategies_for_few-mode_fiber_optical_networks, energy_efficient_multipath_routing_in_space_division_multiplexed_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 7c3742bca3bf -->
Multidimensional overprovisioning is the waste that occurs when the resources assigned to a connection exceed what the request actually needs along more than one dimension. In MGDM-WDM networks, each (mode-group, modulation format) pair has a given capacity and optical reach. If the chosen pair offers more capacity than the requested bitrate, the excess is capacity slack; if its reach is much longer than the path length, the excess is reach slack. Both consume spectrum and mode-group resources that could have served other requests, and so raise blocking probability. Allocation strategies address this in different ways: reach-sorted and capacity-sorted lists each minimize one kind of waste, a perfect-fit policy removes capacity slack entirely but at the price of discarding many feasible options, and demand-aware ranking minimizes a weighted combination of normalized capacity and reach slack for each request. Which dimension matters more depends on the network: where reach is rarely limiting, capacity efficiency dominates, whereas in reach-constrained networks few configurations remain feasible and the choice of prioritization matters less. Similar waste arises in multi-core-fiber SDM-EONs, where splitting a demand into many sublightpaths consumes a one-slot guard band for each, along with an extra transponder. Limiting the number of sublightpaths, for instance by keeping them on a single route and using the largest available gaps, saves these guard-band slots and spectral resources, and also reduces the energy consumed by transponders.

### Fixed Mode-Group and Modulation-Format Allocation (RF-MMA and CF-MMA)
<!-- sources: demand-aware_versus_fixed_mode-group_and_modulation-format_assignment_strategies_for_few-mode_fiber_optical_networks, performance_evaluation_of_list-sorting-based_allocation_algorithms_in_few-mode_fiber_sdm_optical_networks | gen: d4d711b0e1d9 -->
Fixed MMA strategies build a static priority list of all feasible (mode-group, modulation format) pairs offline, by applying an ordering (a bijection) to the pair matrix. Online, each request is served by scanning this list with a First-Fit policy and taking the first pair that satisfies the request's bitrate and path distance. Two variants exist. RF-MMA (reach-prioritized) sorts pairs by ascending optical reach and then by ascending capacity, aiming to minimize reach-related waste. CF-MMA (capacity-prioritized) sorts by ascending transmission capacity and then by ascending capacity-then-reach order, aiming to minimize capacity-related waste. Because the lists are precomputed, the online complexity is O(KGM), where K is the number of candidate routes, G the number of mode-groups and M the number of modulation formats. A related pair of fixed strategies, named Reach-Sorted and Rate-Sorted, sorts the pair list in descending order of optical reach or of transmission capacity, respectively, and is contrasted with a mode-group-sorted list that iterates by highest-capacity mode-group first and then through modulation formats from longest to shortest reach. Reach-Sorted favors the pairs with the maximum optical reach, while Rate-Sorted favors the pairs with the highest data rate; which is better depends on whether reach or capacity is the bottleneck of the network. In simulations on UKNet, where reach is not a limiting factor, CF-MMA was among the best strategies because capacity efficiency drove blocking, with up to 99% lower blocking than BANG. On Extended-UKNet, where reach constraints are scaled by 2.5x, RF-MMA, CF-MMA and DA-MMA performed almost identically since few feasible configurations were left to prioritize. In evaluations on NSFNet and UKNet with three mode-groups, three modulation formats and 81 wavelengths per group, both sorted strategies reduced blocking relative to the mode-group-sorted baseline.

### Demand-Aware Mode-Group and Modulation-Format Allocation (DA-MMA)
<!-- sources: demand-aware_versus_fixed_mode-group_and_modulation-format_assignment_strategies_for_few-mode_fiber_optical_networks | gen: 51ed6612ad3a -->
DA-MMA is an adaptive MMA strategy that builds its priority list online, separately for each incoming request, instead of using a fixed list. For a request it first selects the subset of physically feasible (mode-group, modulation format) configurations that satisfy the requested bitrate and the path distance. It then computes normalized slacks for capacity (Δc) and reach (Δr) for each configuration and ranks them in ascending order of a decision factor F = β·Δc + γ·Δr. The weights β and γ let the operator tune the priority between capacity efficiency and reach efficiency, so the strategy jointly minimizes overprovisioning in both dimensions. The cost of per-request sorting is an online complexity of O(KGM log(GM)), higher than the O(KGM) of fixed lists. In simulations with K=3 routing and First-Fit wavelength assignment, DA-MMA matched CF-MMA as the best strategy on UKNet (up to 99% blocking reduction over BANG), performed nearly identically to the fixed strategies on the reach-limited Extended-UKNet, and was the best on a capacity-limited synthetic 5-node network, with a 95% improvement over BANG. It was therefore the most robust across heterogeneous or resource-constrained scenarios. Proposed future work includes extending it to elastic optical networks and using AI/ML to optimize the decision mechanism.

### BANG Perfect-Fit Allocation Baseline
<!-- sources: demand-aware_versus_fixed_mode-group_and_modulation-format_assignment_strategies_for_few-mode_fiber_optical_networks, performance_evaluation_of_list-sorting-based_allocation_algorithms_in_few-mode_fiber_sdm_optical_networks | gen: 707481960edb -->
BANG (Balanced Allocation of Mode-Group) is a mode-group and modulation-format allocation strategy built on a strict perfect-fit policy. It considers only configurations whose achievable capacity exactly equals the requested bitrate (c = b), and among these it prefers the one with the smallest reach slack. In its fixed-order description, it prioritizes the highest-capacity mode-group and iterates through modulation formats from the longest to the shortest reach. This eliminates capacity overprovisioning by construction, but it also discards many physically feasible configurations whose capacity is larger than the request, which makes the policy overly restrictive. Its online cost is that of adaptive methods, O(KGM log(GM)), since lists are sorted per request. Used as the baseline in comparisons, BANG had the worst blocking probability in the evaluated topologies. Measured by the Blocking Improvement Ratio (BIR), CF-MMA and DA-MMA reduced blocking by up to 99% relative to BANG in UKNet, all proposed strategies achieved a modest gain of about 25% in the reach-limited Extended-UKNet, and DA-MMA improved on BANG by 95% in the synthetic 5-node network. Reach-Sorted and Rate-Sorted lists also outperformed the BANG ordering, with a maximum relative improvement of 21% on NSFNet and blocking reductions of up to 77% at low loads and 9% at high loads on UKNet. These results show that excluding feasible but non-exact configurations, or using a fixed mode-group order that ignores request and topology characteristics, costs more in blocking than the capacity waste it avoids.

The efficiency of resource allocation in MGDM-WDM networks depends strongly on topology, because topology determines whether optical reach or available capacity is the binding constraint. ## Multi-Core Fiber SDM Elastic Optical Networks

### Multi-Core Fiber SDM Elastic Optical Networks and the RMLSSA Problem
<!-- sources: energy_efficient_multipath_routing_in_space_division_multiplexed_elastic_optical_networks, priority-aware_resource_allocation_strategies_for_space_division_multiplexing-enabled_elastic_optical_networks, routing_band_modulation_and_spectrum_assignment_with_dedicated_protection_in_multiband-_elastic_optical_networks, survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: ef12aa7d3391 -->
Elastic Optical Networks (EONs) use OFDM-based flexible spectrum reservation and adaptive modulation, but they must solve the Route, Modulation Level, and Spectrum Assignment (RMLSA) problem and suffer from spectrum fragmentation. Adding Space Division Multiplexing (SDM) through multi-core fibers (MCFs) introduces a spatial dimension that raises capacity but turns the problem into Route, Modulation Level, Spatial, and Spectrum Assignment (RMLSSA), also called Routing, Modulation, Core, and Spectrum Allocation (RMCSA), where a core must also be selected for each connection. The network is modeled as a connected graph with bidirectional MCF links; in typical evaluations each fiber has seven cores in a hexagonal layout and 320 frequency slots (FS) of 12.5 GHz per core. Allocation must satisfy spectrum continuity (the same slots on every link of the route), spectrum contiguity (adjacent slots within a lightpath), a one-slot guard band between neighboring connections, and core continuity (the same core along the whole route), the last of which allows cost-effective ROADMs without lane changes. Supported modulation formats are BPSK, QPSK, 8QAM, and 16QAM, chosen according to transmission reach. Inter-core crosstalk is an additional physical-layer constraint specific to MCFs. Because SDM-EONs must jointly allocate routes, modulation formats, spectrum slots, and cores, resource management is considerably more complex than in single-core EONs, and RMCSA algorithms often rely on score functions to limit crosstalk and fragmentation. A simulation-based comparison (a Python/NumPy model with pre-computed k=3 shortest paths and dynamic synthetic traffic) of a traditional EON against priority-based EON and priority-based SDM-EON variants showed that the traditional EON had the highest blocking, owing to the lack of both prioritization and spatial flexibility. Adding spatial resources through multi-core fibers lowered blocking, with the SDM variant sustaining near-optimal performance for demand levels below 250. A band-division alternative follows the same RMLSA lineage but replaces the core dimension with a band dimension: in Routing, Band, Modulation and Spectrum Assignment (RBMSA) for C+L networks, the same 12.5 GHz slot grid and guard bands are used, and the band is selected instead of the core. This avoids inter-core crosstalk, though the bands differ in transmission reach, which affects the feasible modulation formats. The two dimensions can also be combined: in multi-band SDM-EONs over MCFs, the problem becomes Routing, Spectrum, Core and Band Assignment (RSCBA), where C-band and L-band slots of 12.5 GHz are allocated per core. The same spectrum contiguity, spectrum continuity, and core continuity constraints apply, with the band added as a further choice, and inter-core crosstalk must be considered together with inter-band Stimulated Raman Scattering.

### Inter-Core Crosstalk Awareness in Multi-Core Fibers
<!-- sources: energy_efficient_multipath_routing_in_space_division_multiplexed_elastic_optical_networks, priority-aware_resource_allocation_strategies_for_space_division_multiplexing-enabled_elastic_optical_networks, survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: bb22bee75327 -->
Inter-core crosstalk (XT) is the power leakage between neighboring cores of a multi-core fiber, and it is a main physical impairment of SDM networks. In the model used for resource allocation, XT on a lightpath grows with the number of neighbor cores that use the same frequency slots at the same time and with the transmission length. The accumulated XT along the route must stay below a threshold defined by the selected modulation format, with stricter limits for higher-order formats such as 16QAM than for robust ones such as BPSK, so that the required Quality of Transmission (QoT) is met. XT-aware algorithms therefore check each candidate core and slot block not only for availability but also for the XT it would induce on itself and on existing connections, and reject allocations that violate the thresholds. This check is part of the search loop and contributes to the computational cost of the heuristics. The RMCSA literature commonly addresses crosstalk through score functions that rank candidate core and slot choices to mitigate both crosstalk and spectrum fragmentation. Some simpler simulation studies of priority-aware SDM-EON allocation, however, focus on slot availability and priority logic and do not model crosstalk explicitly, so their blocking results do not capture this impairment. In joint allocation formulations for multi-band SDM networks, XT is imposed as a constraint: in the ILP, end-to-end XT, which arises when adjacent cores use the same spectrum, must remain below a predefined threshold Ω for every assigned lightpath, alongside a minimum SNR threshold Ξ. In these models XT interacts with the SNR affected by Stimulated Raman Scattering, because both limit which cores and slots are viable for a request. Inter-core SRS, meaning Raman power transfer between cores, is not modeled and is left as future work.

### Multipath Routing and Differential Delay in Elastic Optical Networks
<!-- sources: energy_efficient_multipath_routing_in_space_division_multiplexed_elastic_optical_networks, priority-aware_resource_allocation_strategies_for_space_division_multiplexing-enabled_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 4933ddca4c46 -->
Multipath routing splits a bandwidth demand into several sub-flows (sublightpaths), which can use smaller, scattered spectrum gaps and thus mitigate fragmentation and reduce blocking in EONs. The drawback is differential delay: when sub-flows follow different physical paths, they arrive at different times, requiring buffering and compensation at the receiver. Some survivable multipath algorithms improve blocking but incur this penalty, and others increase resource usage by generating excessive sub-demands. In SDM networks with multi-core fibers, an alternative is to split the demand into sublightpaths that use different cores of the same route and therefore the same set of fibers. Because the sublightpaths share the physical route, differential delay is eliminated and the average delay stays close to that of the shortest path, while the extra spatial dimension supplies additional gaps for allocation. Each sublightpath still costs guard-band slots and a transponder, so the number of sublightpaths per demand matters. Single-path routing with precomputed candidates is the simpler baseline: a common scheme precomputes k=3 shortest paths per source-destination pair, tries the shortest first, and falls back sequentially to the second and third if slots are insufficient, blocking the demand if none fits. Simulations of such a scheme reveal traffic hotspots, with certain links consistently overloaded and congestion at specific nodes under high load, indicating a need for greater route diversity. Multicore-aware parallel path provisioning, in which a demand is served over several paths and cores simultaneously, has been identified as a direction to relieve these congested nodes and improve scalability.

### Energy-Efficient Multipath Routing Algorithm (EEMPR)
<!-- sources: energy_efficient_multipath_routing_in_space_division_multiplexed_elastic_optical_networks | gen: e8e01f6ea9ad -->
EEMPR is a dynamic heuristic for routing, modulation level, core, and spectrum assignment in SDM-EONs based on multi-core fibers, aimed at minimizing blocking probability and the energy consumption of bandwidth variable transponders (BVTs). It operates on precomputed K-shortest paths for each source-destination pair. For a request, it takes the shortest path first, selects the most spectrally efficient modulation format allowed by the distance, and scans each core (1 to C) and slot (1 to F) for availability on all links of the route. A Best-Fit strategy first looks for a single contiguous block in one core that exactly matches the demand and satisfies the XT requirements, otherwise the smallest larger block. If no single lightpath is possible, the demand is split into sublightpaths over different cores of the same route, repeatedly taking the largest available gaps until the demand is satisfied or resources run out. If the route fails, the next of the K shortest paths is tried. The worst-case time complexity is O(KC^3F^3E) and the space complexity is O(KEN^2 + FCE). Evaluations in a Python simulator on NSFNET, SmallNet, USNet, JPN12, and DT, with seven-core fibers and 320 slots of 12.5 GHz, compared it with the Multipath Inscribed Rectangles Algorithm (MPIRA/MPIRAXT). Varying K over 1, 3, 5, and 7 showed that larger K lowers the Request Blocking Ratio (RBR) but raises energy consumption and delay, with little blocking gain from K=5 to K=7. For K=5 on NSFNET, EEMPR gave lower RBR and Bandwidth Blocking Ratio (BBR), cutting blocking by up to 50% at high load, lower energy per bit, fewer sublightpaths per demand, and no differential delay.

In elastic optical networks, the transmission reach determines which modulation format can be used: short paths allow spectrally efficient formats such as 16QAM and 8QAM, which need few frequency slots, while long paths require robust formats such as BPSK, which need many more slots for the same bitrate. The modulation choice thus sets the bandwidth of each demand and strongly affects blocking. Distance-adaptive modulation and routing, which select the format and path jointly according to path length, is therefore regarded as a means of further improving spectral efficiency, particularly on long paths under dense traffic. In multiband networks the same principle extends to the band dimension: the C-band has a longer transmission reach than the L-band, so the feasible modulation format depends on the band as well as on path length. A connection routed in the L-band may need a more robust, lower-order format and hence more slots than the same connection in the C-band. This explains why, in protection schemes with backups in the C-band and working paths in the L-band, backup routes require fewer additional spectrum slots than the working routes, giving a Backup to Working Resources Ratio closer to one. Energy consumption in these networks is largely driven by bandwidth variable transponders (BVTs), so the number of transponders in use, and hence the number of sublightpaths into which demands are split, determines energy per bit. Limiting a demand's sublightpaths to a single route reduces transponder count and saves guard-band slots, lowering energy per bit and spectrum use. The effect depends on topology: in long-link networks (SmallNet, NSFNET, USNet), BPSK is frequently required, demands are large, and flexible allocation that avoids rigid shapes gives considerably lower blocking than rectangle-based multipath approaches. In short-link networks (JPN12, DT), high-order modulations are viable, bandwidth requirements are small, and different algorithms perform similarly. The benefit is therefore greatest in continental or intercontinental networks.

## Intelligent and Priority-Aware Resource Allocation

### Priority-Aware Resource Allocation in SDM-EONs
<!-- sources: priority-aware_resource_allocation_strategies_for_space_division_multiplexing-enabled_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 253c972ac5d6 -->
Most resource allocation strategies for SDM-based Elastic Optical Networks treat all connection requests alike, which can waste bandwidth and block critical traffic under load. Priority-aware allocation instead differentiates demands by class (for example real-time or emergency traffic versus ordinary traffic) during route and slot assignment. A related mechanism in the literature, P-TRRA, distinguishes premium, emergency, and best-effort service classes to preserve Quality of Service (QoS) during congestion. In a Python-based simulation of this approach, each request is read sequentially from a traffic file containing source, destination, required slots, and priority. The algorithm first checks the shortest of three pre-computed k-shortest paths and falls back to the second and third if the slots are insufficient; when several candidate paths are available, a criterion such as least-used selects among them. Once a viable path is found, the slots are marked occupied for the demand's duration, and a demand that fits on none of the three paths is logged as blocked. The mechanism deliberately rejects low-priority traffic in order to keep resources available for high-priority demands. Results show that during low-traffic intervals the mechanism protects high-priority demands, with any remaining blocking affecting mainly low-priority traffic, while during peak periods contention in key segments raises blocking overall. As a consequence, priority-based EON shows higher total blocking than priority-based SDM-EON, since the latter combines priority awareness with the extra capacity of multi-core fibers. Identified limitations include room to improve fairness of resource distribution and the need for scalability measures at congested nodes under heavy load.

### Machine Learning and Heuristic Resource Allocation for SDM-EONs
<!-- sources: priority-aware_resource_allocation_strategies_for_space_division_multiplexing-enabled_elastic_optical_networks, survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 0ae93eb1ffe8 -->
Resource allocation in SDM-EONs must jointly choose routes, modulation formats, spectrum slots, and cores under physical impairments such as crosstalk, and the problem is hard enough that much of the literature relies on heuristics and learning-based methods. Machine-learning-based allocators are used to adapt to dynamic traffic: Tridental Resource Assignment (TRA) and Spectrum Wastage Avoidance-based Resource Allocation (SWARM) are examples that use ML to adjust allocation decisions to changing load and reduce spectrum wastage. Other heuristics target cost and operational efficiency under large-scale internet traffic. Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to the placement of service function chains, and impairment-aware models account for nonlinear effects in the allocation decision. In multi-layer networks, hidden optical bypasses have been proposed to reroute data dynamically. Related work also covers fragmentation-aware load balancing, advance reservation for offline survivability, and priority-differentiating schemes such as P-TRRA, which separates premium, emergency, and best-effort classes to maintain QoS during congestion. These approaches contrast with simple sequential heuristics based on pre-computed k-shortest paths, which have low runtime overhead but do not adapt to traffic dynamics. Metaheuristics offer another route for large survivable multi-band SDM instances where ILP is computationally intractable. A Genetic Algorithm for Survivable RSCBA with band partitioning (GA-S-RSCBA-BP) encodes each gene as the selected working/protection path pair and core assignments for one demand, and a chromosome as a complete network solution. The fitness function weights the successful protection rate (Sp) against resource utilization, measured by the maximum allocated FS index, and a weight w trades protection against spectrum compactness. The operators are elite selection, 2-point crossover, and mutation, with crossover (ρC) and mutation (ρM) probabilities adjusted adaptively according to each individual's fitness relative to the population's best, to avoid premature convergence to local optima. Compared with an optimal ILP on small topologies, the maximum difference in FS index was within 8%. Against Dijkstra-based heuristics (D-C+L-BP and D-C only) and C-band-only GA variants, the GA reached lower maximum FS indices, attributed to global optimization and bandwidth-priority sorting of demands, and a higher protection rate. Evaluations used the 6-node and NSFnet topologies, with Gurobi for the ILP and MATLAB for the heuristics.

## Multiband Elastic Optical Networks

### Multiband Elastic Optical Networks and the RBMSA Problem
<!-- sources: routing_band_modulation_and_spectrum_assignment_with_dedicated_protection_in_multiband-_elastic_optical_networks, survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 6efafc351f87 -->
Multiband elastic optical networks (MB-EONs) extend capacity beyond the C-band by applying band division multiplexing (BDM) over additional transmission windows, namely the L, S, E, and O bands. Many studies focus on C+L deployments, since wideband amplifiers provide roughly 11.5 THz of usable bandwidth and can be installed over existing fiber infrastructure, making this a comparatively practical path to meet exponential traffic growth. The network is modeled as a graph G(N, L) in which every link carries both C and L bands, each divided into spectrum slots (SS) of 12.5 GHz, with a guard band between adjacent connections. Resource allocation becomes the Routing, Band, Modulation and Spectrum Assignment (RBMSA) problem, which extends the RMLSA family of elastic-network problems by adding band selection to route choice, modulation-format selection, and slot assignment. For each request, the number of required slots follows from the bitrate demand (e.g., 100, 200, or 400 Gbps), the modulation level m, and the slot width including the guard band. Because transmission reach differs between bands, with the C-band being more robust than the L-band, the chosen band directly constrains which modulation formats are feasible. RBMSA can therefore be viewed as a band-division alternative to core-based SDM, since it adds capacity through a spectral dimension rather than through additional cores or fibers. When multi-band transmission is combined with SDM over multi-core fibers (MB-SDM-EONs), the two dimensions coexist and the problem becomes Routing, Spectrum, Core and Band Assignment (RSCBA), the band-aware extension of Routing, Spectrum and Core Assignment (RSCA). In this setting, C-band and L-band frequency slots of 12.5 GHz are allocated per core, and combining MB and SDM serves significantly more requests than single C-band SDM. The band dimension also introduces physical-layer interactions, notably Stimulated Raman Scattering between bands, that must be considered alongside inter-core crosstalk when evaluating Quality of Transmission.

### Stimulated Raman Scattering in Multi-Band Transmission
<!-- sources: survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: c1deb0a54a3e -->
Stimulated Raman Scattering (SRS) is a nonlinear fiber effect in which power is transferred from higher-frequency (shorter-wavelength) channels to lower-frequency (longer-wavelength) ones. In multi-band systems such as C+L, where the occupied spectrum spans several THz, this transfer is significant: it alters the power profile along the fiber, producing a spectral tilt in which the gain/loss of each channel depends on its position in the band and on the load of the other bands. Because the launch power evolution changes, SRS modifies both the Amplified Spontaneous Emission (ASE) noise and the Nonlinear Interference (NLI) accumulated by each channel. NLI is commonly decomposed into Self-Channel Interference (SCI), generated by a channel on itself, and Cross-Channel Interference (XCI), generated by neighboring channels, and both terms are recomputed under SRS-aware models. The resulting Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is the quantity used to estimate Quality of Transmission (QoT) for lightpath admission. A practical consequence is that the allocation of traffic across bands affects the quality of already-established connections: when the L-band carries working traffic, SRS interferes with C-band channels and lowers their SNR, which can push slots below the minimum SNR threshold and make them unusable. Resource allocation schemes for multi-band networks therefore treat SRS as a coupling between bands, and one way to limit it is to keep a band idle until needed, as in cold-backup protection with the L-band reserved for backup resources. In multi-core fibers, studies to date model SRS within a core, and inter-core SRS effects are identified as an open topic.

## Survivability and Protection

### Dedicated Path Protection (1+1) and Survivability in Optical Networks
<!-- sources: routing_band_modulation_and_spectrum_assignment_with_dedicated_protection_in_multiband-_elastic_optical_networks, survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 27d1caa988bd -->
Dedicated Path Protection (DPP) is a 1+1 redundancy strategy in which each connection reserves both a working route and a backup route, with the two routes link-disjoint, so that a single failure cannot interrupt the service. In MB-EONs, link-disjointness is required to guarantee full survivability against both fiber cuts and amplifier failures, a requirement that matters in fully upgraded networks equipped with wideband amplifiers. Backup resources are reserved at connection setup, so protection comes at the cost of additional spectrum, which raises the tension between resilience and traffic acceptance. In a flexible first-fit formulation that searches both C and L bands, with priority given to the C-band, a request for which a working route exists but no disjoint backup route can be found is accepted as unprotected rather than blocked. This makes the percentage of protected requests (PR) a metric distinct from blocking, since a network can accept more traffic by tolerating a larger share of unprotected connections. Earlier survivability work in MB-EONs addressed band partitioning, GSNR-sensitive schemes, and virtual bypasses for backup paths; the dedicated 1+1 case with a strict disjoint-route requirement offers the strongest guarantee but also the highest resource cost, and shared backup resources are noted as a possible way to further optimize capacity. In MB-SDM networks with multi-core fibers, the same principle is applied statically: for each demand, defined by source, destination, and required bandwidth, k-shortest link-disjoint path pairs (working and protection) are pre-computed, and exactly one pair is selected. The core assignment must be consistent across the two paths, in addition to spectrum contiguity, spectrum continuity, and core continuity along each path. Survivability is then evaluated through the successful protection rate (Sp), the share of demands for which a valid protection allocation satisfying crosstalk and SNR constraints is found. Link failures in high-capacity networks interrupt very large amounts of traffic, which motivates this strict protection even though it consumes additional spectrum.

### Dedicated Band Protection Schemes in MB-EON
<!-- sources: routing_band_modulation_and_spectrum_assignment_with_dedicated_protection_in_multiband-_elastic_optical_networks, survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 471e7f0eb718 -->
Five protection approaches have been compared in C+L MB-EONs. DPP-RBMSA is fully flexible: a first-fit search over both bands finds working and backup routes, prioritizing the C-band. The two dedicated band protection (DBP) schemes fix the role of each band: DBP-Cw-Lb places working resources strictly in the C-band and backup resources strictly in the L-band, while DBP-Cb-Lw does the reverse, with working in the L-band and backup in the C-band. The two hybrid schemes relax only the working side: hyb-DBP-Cw-Lb restricts backups to the L-band but searches both bands for working resources, and hyb-DBP-Cb-Lw restricts backups to the C-band while searching both L and C for working resources. Simulations on six topologies (US-24, Paneuro, Coronet, Cost239, DT-17, JP-12), using an event-driven Matlab simulator with Poisson arrivals and exponential holding times, showed that hyb-DBP-Cb-Lw consistently achieved the lowest bitrate blocking probability, which is attributed to its freedom in choosing the working band combined with the longer reach of the C-band for backups. However, it often protected a smaller share of requests than DPP-RBMSA. In large networks (US-24, Paneuro, Coronet), DPP-RBMSA provided significantly higher protection with only a marginal increase in blocking, so the choice between schemes reflects a trade-off between maximizing accepted traffic and maximizing resilience. A related band partition protection scheme for MB-SDM-EONs uses cold backup, with working resources strictly in the C-band and protection resources strictly in the L-band, so that the L-band stays idle until a failure occurs. Because no working traffic is carried in the L-band during normal operation, Stimulated Raman Scattering (SRS) from L-band channels onto C-band channels is avoided, which maximizes SNR for active services. In simulations on a 6-node topology and NSFnet, this partitioned scheme (GA-S-RSCBA-BP) yielded a higher SNR ratio than a mixed scheme in which working and protection resources are randomly distributed across bands (GA-C+L-mix), with the gap growing as request volume increased. It also achieved the highest successful protection rate, since reduced SRS interference leaves more frequency slots above the SNR threshold and therefore more valid protection resources. The reported cost in spectrum compactness was small, so the scheme improves QoT without significantly sacrificing spectrum utilization. Placing working resources in the L-band instead is left as future work.

### Protection Evaluation Metrics: BBP, BWRR and BSU
<!-- sources: routing_band_modulation_and_spectrum_assignment_with_dedicated_protection_in_multiband-_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 508577777318 -->
Protection schemes in MB-EONs are evaluated with several complementary metrics. Bitrate Blocking Probability (BBP) measures the fraction of requested bitrate that the network fails to serve, and is the main indicator of traffic acceptance. The Backup to Working Resources Ratio (BWRR) compares the spectrum slots consumed by backup routes to those consumed by working routes, quantifying the redundancy overhead of protection; values closer to one indicate that backup paths need roughly the same spectrum as working paths. Band Spectrum Utilization (BSU) reports how heavily each band (C or L) is occupied, revealing how a scheme distributes load across bands. These are complemented by the percentage of protected requests (PR), which counts accepted connections that actually received a disjoint backup, since flexible schemes may accept requests as unprotected. Read together, the metrics expose the trade-off between acceptance and resilience: a scheme with the lowest BBP, such as hyb-DBP-Cb-Lw, may show a lower PR than DPP-RBMSA, and BWRR values near one for the Cb-Lw variants arise because C-band backup routes, thanks to better reach, need fewer extra slots than L-band working routes.

### ILP Formulations for Survivable Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: survivable_routing_spectrum_core_and_band_assignment_in_multi-band_space_division_multiplexing_elastic_optical_networks | gen: da5b5898d874 -->
Integer Linear Programming (ILP) provides exact formulations for survivable resource allocation in multi-band SDM elastic optical networks, serving as an optimality reference for heuristics. The ILP for Band Partitioning (ILP-BP) minimizes the maximum index of allocated frequency slots (Fmax) while maximizing SNR. Its constraint groups are: path and core selection, requiring each request to have exactly one primary and one backup path that are link-disjoint, with a consistent core assignment across both paths; spectrum assignment, enforcing non-overlapping FS usage and spectrum contiguity; band partitioning, mandating that working resources lie strictly in the C-band and protection resources strictly in the L-band; and physical-layer constraints, keeping end-to-end inter-core crosstalk below a threshold Ω and SNR above a minimum threshold Ξ, with SNR accounting for ASE noise and nonlinear interference modified by Stimulated Raman Scattering. As a baseline, ILP-MIX uses the same formulation without band partition constraints, so working and backup resources may be allocated in either the C or L band, following a first-fit approach. Traffic demands are static, with 12.5 GHz slots in each band and k-shortest disjoint path pairs pre-computed per demand. The ILPs were solved with Gurobi on small topologies such as a 6-node network, where they act as the reference against which the genetic-algorithm heuristic is validated; the heuristic's FS index differed from the ILP-BP optimum by at most 8%. For larger networks such as NSFnet with many requests, the ILP becomes computationally intractable, which motivates the heuristic approach.

## Forward Brillouin Scattering in Fibers

### Forward Brillouin Scattering in Optical Fibers
<!-- sources: forward_brillouin_scattering_in_few-mode_fibers | gen: 3dc258421b63 -->
Forward Brillouin scattering (FBS) is an opto-mechanical interaction in which co-propagating optical fields couple to acoustic waves. Unlike backward Brillouin scattering, which stimulates longitudinal acoustic modes confined mostly to the core, FBS couples light to predominantly transverse acoustic modes guided by the whole fiber cladding, which acts as an acoustic waveguide. The process has two steps. First, two co-propagating optical fields whose frequencies differ by Ω create an electro-strictive stress tensor and a driving force per unit volume that travels with frequency Ω and axial wavenumber K = β1 − β2. The strength of stimulation is set by the spatial overlap integral between this force and the displacement profile of the acoustic mode. Second, the acoustic strain perturbs the dielectric tensor through the photoelastic effect, which scatters light between optical modes and produces phase or polarization modulation; this step has its own overlap integral, and in Cartesian coordinates it can be shown to equal the electro-strictive one when the fields vanish at the cladding boundary. The forward Brillouin gain coefficient peaks at a resonance frequency Ω_pm^(R). For intra-modal processes (both fields in the same optical mode), K is very small (1–10 rad/m), so frequency matching automatically ensures wavenumber matching and the resonance lies approximately at the acoustic cut-off frequency. For inter-modal processes, K is larger and the resonance shifts upward. In standard single-mode fibers, FBS is restricted to radial and two-fold azimuthally symmetric acoustic modes with frequencies typically below 600 MHz, because of overlap constraints. Using a commercial step-index few-mode fiber (4.8 µm core, 62.8 µm cladding, supporting LP01, LP02 and LP11), FBS was shown to reach 1.8 GHz and to stimulate acoustic modes of first-order and fourth-order azimuthal symmetry. Intra-modal LP01 results mirror single-mode behavior, with radial modes stronger than two-fold symmetric ones and efficiency dropping sharply above 500 MHz, whereas LP02 supports acoustic frequencies up to 1.8 GHz because its higher-order profile matches the displacement profiles of high-frequency modes (shear modes dominate at 500–900 MHz and dilatational modes above 1 GHz). Experimentally, a 1550 nm pump modulated by an electro-optic modulator was launched through a mode division multiplexer, a 1532 nm signal probed the acoustic waves, and detection used a Sagnac interferometer (radial modes via photoelastic phase modulation) or a polarizer (torsional-radial modes via polarization rotation), with the Kerr contribution removed by inverse Fourier transform and time-gating of the impulse response. Measured linewidths scaled linearly with frequency, attributed to cladding diameter nonuniformity, and angular momentum was shown to be transferred between the optical and acoustic orbital degrees of freedom.

*Fontes: [Forward Brillouin scattering in few-mode fibers](md/forward_brillouin_scattering_in_few-mode_fibers_summary.md)*

### Torsional-Radial Acoustic Modes and Azimuthal Selection Rules
<!-- sources: forward_brillouin_scattering_in_few-mode_fibers | gen: b95f0def82c2 -->
The fiber cladding supports torsional-radial acoustic modes TR_pm, labeled by azimuthal order p and radial order m, together with purely radial modes R_0m and purely torsional modes T_0m. Each mode has a cut-off frequency Ω_pm below which it cannot propagate axially; at cut-off its axial wavenumber vanishes. Modes are classified as predominantly dilatational (longitudinal wave motion) or shear-like (transverse wave motion). Near cut-off the dispersion follows Ω − Ω_pm ≈ (ν²_{L,S} / 2Ω_nm) K², where ν_L or ν_S is the longitudinal or shear velocity. Which modes can be stimulated is governed by a selection rule: the electro-strictive force produced by optical modes with azimuthal orders l1 and l2 only has azimuthal orders l1 ± l2, so an acoustic mode is stimulated only if p = l1 ± l2 and the overlap integral with its displacement is nonzero. Optical modes are described as LP_ln groups (l azimuthal order, n radial order), with transverse fields split into HE_ln and EH_ln components. In single-mode fibers the fundamental mode has l = 1, which limits stimulation to p = 0 and p = 2. Few-mode fibers allow higher l and therefore other symmetries. Calculations and measurements for the LP11 group show stimulation of R_0m, TR_2m and four-fold symmetric TR_4m modes, the latter driven by the HE21 component; TR_4m modes were observed for the first time in fibers, identified by a frequency 1–2 MHz below the adjacent TR_2m peaks. Purely torsional T_0m modes, expected to be driven through TE01 and TM01 components, were not resolved, likely because of weak signal-to-noise ratio or insufficient power in those components. Combining LP01 with LP11 (inter-modal) gives l1 ± l2 = 0, 2 for the same-parity terms but also activates first-order TR_1m and third-order TR_3m modes, which are inaccessible in single-mode fibers; TR_1m was observed, while TR_3m was not, owing to limited signal-to-noise ratio or overlap with stronger peaks.

### Inter-Modal Forward Brillouin Scattering and Wavenumber Matching
<!-- sources: forward_brillouin_scattering_in_few-mode_fibers | gen: 0ad1a12a040e -->
In inter-modal forward Brillouin scattering the two optical fields occupy different spatial modes, so the driving force has axial wavenumber K = β1 − β2 on the order of 10⁴ rad/m, comparable to bulk acoustic waves in silica, in contrast with 1–10 rad/m for intra-modal processes. Such large K means the acoustic modes are excited well above their cut-off frequencies, where they have large axial wavenumber, which is not possible in intra-modal single-mode operation. Phase matching of the photoelastic scattering step between optical modes 3 and 4 requires (n_eff,3 − n_eff,4) ω_s/c = ±K, so the effective index difference between the optical modes fixes the acoustic wavenumber that can be probed. Because the dispersion near cut-off is quadratic in K, the gain resonance is shifted above the cut-off frequency: Ω_pm^(R) ≈ Ω_pm + (k0² ν²_{L,S} / 2Ω_pm)(n_eff,1 − n_eff,2)², where k0 is the vacuum wavenumber. The shift is therefore inversely proportional to the acoustic frequency. For LP01 with LP02, measured resonances were consistently higher than in the intra-modal case and the shift ΔΩ scaled inversely with Ω, matching the prediction. Spectra of radial and two-fold symmetric modes differ from the intra-modal ones because of this shift. LP01 with LP11 additionally stimulates first-order TR_1m modes, observed experimentally. The inter-modal experiment used two pump branches, one shifted by Ω with a single-sideband modulator, each intensity-modulated at a distinct frequency (f1, f2) and read with lock-in amplifiers; mode coupling appeared as intensity modulation at f1 ± f2, monitored with a 20 GHz photoreceiver. The large-K regime suggests possible non-reciprocal optical coupling and narrowband isolation.

### Applications of Forward Brillouin Scattering in Few-Mode Fibers
<!-- sources: forward_brillouin_scattering_in_few-mode_fibers | gen: 8a22a26525f5 -->
Extending forward Brillouin scattering to few-mode fibers opens several application directions, since arbitrary azimuthal acoustic symmetries and frequencies up to 1.8 GHz become accessible. In sensing, different optical mode groups can probe cladding acoustic modes over a broader frequency range, allowing the elastic properties of the medium surrounding the fiber to be characterized more completely than with the sub-600 MHz modes of single-mode fibers. In lasers, forward Brillouin lasers with extremely high coherence can be designed with greater flexibility than is possible with polarization-maintaining fibers, because more optical and acoustic mode combinations are available. In non-reciprocal photonics, the ability to excite acoustic modes above their cut-off frequencies with large axial wavenumbers, through inter-modal processes, suggests non-reciprocal optical coupling and narrowband optical isolation. In quantum technology, the demonstrated transfer of angular momentum between the orbital degrees of freedom of light and sound points to manipulating quantum states through exchange of orbital angular momentum between optical and mechanical waves. Some predicted modes (e.g., TR_3m and T_0m) were not observed because of limited signal-to-noise ratio or spectral overlap with stronger peaks, which bounds current practical use.

## Free-Space and Mid-Infrared Optical Communication

### Mid-Infrared Free-Space Optical Communication
<!-- sources: high-capacity_free-space_optical_communications_using_wavelength-_and_mode-division-multiplexing_in_the_mid-infrared_region | gen: 163e9f68657f -->
Mid-infrared (mid-IR) free-space optical (FSO) communication operates at wavelengths such as ~3.4 µm, outside the C-band normally used for FSO and fiber links. Compared with the C-band, mid-IR offers lower atmospheric attenuation in clear weather and is less susceptible to turbulence-induced phase distortion. Its drawbacks are a larger beam divergence, which requires larger receiver apertures to avoid power loss (and, in OAM systems, modal coupling and crosstalk), and the immaturity of native mid-IR modulators and detectors. Practical systems therefore often reuse C-band transceivers and translate the signal to the mid-IR by nonlinear wavelength conversion. Pointing errors remain a key concern: lateral displacement and angular tip/tilt reduce received power for Gaussian beams and create inter-channel crosstalk in mode-multiplexed links. For eye safety, transmitted power should stay below about 10 mW at wavelengths above 1.4 µm. An experimental link combining wavelength- and mode-division multiplexing at ~3.4 µm reached 300 Gbit/s over a 0.5 m free-space path, with all channels below the 7% FEC BER threshold.

### Wavelength Conversion via Difference Frequency Generation in PPLN Waveguides
<!-- sources: high-capacity_free-space_optical_communications_using_wavelength-_and_mode-division-multiplexing_in_the_mid-infrared_region | gen: 9cb80b98f5ec -->
Nonlinear periodically-poled lithium niobate (PPLN) waveguides can translate signals between the C-band and the mid-IR through difference frequency generation (DFG), allowing mature C-band coherent transceivers to be used for mid-IR links. At the transmitter, a C-band signal is mixed with a 1064 nm pump (amplified by a YDFA) to generate a mid-IR idler around 3.4 µm; at the receiver, a second PPLN waveguide with another pump converts the mid-IR signal back to the C-band for coherent detection. Mid-IR output power depends on pump power, signal power (saturating above about 1 W because of possible pump depletion), and PPLN temperature, which must be tuned for quasi-phase-matching. Measured conversion efficiency is approximately -26.5 dB, so high pump power is needed; more efficient PPLN designs could reduce this. The phase-matching bandwidth allows several WDM channels (for example 3.396, 3.397, and 3.398 µm at 27.5 GHz spacing) to be converted simultaneously in one waveguide, and scaling to more channels would need wider phase-matching bandwidths, for instance in thin-film lithium niobate. Conversion adds an OSNR penalty of ~2 dB relative to direct C-band transmission, caused by in-band crosstalk and pump laser noise. Using independent pump lasers at the two ends introduces phase noise and frequency offsets, which are handled by digital signal processing. Germanium windows serve as mid-IR band-pass filters to remove residual pump and signal.

### OAM Mode-Division Multiplexing with Spiral Phase Plates
<!-- sources: high-capacity_free-space_optical_communications_using_wavelength-_and_mode-division-multiplexing_in_the_mid-infrared_region | gen: 2684c0bdf651 -->
Free-space mode-division multiplexing (MDM) can use orbital angular momentum (OAM) beams as orthogonal spatial channels. A spiral phase plate (SPP) with a helical surface converts a Gaussian beam into an OAM beam; the surface height gradient is calculated from the operating wavelength (~3.4 µm in the mid-IR), the OAM order, and the material refractive index (about 2.4 for zinc selenide, ZnSe). ZnSe SPPs for orders +1 and +3 show an insertion loss of about 0.45 dB. The OAM beams are verified by ring-shaped intensity profiles and twisted-arm interferograms. At the receiver, an inverse SPP (orders -1 and -3) converts the desired OAM mode back to a Gaussian beam, while the other modes remain ring-shaped and are rejected by spatial filtering. The measured OSNR penalty of OAM multiplexing is below 1 dB, mainly due to modal crosstalk from misalignment between the beam axis and the SPP center. The scheme is also sensitive to lateral displacement and angular tip/tilt between transmitter and receiver, and to the larger divergence of mid-IR beams, which can couple power between modes if receiver apertures are too small.

### Hybrid WDM-MDM Multiplexing for Capacity Scaling
<!-- sources: high-capacity_free-space_optical_communications_using_wavelength-_and_mode-division-multiplexing_in_the_mid-infrared_region | gen: fa7950f8f1e6 -->
Combining wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) with mode-division multiplexing (MDM) multiplies capacity by using two independent dimensions. In a mid-IR free-space demonstration, three wavelengths (3.396, 3.397, and 3.398 µm) were combined with two OAM modes (+1 and +3), giving six channels of 50 Gbit/s QPSK (25 Gbaud) and a total of 300 Gbit/s. All channels achieved a BER below the 7% forward error correction (FEC) threshold. WDM alone (three Gaussian-beam channels, 150 Gbit/s) showed adjacent-wavelength crosstalk below -13 dB, dependent on optical filter sharpness, and an additional ~1 dB OSNR penalty from multiplexing three wavelengths, on top of ~2 dB from wavelength conversion. OAM multiplexing added a penalty of less than 1 dB, mostly from modal crosstalk due to beam-SPP misalignment. Further scaling would depend on wider phase-matching bandwidths for more WDM channels, additional OAM orders, and native mid-IR modulators.
