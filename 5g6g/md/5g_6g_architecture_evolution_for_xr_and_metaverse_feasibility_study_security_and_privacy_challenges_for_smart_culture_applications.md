**ABSTRACT** This paper investigates the evolution of 5G/6G architectures to support demanding Extended Reality (XR) and Metaverse applications, focusing specifically on the ''smart culture'' domain. We evaluate the capabilities of the 5G Service-Based Architecture (SBA), including Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) and network analytics, through a comprehensive feasibility study comparing stringent XR requirements (bitrate, latency, capacity, power, accuracy) against current 5G performance. Our key contribution is the identification of significant performance gaps where 5G struggles to meet the demands of advanced XR, particularly concerning capacity, scalability, and ultra-low latency. Furthermore, we provide a detailed analysis of critical security and privacy challenges inherent in 5G-enabled XR environments, including virtualization vulnerabilities, API security, and sensitive data protection. While 5G provides core capabilities, significant challenges persist, emphasizing the need for continued research and the evolution toward 6G to effectively support immersive experiences in smart culture and the Metaverse.

**INDEX TERMS** 5G/6G networks, AR/XR/VR, metaverse, smart culture, education, edge computing, MEC, immersive applications, cybersecurity, privacy.

# **I. INTRODUCTION**

EXtended Reality (XR) technologies, including Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Mixed Reality (MR), are revolutionizing various sectors, from education to cultural heritage. By merging physical and digital environments, XR provides immersive and interactive experiences that transform the way users engage with information. As the demand for high-quality XR applications grows, network infrastructures must adapt to support the intensive requirements of these technologies, particularly in terms of bandwidth, latency, and reliability.

Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) resources pose new threats. XR devices, equipped with various smart sensors, collect vast amounts of personal data, such as user movements, physiological details, and interactions in virtual spaces, making them appealing targets for data leaks, unauthorized profiling, and data interception. Moreover, XR applications face specific risks from network-level attacks, such as those exploiting Radio Resource Control (RRC) or GTP protocols, which can lead to session hijacking or denial of service, especially in real-time environments. With the growing adoption of XR technologies, these security and privacy risks emphasize the need for strong countermeasures and comprehensive data protection protocols.

### B. PAPER CONTRIBUTIONS

This paper contributes a complementary perspective to the existing literature by focusing on the current capabilities of 5G networks to support XR applications in smart culture scenarios. Unlike works that examine either abstract architectural models or anticipated 6G functionalities, our paper assesses whether today's 5G infrastructure—particularly its Service-Based Architecture (SBA) and Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC)—can meet the demands of immersive, interactive cultural experiences.

By comparing key performance indicators (bitrate, latency, power, and reliability) with the requirements of typical XR workloads, we present a detailed feasibility study grounded in real-world constraints. In addition, our work uniquely explores security and privacy risks in these deployments, including threats to virtualized infrastructure, MEC exposure, and identity leakage, and proposes mitigation strategies such as Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) sharing and Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI) wallets.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

- A comprehensive exploration of 5G's role in enabling XR applications, particularly for smart culture use cases. It examines how 5G SBA and MEC support low-latency, real-time interactivity while emphasizing the role of network analytics in optimizing resource allocation.
- A detailed assessment of performance metrics including bitrate, latency, reliability, and power consumption—against XR and Metaverse application requirements, grounded in both literature and practical observations.
- A focused analysis of security, privacy, and ethical concerns associated with immersive XR technologies, proposing identity-centric safeguards and networkintegrated trust mechanisms.

## A. 5G SERVICE-BASED ARCHITECTURE (SBA)

The Service-based Architecture for 5G Core introduced new features to meet the diverse demands of modern telecommunication systems, which legacy networks cannot adequately address. Such demands include stringent service requirements for specialized industries, *e.g.*, Automotive Industry, XR applications, Smart Cities and IoT. 3GPP designed the 5G SBA to provide a modular design that enables rapid service deployment based on cloud-native principles. The 5G SBA features are summarized as follows:

- Network Functions (NFs): In the 5G SBA, the traditional entities of legacy cellular networks are decomposed into distinct NFs, each with specific features, protocols and responsibilities, such as user authentication and session management.
- <span id="page-4-5"></span>• Stateless Design: NFs are designed to be stateless. Session states are managed by designated data repositories, such as the Unified Data Management (UDM).
- HTTP/2 Protocol: SBA leverages HTTP/2 for communication among NFs, reducing latency and improving network efficiency.
- Network Slicing: SBA supports network slicing, allowing the creation of several virtual networks over a shared physical infrastructure. Each slice is configured to address specific service requirements, such as eMBB, uRLLC. Network slicing was introduced as a native feature of the 5G SBA.
- API-driven: The 5G SBA provides the standardized exposure of network capabilities to third-party developers, supporting the development of new and innovative applications that leverage 5G NFs.
- Multi-Access Edge computing (MEC): The 5G SBA aligns with MEC principles and facilitates the deployment of network resources closer to the user allowing the deployment of NFs at the edge.

SBA architecture includes a set of NFs that interact with each other over standardized interfaces. The NFs are interconnected through service-based interfaces (SBIs) that use HTTP/2 as the communication protocol, and each NF acts as a consumer or producer of services. 3GPP defines

![](_page_5_Picture_1.jpeg)

the interactions among NFs to ensure interoperability and consistency across different vendors.

### B. MULTI-ACCESS EDGE COMPUTING (MEC)

Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) is a network architecture concept that brings computational resources, data storage, and applications closer to the user at the edge of the network. In the context of 3GPP's 5G New Radio (5G-NR), MEC is implemented to support ultra-reliable lowlatency communication (URLLC), enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), and massive machine-type communications (mMTC), which are the three primary service types defined by 3GPP for 5G.

<span id="page-5-2"></span>MEC in 5G NR is realized through the deployment of edge servers that are integrated with the 5G core network, as depicted in Fig. [2.](#page-5-0) 3GPP TR 23.758 Rel. 17 [\[31\]](#page-15-30) and TS 23.558 Rel. 17 [\[32\]](#page-15-31) introduce application layer interfaces for MEC, facilitating the discovery of edge servers, exposing capabilities to these servers, and managing the lifecycle of edge applications. These servers host functions and applications that traditionally reside in centralized data centers. By decentralizing these functions, MEC reduces the end-toend data distance, reducing latency and improving service delivery. 3GPP has standardized the interfaces and protocols for MEC integration within the 5G system, as depicted in Figure [1.](#page-2-0) This includes the definition of service-based architectures (SBA) for the 5G core, which allows network functions to be modular and interoperable. MEC leverages these SBAs to interact with core network functions such as the User Plane Function (UPF) for traffic routing and the Session Management Function (SMF) for session control.

<span id="page-5-0"></span>![](_page_5_Figure_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.** MEC integration within the 5G SBA [\[32\].](#page-15-31)

Furthermore, 3GPP has worked on defining the requirements and frameworks for network exposure functions (NEF), which enable MEC applications to access network capabilities and information. This allows MEC applications to be context-aware and to adapt to real-time network conditions and user needs.

In MEC, the User Plane Function (UPF) and Data Networks (DN), including Local Area Data Networks (LADN), are located near the base station, which could be integrated with the station itself or a nearby router. This reduced proximity can enable Extended Reality (XR) and cloud gaming (CG) services that require immediate data processing for real-time analytics. For example, a museum could leverage Augmented Reality (AR) to overlay digital information on exhibits, with the edge computing server tracking visitors' locations to deliver tailored content.

In addition, edge computing supports content caching for rapid streaming of high-demand XR content. Such content may include interactive features, audio tracks, commentary, popular camera angles, highlights, and events—all of which can be cached at the edge based on user demand and engagement patterns.

### C. XR SUPPORT IN 5GS

<span id="page-5-3"></span><span id="page-5-1"></span>3GPP began formalizing XR support through NR in 2016 when the Service and System Aspect (SA) working group on ''Services'' introduced the 5G service requirements for XR applications that demand high data rates and low latency. This effort continued in 2018 with SA4 (''Multimedia Codecs, Systems and Services'') detailing the traffic characteristics for XR in TR 26.925 Rel. 18 [\[33\]](#page-15-32) and surveying XR applications. SA (''System Architecture and Services'') established new 5G Quality of Service (QoS) identifiers (5QIs) to cater to interactive services, including XR, in TS 23.501 Rel. 18 [\[34\]. R](#page-15-33)elease 17 introduced a unified set of traffic models and an evaluation framework for XR, while preliminary studies on potential XR solutions began in the early phase of Release 18.

<span id="page-5-4"></span>3GPP's Release 18 introduces the XRM Work Item to enhance 5G capabilities for XR and media services. This includes improvements for multi-modality transmission, network information exposure for media service adaptation, and Packet Data Unit (PDU) Set-based QoS handling for precise media unit delivery. It also features uplink-downlink coordination to meet real-time latency requirements, Packet Delay Variation monitoring for consistent network performance, and power-saving features for XR devices. Policy control is updated to manage various data types for synchronized delivery. Network information exposure uses Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN) marking and APIs for media adjustments. The PDU Set concept is designed for media-specific QoS control, while transmission coordination and PDV monitoring ensure service quality. Energy-saving strategies are developed for the intermittent nature of media services, supporting battery-limited XR devices.

In contrast, external DN functions must communicate through the Network Exposure Function (NEF) using N33.

Despite these advancements, however, delivering ultrahigh-resolution XR content will continue to be a challenge, and further research into network optimization and video compression is needed to meet future demands.

# C. LATENCY

XR experiences demand extremely low latency, especially to avoid motion sickness. Motion-to-sound delays should remain below 20 ms, and the critical motion-to-render-tophoton latency (the time from a user's movement until the corresponding frame is rendered and displayed) must also remain under 20 ms for XR systems to offer a seamless experience [\[1\].](#page-15-0)

5G URLLC (Ultra-Reliable Low Latency Communication) can meet these requirements by offering sub-10 ms latencies in optimal conditions, enabling real-time interactivity. However, in dense environments -like urban settings or museumswhere many users may share the network, buffering delays and congestion can increase latency significantly, sometimes reaching up to 50–100 ms, which may disrupt user experiences and cause discomfort. Therefore, as user density increases, latency grows, especially in XR applications. For example, according to [\[39\], m](#page-16-3)otion-to-photon latency can jump to 50–100 ms in high-density environments, negatively impacting XR user experiences. Such delays are problematic in scenarios like interactive exhibits in museums, where user interactions must be real-time to be effective.

Although 5G provides sufficient performance in some cases, as discussed in [\[37\],](#page-16-1) increasing traffic and PRB (Physical Resource Block) utilization for eMBB (enhanced Mobile Broadband) traffic often causes delays to exceed the 10 ms Packet Delay Budget (PDB). This impacts the overall experience, especially in applications requiring strict real-time feedback like VR and AR environments.

In summary, 5G networks can meet the latency demands of XR use cases under optimal conditions but fall short in highdensity environments, where delays can surpass acceptable thresholds and directly impact the user experience.

## D. RELIABILITY

In XR applications, maintaining high reliability means ensuring that a significant percentage of video frames (e.g., 99%) are delivered within the target latency to avoid user dissatisfaction. The reliability metric is influenced by several factors, including the density of users and interference from other types of traffic, such as enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) traffic.

In dense urban environments, the 5G network can support approximately 13 XR user devices (UEs) per cell when each user is consuming 30 Mbps of XR traffic. However, as more users are added, the satisfaction rate decreases significantly. For example, with eMBB traffic present, there can be a loss of up to 80% in XR capacity, especially if a strict packet delay budget (PDB) of 10 ms is enforced [\[36\]. B](#page-16-4)y adjusting the PDB to 20 ms, the XR capacity can be maintained, although eMBB throughput must be restricted by 50% to balance the demands of both traffic types [\[36\].](#page-16-4)

5G URLLC provides the mechanisms to meet reliability requirements, but as user density increases, the performance declines. In high-density scenarios such as urban environments or museums, the motion-to-photon latency in XR applications can reach up to 50–100 ms, which negatively affects the user experience [\[39\]. T](#page-16-3)he ability to sustain high reliability decreases as user congestion increases, primarily due to delays introduced by the growing need to process more user requests.

To mitigate interference, techniques like Inter-cell Interference Coordination (ICIC) and restrictions on the use of eMBB resources have been proposed. These mechanisms aim to protect XR services from degradation due to mixed traffic scenarios, enhancing the performance and reliability of XR under high network load conditions [\[36\].](#page-16-4)

Summarizing the above, although 5G provides the infrastructure to meet XR's reliability requirements under normal conditions, its performance can significantly degrade in densely populated environments. Techniques such as restricting eMBB traffic or adjusting the packet delay budget (PDB) can help maintain reliability and improve user satisfaction when traffic congestion occurs.

# E. XR CAPACITY

XR capacity refers to the maximum number of UEs per cell that can meet the satisfaction threshold under varying network conditions. Satisfaction is typically defined as 99% of the packets being successfully delivered within the set packet delay budget (PDB) requirements. For XR applications, meeting these thresholds is influenced by traffic

<span id="page-10-0"></span>types, packet size, and interference from other services like enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB). For advanced Metaverse use cases, where the experienced service bit rate is provisioned on at a speed of 225Mbps to allow HD VR streaming [\[43\]. 3](#page-16-5)GPP has defined the experienced user bit rate for viewport streaming -offloading heavy rendering tasks to a powerful device, which streams only the user's current view to AR glasses via tethering- between 200-2000Mbps [\[44\]. B](#page-16-6)ased on the results reported in Table [3,](#page-8-0) these bit rates are not yet sustainable by 5G.

<span id="page-10-1"></span>In frequency range 1 (FR1), 5G supports between 2 and 5 UEs per cell when each device requires 45 Mbps for XR traffic. This capacity can improve to 3-8 UEs per cell when the traffic demand is reduced to 30 Mbps and staggered traffic scheduling is employed. In frequency range 2 (FR2), the performance is notably better, supporting between 20 and 28 UEs at 45 Mbps, and 26 to 35 UEs at 30 Mbps [\[39\].](#page-16-3)

However, mixed traffic scenarios, where both XR and eMBB services coexist, can significantly reduce XR capacity. In such scenarios, the introduction of eMBB traffic can cause up to an 80% loss in XR capacity, primarily due to increased inter-cell interference (ICI) from eMBB. Experiments showed that with strict PDBs of 10 ms for XR, this interference poses a major challenge. Capacity can be maintained by employing radio resource management strategies that limit eMBB throughput by around 50%, enabling better XR performance even when both types of traffic coexist. For example, by increasing the XR PDB to 20 ms, the negative impact of eMBB can be mitigated, allowing up to 6 XR devices per cell at 30 Mbps in dense urban environments [\[36\].](#page-16-4)

Increasing the PDB for cloud gaming services to 15 ms, compared to AR/VR services with 10 ms, also results in a capacity improvement of about 1.3 times. Simulations have demonstrated that by using radio resource management, XR capacity can be maintained, although the presence of eMBB traffic can still reduce the total number of satisfied users per cell to as low as one, particularly in dense urban scenarios [\[40\].](#page-16-7) Furthermore, in [\[45\],](#page-16-8) it is argued that 5G networks, particularly those up to 3GPP Release 17, are capable of supporting many Metaverse use cases. 5G can handle up to 10 simultaneous high-quality realtime XR users, depending on the application and network conditions. However, scaling these services to support more users or highly demanding applications may lead to limitations. To address these issues, 5G-Advanced (5GA), with enhancements introduced in Release 18, incorporates features like flexible Quality of Service (QoS) handling and congestion control mechanisms. These improvements, such as the PDU set optimization and L4S (Low Latency, Low Loss, and Scalable Throughput), are designed to meet the latency, reliability, and throughput needs of more demanding XR and Metaverse applications [\[45\].](#page-16-8)

In summary, while 5G provides adequate support for isolated or smaller-scale Metaverse applications, its current deployments cannot cope with scaling and underload conditions. The future enhancements in 5GA (Release 18 and beyond) aim to overcome these limitations, making 5G more suitable for large-scale metaverse services deployment.

### F. POWER CONSUMPTION

In the context of power consumption for XR devices, which includes head-mounted displays (HMDs) and AR glasses, several methods have been suggested to meet the stringent requirements for low power consumption (<3W). According to [\[39\], t](#page-16-3)he power consumption for 5G modems used in XR devices is typically around 0.5W, which is within acceptable limits. However, as XR traffic grows and involves low-latency, high-reliability transmissions, reducing power consumption while maintaining service quality becomes challenging.

In addition, optimizing power consumption in XR applications is complex due to the quasi-periodic nature of XR traffic, which requires active management of the modem's sleep states to avoid unnecessary energy use. The authors in [1] [dis](#page-15-0)cuss several advancements introduced in 5G-A that aim to mitigate power consumption. One such advancement is optimizing Discontinuous Reception (DRX) cycles to better align with the periodicity of media streams, such as a 60 FPS video. This can reduce power consumption without compromising the delivery of traffic. In 5G-A, a notable improvement is the ability to detect the end of a media burst, allowing the network to immediately signal the device to enter a power-saving state. By better aligning DRX cycles with media packet arrival times, especially for media streams with fixed periodicity (e.g., 60 FPS), the system reduces the modem's active time, thus preserving energy.

Even though these optimizations reduce power consumption, challenges arise in dense traffic scenarios. The need to maintain low latency while ensuring devices stay in low-power states under high-load conditions can lead to increased power draw. The authors in [\[39\]](#page-16-3) highlight that additional innovations in managing power consumption are necessary as the XR user base expands in dense urban environments.

<span id="page-10-2"></span>Thus, while the advancements in 5G-A allow for more efficient power usage, particularly with DRX cycle optimization, maintaining this efficiency in high-demand environments remains a significant challenge.

# G. POSITION AND TIMING ACCURACY

5G and 5G-Advanced offer significant advancements in tracking precision for AR/VR experiences. According to [\[41\],](#page-16-9) 5G-A can achieve microsecond-level accuracy (1-10 µs) by utilizing over-the-air synchronization (OTA), Precision Time Protocol (PTP), and Time Sensitive Networking (TSN). This level of precision supports high-quality, real-time positioning necessary for immersive Metaverse applications and AR/VR environments.

In terms of location accuracy, the authors in [\[42\]](#page-16-10) propose a method that uses 5G uplink (UL) carrier phase measurements for six degrees-of-freedom (6DoF) tracking.

![](_page_11_Picture_1.jpeg)

The results show that this method achieves sub-centimeter positioning accuracy—down to less than 1 cm—and can provide orientation accuracy below 1 degree, even in complex environments such as indoor factories. The authors suggest that this level of precision is achievable at higher frequency bands like 28 GHz, making 5G a robust solution for real-time XR device tracking.

Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) frameworks are expected to further enhance positioning and awareness capabilities. For example, the authors in [\[46\]](#page-16-11) demonstrate that mmWave MIMO-OTFS systems can jointly estimate spatial parameters, such as delay, Doppler, angle-of-arrival (AoA), and angle-of-departure (AoD), with high accuracy, even under mobility and noise. While not XR-specific, such mechanisms may serve as foundational components in future 6G networks, enabling real-time scene awareness and spatial context essential for immersive XR and Metaverse applications.

These advancements demonstrate that 5G-A can handle the demanding requirements of XR applications, providing both accurate real-time location tracking and synchronization critical for smooth, immersive experiences.

# <span id="page-11-0"></span>**V. SECURITY AND PRIVACY CONCERNS IN 5G-ENABLED IMMERSIVE APPLICATIONS**

# A. SECURITY ASPECTS

XR/VR service providers are often not aware of the inherent dangers that come with the usage of Immersive Technologies. The vast number of threats are not limited to conventional cybersecurity threats and vulnerabilities. As immersive technologies and applications tend to adopt a service-based notion, they are vulnerable to typical dangers faced in ICT systems such as malware, phishing and denial-of-service, unauthorized usage, and well-known weaknesses in systems (e.g., unpatched software, weak passwords, and miss-configurations). Depending on the services and the implementation of XR/VR use cases, the attack vector becomes significantly larger and encapsulates threats from verticals. The case, highlighted in this work, is the integration of immersive technologies and advanced networks such as 5G/B5G that follow the MEC paradigm. The authors in [\[47\]](#page-16-12) discuss advanced networks and XR/VR as separate vectors that each one of them is comprised of different characteristics, threats, vulnerabilities, and countermeasures.

<span id="page-11-3"></span><span id="page-11-2"></span>Specifically, 5G/B5G as a standalone enabler of XR/VR use cases comes with severe threats and vulnerabilities, especially due to the trend of network virtualization, shared resources within the MEC concept, and the extended attack vectors that come from the modular components that define such network architectures, as mentioned in Section [III.](#page-4-0) ENISA in [\[48\]](#page-16-13) identified that vulnerabilities are most common in the categories described below. These categories are filtered out with respect to immersive application applicability in corresponding XR Use Cases.

### <span id="page-11-4"></span>1) VIRTUALIZATION [\[49\]](#page-16-14)

Virtualized components must be trusted by the virtualization machine that handles and monitors their proper functionality. The compromising of the VM host is of major importance as it directly affects the availability of residing virtualized functions and services that users or service providers can create (deployed as VMs) to be accessed and used through an XR Use Case. Moreover, the transparency of VMs poses security risks when not handled correctly, leading to reverse process engineering and compromising cryptographic assets.

<span id="page-11-1"></span>One effective measure to enhance the security of virtualized components is the use of a Trusted Platform Module (TPM) [\[49\]. T](#page-16-14)he TPM offers secure storage for cryptographic keys and ensures the integrity of virtual machine (VM) components through chains of trust. By encrypting sensitive VM data, TPM adds an additional layer of security, helping to prevent unauthorized access and protect against potential vulnerabilities in the virtualization environment.

# 2) APPLICATION PROGRAMMABLE INTERFACES (API) SECURITY

<span id="page-11-5"></span>Significant emphasis is placed on the development of secure APIs, as they form a critical component of modern systems. The authors in [\[50\]](#page-16-15) demonstrate this by incorporating robust authentication mechanisms within APIs. It is ensured that access is restricted to authorized users only, thereby preventing unauthorized or potentially malicious entities from exploiting an API. As a result, sensitive data is protected and the integrity of the system is maintained

- Token-based authentication In this approach, unique identification strings (tokens) are assigned to user sessions following successful authentication. These tokens, typically encrypted, are exchanged between the client and server during request-response cycles and remain valid for a predefined time.
- Cookie-based authentication Similar to token-based methods, cookie-based authentication creates a small file (cookie) to store authentication data. This method relies on the framework in which the API is developed, managing authentication on the server side.
- Key-based authentication Unlike token or cookie methods, key-based authentication does not require user credentials for each session. Instead, it uses cryptographic keys (like API keys) to verify user identity, ensuring secure access through cryptographic validation.

Beyond authentication, securing APIs requires additional measures, particularly for Immersive service-based systems like XR environments. Input data, especially complex requests from both data producers and consumers (e.g., for virtual events or remote exhibitions), needs to be sanitized and validated to prevent injection attacks. Many APIs do not mandate cryptographic protocols by default, which leads to the necessity of implementing custom security measures, especially in high-security XR/VR applications. Common

![](_page_12_Picture_1.jpeg)

cryptographic solutions include OAuth 2.0, OpenID Connect, and Mutual TLS (mTLS). In mTLS, both parties exchange certificates to authenticate each other and perform a TLS handshake using asymmetric cryptography, ensuring secure, encrypted communication.

### 3) 5G PRIVATE NETWORK SECURITY

Since the usage of immersive applications for cultural heritage is a privilege for private B5G networks, this category falls under the latter. We consider that these 5G network attacks do not fall under a broad network that integrates other networks of previous generations and their concurrent usage. The paper in [\[51\]](#page-16-16) distinguishes attacks based on the protocols that are used.

<span id="page-12-0"></span>In particular, the authors mention that attacks in the RRC protocol can lead to subscriber ID tampering, user authentication bypass, and DoS/DDoS attacks on the base station. In an immersive environment, where users are continuously interacting with the virtual space, adversaries could exploit RRC vulnerabilities, resulting in users being disconnected or experiencing latency issues that render the immersive experience useless [\[51\]. B](#page-16-16)y tampering with the RRC signaling messages, attackers can impersonate users, leading to unauthorized access to exclusive content or personal user data leakage. Similarly, by flooding a private 5G network with fake RRC connection requests, attackers could overwhelm 5G base stations and prevent legitimate users from establishing or maintaining their connection. This could cause users to experience severe interruptions in their experience, disconnecting them from the virtual environment.

<span id="page-12-1"></span>As users move through different virtual environments or interact with augmented reality content, NAS protocol vulnerabilities could be exploited to track their location and movements [\[52\]. N](#page-16-17)AS-based attacks could also lead to session hijacking, allowing attackers to take control of the user's session, modify their interactions with the heritage site, or cause authentication bypasses.

<span id="page-12-2"></span>Moreover, the authors in [\[53\]](#page-16-18) explain that GTP protocols managing data tunneling could be compromised, allowing attackers to intercept or manipulate the real-time data that generates the immersive environment. For example, in a VR heritage tour, this could result in corrupted or incomplete virtual environments. Man-in-the-middle (MitM) attacks can potentially alter the historical content presented to users, severely undermining the educational and historical accuracy of the application.

<span id="page-12-3"></span>Significant importance also falls on the diameter protocol which is an IP-based protocol for authentication, authorization, and accounting, providing high levels of QoS to the 5G network infrastructure. Similarly, since the Diameter protocol handles critical services like user authentication, an attacker could hijack user sessions, potentially exposing sensitive information or causing financial damage if the immersive service includes premium features. Replay attacks may also disrupt real-time interactions within the heritage experience, degrading the quality of the engagement [\[54\].](#page-16-19)

The work in [\[53\]](#page-16-18) showcases that such protocol-based attacks could affect most of the 5G NSA network domains, varying from UEs, RAN, Core Network, and even external Networks connected to a private 5G infrastructure. Precisely, the authors explain that especially GTP-based and Diameter protocol attacks can directly target Core Network functions such as the PCF, AMF, and UPF.

# B. PRIVACY ASPECTS

XR Devices have a great number of smart sensor capabilities, capturing user, environment, and bystander information from actions, physiology, visual, audio, and haptic input from the sources. These sensors are fundamental for the proper functionality of XR technologies, but at the same time capture a considerable amount of data. Data available to XR devices amplify insight generation capabilities and, in many cases, sensitive information can be either estimated or inferred by reverse engineering either personal or environmental attributes such as user sexuality, gender, location, age, height, and even personal preferences [\[55\]. I](#page-16-20)n our analysis, we identify the following privacy threats related to Smart Culture Applications:

- <span id="page-12-4"></span>• Anonymity and Privacy in XR. The issue of user privacy and network security is magnified with the use of XR technologies, especially in the context of museums and interactive exhibits. The incorporation of personal data into these applications may lead to potential disclosure to third parties, raising significant privacy and security concerns. It is crucial, therefore, to extensively investigate these issues and develop robust personal data protection protocols. This could involve implementing advanced encryption techniques, anonymization of data, stringent access control mechanisms, and educating users about potential risks and their rights [\[56\],](#page-16-21) [\[57\]. It](#page-16-22)'s essential also to ensure compliance with data protection laws such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
- <span id="page-12-8"></span><span id="page-12-7"></span><span id="page-12-6"></span><span id="page-12-5"></span>• Pervasive Data Collection. XR applications are often multi-user and demand remote collaboration. The immersive interaction between avatars requires vast amounts of information derived from user profiling activities [\[58\]. T](#page-16-23)he authors explain how unreasonably granular activities such as the forming of facial expressions, hand movement, speech, biometric features, and even brain-wave patterns are commonly tracked between advanced XR technologies and can lead to person identification. Motion sensors built within cameras in AR helmets such as Oculus, are able to track head directions, position, and environment in real time with considerable accuracy levels. Only considering the level of personal information these data possess, form great risks for businesses that collect them and especially for data subject individuals. In addition, the authors in [\[59\]](#page-16-24) discuss that captured data from XR and enabling devices can form detailed invasive privacy models by combining pervasive data collection and

![](_page_13_Picture_1.jpeg)

interactive behavioral metrics, such as 'walk-through and click-through rates''. The aforementioned models are also able to derive a subject's subconscious behavior, deliberate intent, learning and memory, social cues, and sensory profiles without consent.

- Leakage in Data Transfer. In many cases, wearable equipment, used for XR use cases and applications, transfers personally identifiable information via wired and wireless communications, especially within 5G ecosystems. As a result, it is necessary to ensure secure data transfer for sensitive data. Although communication is encrypted and information is transmitted with confidentiality, adversaries are still able to access raw data by eavesdropping (MitM attack) on the specific channel and still track the user's location via differential and advanced inference attacks [\[60\].](#page-16-25)
- Data Leakage during Storage. Storing confidential and sensitive data, such as user profiles, for a large number of users in cloud servers or edge devices can give rise to concerns regarding privacy breaches. Furthermore, the work in [\[61\]](#page-16-26) highlights that shifts to user-centric data storage models allow the usage of adversary analytical tools that can steal sensitive information. Moreover, as B5G adopts cloud-native concepts (SBA), various data-at-rest attacks are also affecting XR applications [\[62\].](#page-16-27) These include linkage attacks where unauthorized access could grow with linked XR data sources, homogeneity attacks, targeting k-anonymity techniques, and traditional data manipulation attacks in which modified data could be altered by service-specific exploits. One notable concern is the potential for privacy disclosure through frequent queries, as malicious actors might employ differential attacks to deduce users' private information. Additionally, the security of cloud/edge storage may be compromised through distributed network attacks, allowing unauthorized access to the stored data. A similar incident occurred in [\[63\]](#page-16-28) when a customer database of Second Life, a Metaverse game, fell victim to hacking, resulting in the exposure of sensitive user data. The compromised information included unencrypted usernames and addresses, along with encrypted payment details and passwords. This incident serves as a poignant example of the real-world consequences of insufficient privacy measures in cloud/edge storage systems.

## <span id="page-13-0"></span>**VI. CHALLENGES AND FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS**

### A. XR CAPACITY LIMITATIONS

Capacity remains a significant challenge in supporting high bit rates for XR applications, especially with increasing numbers of users. Radio resource management and interference mitigation techniques are key to addressing capacity issues. The PDU-set scheduling algorithm proposed by [\[36\]](#page-16-4) is one such approach, focusing on efficient resource allocation between XR and eMBB services. Similarly, [\[64\]](#page-16-29) introduced a time and frequency domain scheduling technique for URLLC and eMBB coexistence. This solution improved eMBB throughput by 90% while reducing URLLC latency by 29% [\[64\].](#page-16-29)

<span id="page-13-8"></span><span id="page-13-7"></span><span id="page-13-6"></span>Another method proposed [\[65\], u](#page-16-30)tilizes Radio Environment Maps (REMs) to coordinate interference, improving system throughput by 72% in ultra-dense environments [\[65\].](#page-16-30) Furthermore, the authors in [\[66\]](#page-16-31) presented an interference vector judgment-based user clustering method to enhance capacity, particularly for cell-edge users in ultra-dense networks. Another challenging field is mMIMO. In [\[67\], th](#page-16-32)e authors investigated the coexistence of eMBB and URLLC traffic in multi-cell massive MIMO systems. Their work compared puncturing (PUNC) and superposition coding (SPC) strategies, showing that SPC offers higher spectral efficiency while still meeting URLLC reliability needs.

<span id="page-13-2"></span><span id="page-13-1"></span>Future studies are expected to focus on further reducing inter-cell interference and improving scheduling algorithms as 6G systems aim to manage much higher user densities and more complex service requirements. With the increasing demand for mixed services like XR and eMBB, future network releases must refine these methods to sustain high-capacity performance in ultra-dense and dynamic environments.

### B. POWER CONSUMPTION

<span id="page-13-9"></span><span id="page-13-3"></span>Reducing power consumption in XR devices, such as AR headsets and VR goggles, remains a significant challenge. Currently, most smartphones cannot support full metaverse platforms by themselves [\[43\].](#page-16-5) Recent studies highlight various methods aimed at optimizing energy efficiency while maintaining the performance needed for immersive applications. Current trends focus on using embedded AI and task-offloading techniques to minimize the energy demands of real-time processing. For example, the paper in [\[68\]](#page-16-33) discusses the use of embedded AI within 6G networks to optimize power consumption. By allowing devices to offload computationally intensive tasks to the network edge or cloud, this approach reduces energy use without sacrificing performance. Similarly, the authors in [\[69\]](#page-16-34) explore optimizing power consumption through energy-aware MEC. Their work highlights dynamic offloading and adaptive task scheduling as methods to manage power demands more effectively in next-generation networks, emphasizing energy-efficient offloading for resource-intensive XR applications.

<span id="page-13-10"></span><span id="page-13-4"></span>Future research should continue exploring these methods, with a particular focus on balancing energy efficiency and performance to meet the growing demands of next-generation XR applications. This includes improving AI offloading mechanisms, better managing sleep-wake cycles of devices, and enhancing edge computing strategies to allow XR devices to operate efficiently over extended periods.

# C. XR FOR DIGITAL TWINS

<span id="page-13-5"></span>Digital Twin Networks (DTNs) offer a real-time virtual replica of physical networks, facilitating the design,

![](_page_14_Picture_1.jpeg)

<span id="page-14-1"></span>simulation, and management of complex 5G and 6G wireless environments. These systems enable continuous feedback loops between the digital and physical network layers, allowing for real-time optimizations and decision-making. In 6G, DTNs are anticipated to support various use cases such as network simulation, predictive maintenance, and AIdriven operations, making network operations more efficient and adaptive [\[70\]. B](#page-17-0)y providing accurate simulations and predictive insights, DTNs help network operators optimize resource allocation and plan deployments more effectively, improving overall network performance and reliability in dynamic conditions. In addition, DTNs enable operators to explore ''what-if'' scenarios, ensuring network robustness against potential failures or misconfigurations, ultimately supporting more resilient and efficient 6G networks [\[70\].](#page-17-0)

The future of Digital Twins (DTs) research focuses on several important areas aimed at optimizing their integration into complex systems. A key challenge is the issue of ownership, where the physical object and its digital representation might be controlled by different entities, raising questions around data privacy, control, and storage, especially in multi-component systems like industrial setups [\[71\].](#page-17-1) Ensuring real-time synchronization between the physical object and its DT requires ultra-low-latency, highly reliable communication, which current technologies cannot provide, making further advancements necessary. The creation of DTs for entire 5G networks will expand network exposure (Fig. [3\)](#page-6-1) and may involve the extension of the 5G Core to support DTs, enabling the development of comprehensive digital replicas of these complex systems.

# D. SECURITY AND PRIVACY IN THE METAVERSE

As mentioned in Section [V,](#page-11-0) the introduction of 5G has expanded the attack surface due to the integration of various technologies. Ongoing research is exploring new solutions to address the resulting security threats. Edge processing—used to offload tasks for advanced XR use cases—highlights the need for trusted execution environments [\[72\]. F](#page-17-2)urthermore, the authentication of users is a key area of focus, particularly in immersive and collaborative Metaverse scenarios, which raise serious privacy concerns. Another concern is the monolithic identity management model used by centralized authorities, which is prone to single points of failure, has limited scalability, and restricts user control, [\[73\].](#page-17-3) This highlights the need for new decentralized identity models that offer greater resilience and empower users with more control over their personal data and authentication processes.

A promising approach to tackle these concerns is the implementation of self-sovereign identities (SSI) and credential wallets. SSI allows individuals to retain control over their personal data, sharing only the necessary details with service providers while keeping sensitive information private. Credential wallets, like the EU's Digital Identity Wallet Initiative, securely store personal documents and identity credentials, enabling users to authenticate themselves without disclosing excessive information.

In XR environments, where extensive data collection occurs—from biometric data to behavioral patterns—SSI empowers users to manage who can access their information and how it's utilized. These decentralized identity systems reduce dependence on centralized authorities, lowering the risks of data breaches and unauthorized tracking. Integrating SSI and credential wallets into XR use cases enhances user privacy, ensures compliance with regulations like GDPR, and strengthens trust in immersive applications by giving individuals more control over their digital identity.

Additionally, 3GPP has considered credential wallets as part of its feasibility study on localized mobile Metaverse services [\[44\].](#page-16-6) The EU's Digital Identity Wallet aims to provide a unified, secure platform for storing personal data and documents, such as IDs and certificates, while offering users the ability to control data sharing across services in the EU. Though primarily designed for Europe, this initiative's principles could be extended globally, helping to establish trust in digital services and facilitating secure e-commerce and e-government interactions.

### <span id="page-14-2"></span><span id="page-14-0"></span>**VII. CONCLUSION**

This paper examined the integration of XR applications within 5G networks, focusing on how the combination of advanced network infrastructure and immersive media can reshape various sectors. We provided an overview of 5G's service-based architecture, highlighting how MEC improves XR support by reducing latency and bringing processing power closer to users. The role of 5G network analytics was discussed in relation to XR use cases, offering a path toward more efficient network management for these demanding applications.

Through exploring XR's application in cultural heritage and education, the paper demonstrated the potential of this technology to enhance user experiences and access to digital content. The analysis also addressed security, privacy, and ethics concerns surrounding XR environments, identifying specific risks and the need for stronger data protection measures as these technologies become more widely adopted.

<span id="page-14-3"></span>While 5G presents a strong foundation for XR, challenges remain in areas like scalability under heavy network usage, power consumption, and the complexity of handling user data. As 3GPP begins its study items on 6G, it remains to be seen whether the fully immersive Metaverse experience will be realized in future 6G networks.

<span id="page-14-4"></span>In terms of future research, several directions emerge from this work. There is an imperative need for further investigation into the integration of advanced functionalities in 6G, including AI-driven predictive network analytics and enhanced security mechanisms tailored specifically for XR scenarios in smart culture applications. In addition, addressing scalability issues through efficient resource allocation and optimized MEC deployments could significantly improve user experience in high-density environments. Finally, exploring novel identity management frameworks, such as decentralized Self-Sovereign Identities and robust

![](_page_15_Picture_1.jpeg)

privacy-preserving mechanisms can ensure secure and personalized user interactions within the emerging Metaverse ecosystem.
