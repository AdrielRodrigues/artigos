# Optical Data Center Networking: A Comprehensive Review on Traffic, Switching, Bandwidth Allocation, and Challenges

PERISTERA A. BAZIAN[A](https://orcid.org/0000-0002-0586-513X) , (Member, IEEE)

Department of Informatics and Telecommunications, University of Thessaly, 351 00 Lamia, Greece e-mail: baziana@uth.gr

**ABSTRACT** The accelerated growth of data traffic in data centers (DCs) globally is driven by the dominance of multiple emerging data-intensive applications hosted by edge/cloud DCs, such as Internet of Things (IoT), augmented and virtual reality etc. The advent of machine learning/artificial intelligence (ML/AI) and its exploitation in several applications for prediction and optimization purposes has further burdened the traffic conditions within existing DC networks (DCNs) where traffic burstiness and high variability dominates. Most of the existing DCNs worldwide use conventional electrical switches which cannot effectively serve the highly differentiated traffic, while optical switching is currently used by few big-data companies' DCs and only in the core and aggregation DCN tiers. As a result, limited DCN performance in terms of end-to-end delay and bandwidth utilization is provided. Therefore, the investigation on all aspects of optical networking (switching, transmission techniques etc.) in DCs attracts high attention in both academia and industry, aiming to provide high DCN bandwidth utilization, low end-to-end latency (in the order of few µs), and low data loss. In this paper, a comprehensive review on traffic characteristics, switching techniques, bandwidth allocation schemes and power consumption reduction technologies for next-generation optical DCNs is extensively given. Moreover, trends of optical technology enabled in optical DCNs are comprehensively discussed, putting in the agenda the technical challenges and limitations towards efficient optical DCNs capable of serving the stringent traffic classes demands for guaranteed low completion time of diverse edge/cloud dataintensive applications.

**INDEX TERMS** Bandwidth allocation, data center, data center network, optical switching, power consumption, traffic classification.

### **I. INTRODUCTION**

The continuous rise of multiple emerging machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) applications has caused a tremendous traffic growth in existing data centers (DCs) around the globe. Large datasets are exchanged among servers within DCs to train diverse deep neural networks (DNNs), aiming to provide more accurate ML/AI models. <span id="page-1-8"></span><span id="page-1-6"></span><span id="page-1-5"></span>In a DCN, each end-user application request is divided into a number of separate tasks that are assigned to multiple servers to exploit the distributed processing, memory and storage resources. As both Facebook and Google have already noted for their DCNs, many of the requested computations get over a single server resources capabilities, mainly due to the datasets large size [\[12\],](#page-24-11) [\[13\]. F](#page-24-12)or this reason, high DCN server-to-server bandwidth -which is referred as bisection bandwidth- is requested by DCs operators to effectively interconnect the servers and to provide guaranteed low end-to-end latency to the multiple data-intensive applications [\[14\]. D](#page-24-13)espite the technology evolution in hardware accelerators and the use of specific hardware like graphics processing units (GPUs) for intense computation purposes, the response to a DC application request still faces significant delay. For example, today's training tasks for large ML/AI models may last for days, even for weeks [\[15\],](#page-24-14) [\[16\],](#page-24-15) [\[17\],](#page-24-16) while 50% of natural language processing (NLP) models needs more than a day to train, according to measurements at Facebook's DCs [\[18\]. I](#page-24-17)t is noticeable that the large training response time is primarily due to the limited DCN bisection bandwidth and not due to hardware capabilities limitations, since the use of hundreds or even thousands of GPUs is not by itself a sufficient precondition to guarantee the required speed-up [\[1\].](#page-24-0)

<span id="page-1-12"></span><span id="page-1-11"></span><span id="page-1-0"></span>Apart from the traffic growth, another significant parameter that DCs' operators take under consideration for the next-generation DCNs deployment is their energy efficiency, since an exponential increase of global DCs energy footprint is noticed with their expansion [\[19\]. E](#page-24-18)specially, the DCs energy consumption globally is almost 1% of the total worldwide energy consumption reaching 200 TWh [\[19\], w](#page-24-18)hile it is expected to rise to 3-4% by the end of the decade due to ML/AI dominance. Note that only in the USA, the annual DCs energy consumption is more than 90 billion KWh [\[20\].](#page-24-19) Hence, in order to reduce the energy footprint, large-scale DC companies gradually decrease the number of active components used in DCNs, while renewable energy procurement is incorporated. The paradigm of Google is representative of this trend, since it has been working to move to optical circuit switches based on micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) for its Jupiter DC by 2022, to provide energy saving up to 41% and transparency increase [\[21\].](#page-24-20)

#### <span id="page-1-13"></span><span id="page-1-3"></span><span id="page-1-2"></span>A. MOTIVATION

<span id="page-1-4"></span>In this context, efficient DCN performance arises as a dominant concern for DCs operators, while its limits investigation attracts the interest of networks research community. DCs operators are mainly interested in providing high-bandwidth DCN whilst guaranteeing low and deterministic end-to-end latency under limited power consumption. Specifically, the relative network engineering challenges aim to respond to the following DCN requirements: 1) high bisection bandwidth for servers interconnection, 2) guaranteed low end-to-end latency considered as network-layer time response in the order of µs, 3) fast switching time in the order of ns, 4) fast clock data recovery (CDR), 5) effective access control strategy to avoid data loss, 6) DCN reconfigurability under varying traffic conditions, 7) high fault tolerance, 8) low energy consumption, and 9) cost efficiency. According to the above requirements, the network switching technology that can support such switching time constraints is the optical switching technology, as compared to the traditional electrical switching one, since it avoids the power-consuming optical-to-electrical (O/E) and electrical-to-optical (E/O) conversions, removes the electronic data processing delay and provides data transparency, irrespectively of the data rate and format [\[22\],](#page-24-21) [\[23\]. T](#page-24-22)owards this direction, large-scale DCs operators -like Google- have already moved to the optical switching era in order to achieve power and performance efficiency [\[21\].](#page-24-20)

### C. ORIGINAL REVIEW CONTRIBUTION AND STRUCTURE

In the light of the aforementioned, this paper provides an extensive review on the main characteristics of existing DCNs as well as on the dominant technical challenges that will enable next-generation large-scale DCNs to meet the requirements of emerging data-explosive time-sensitive cloud/edge DC applications. Especially as Table [1](#page-2-0) shows, we delve into modern DCN traffic characteristics, switching technologies, bandwidth allocation/access control strategies, energy footprint, optical multiplexing technologies like SDM and disaggregated DCN architectures, aiming to derive design directions and trends suitable for performance and power efficient future optical DCNs. To the best of our knowledge, no other review or survey on optical DCNs combinedly presents all the above optical DCN topics, while none of them reviews bandwidth allocation/access control schemes suitable for optical DCNs.

This paper is organized as follows: Section [II](#page-3-0) gives the main background and basic concepts in existing DCNs while it addresses the trends for near future optical DCN technology evolution. Especially, technology evolutions in DCN optical links are introduced, whilst DCN traffic characteristics are extensively discussed. Moreover, the main design objectives of future efficient all-optical DCNs are comprehensively studied. Section [III](#page-10-0) deals with the switching technologies in DCs, focusing on the optical switching one since it can provide low end-to-end latency and high DCN performance. In specific, different optical technologies used in commercially available and research prototype optical switches for DCNs are presented. Moreover, those optical switching technologies used in current big-data companies' DCNs are lighted along with those promising technologies that are expected to be utilized in next-generation efficient optically switched DCNs. Section [IV](#page-14-0) gives a comprehensive overview on bandwidth allocation schemes and access control algorithms suitable for all-optical DCNs in both intra- and inter-rack optical domain. Different bandwidth allocation techniques are classified according to the performance enhancement provided in terms of end-to-end delay and bandwidth utilization, data contention resolution mechanism, transmission scheme, traffic pattern, medium access control (MAC) protocol etc. Section [V](#page-16-0) focuses on the DCs power consumption since it critically affects their effectiveness. Particularly, different DCN architectures and switching technologies are compared with respect to their energy footprint, providing useful comments for power efficient future optical DCNs design. The main technical challenges and open research topics regarding all-optical DCNs are discussed in Section [VI,](#page-20-0) providing research directions for next-generation efficient optical DCNs capable of effectively serving the heterogeneous traffic of multiple data-intensive DC applications. Finally, Section [VII](#page-23-0) concludes our review.

### <span id="page-3-0"></span>**II. BACKGROUND AND BASIC CONCEPTS**

With the tremendous traffic increase in DCNs, there is a constant need for the DCs operators to enhance the DCN performance in order to guarantee the required low task completion time. In this direction, considerable efforts have been put by academia and industry. Based on the latest optical technology evolutions, novel solutions have been proposed for the DCN links bandwidth upgrade, the switching speed-up, and the effectiveness of packet transmission/access control strategies, taking into account the DCN traffic diversity, burstiness and priority classes. Therefore, apart from the energy footprint and cost, other performance parameters such as DCN throughput and end-to-end latency are lately taken under consideration for the DCN performance evaluation.

### <span id="page-4-0"></span>A. LINKS

<span id="page-4-2"></span>Large-scale data companies like Google, Facebook and Microsoft have exploited the wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) technique in DCN links to enhance the total data rate provided [\[50\],](#page-25-4) [\[51\],](#page-25-5) [\[52\]. E](#page-25-6)specially, the coarse WDM (CWDM) technique has been used at the first inter-rack/ intercluster DCN links based on its low cost technology. CWDM supports in a single fiber the operation of up to 18 wavelengths with a 20 nm distance between them, while they can efficiently work up to few kilometers depending on the exact number of working wavelengths. The main disadvantage of CDWM is the amplification inability because of the large distance between wavelengths, resulting in limited data rate per wavelength. In order to achieve higher data rates using more wavelengths per link and based on improved laser frequency stability technology, the dense WDM (DWDM) technique has been proposed for DCN links [\[53\], s](#page-25-7)ince it supports amplification by optical amplifiers. Especially, DWDM can support in a single fiber the operation of many decades of different wavelengths with a smaller distance between them (0.8 and 0.4 nm distance for 48 and 96 wavelengths respectively) without any interference, providing higher data rates per wavelength and longer distance interconnection [\[54\].](#page-25-8)

<span id="page-4-5"></span><span id="page-4-3"></span>The actual data rate achieved per wavelength in a DCN is driven by the pluggable transceiver capabilities as well as by the provided bandwidth of optical interconnects. In a timeline, in 2007 Googe first transited to 10 Gb/s optical links in its DCNs for transmissions up to 200 m, using vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers (VCSELs) with multimode fiber (MMF)-based small form-factor pluggable (SFP) transceivers [\[51\]. A](#page-25-5)lmost a decade later, in 2016 Google's Jupiter DCN introduced the use of 40 Gb/s data center interconnects (DCIs) [\[55\]. A](#page-25-9)s the 100 Gb/s optical links were commercially available since 2014, their use in DCNs was adopted almost in 2018 [\[48\]. S](#page-25-2)ince the traffic provided by data-intensive AI applications grow exponentially in DCNs, the requirement for higher link data rates becomes critical. Thus, the 200 Gb/s and 400 Gb/s transceivers standards were presented in 2017 by the IEEE P802.3bs Task Force [\[56\].](#page-25-10) In 2019 experimental results on 400 Gb/s DCIs performance were also introduced [\[57\]. C](#page-25-11)urrently the 400 Gb/s technology for DCN links and switching fabric is dominant in big-data DCNs, such as those of Cisco and Google Jupiter [\[58\],](#page-25-12) [\[59\].](#page-25-13) It is evident that the evolution on optical interconnects technology is moving towards the throughput level of Tb/s in order to keep up with the requirements of massively increasing traffic in DCNs. Commercially available switching chips currently support 64 × 400G ports providing 25 Tb/s switching bandwidth [\[60\], w](#page-25-14)hile the prospects for the near future are the support of 32 × 800G ports. Moreover, since the traffic explosion due to big-data market has propelled DCNs into uncharted territory, requiring extremely high bandwidth capabilities, in 2022 IEEE 802.3ck, P802.3cn, and P802.3df Task Forces introduced relative standards to support the 800G Ethernet technology [\[61\], a](#page-25-15)fter the corresponding proposal of Optical Internetworking Forum (OIF). The introduction of <span id="page-4-13"></span><span id="page-4-12"></span><span id="page-4-11"></span>800G transmission capability in DCN links over distances up to 120 km is a milestone and allows for extremely high bandwidth DCNs based on coherent digital signal processor (DSP) technology without the need of costly DWDM systems. The optical market currently provides 800G ZR pluggable modules like [\[62\], w](#page-25-16)hile the introduction of 800G switches for large-scale DCNs is gradually being put in the agenda. Therefore, the 800G DC migration becomes a near future reality. According to the market, we expect to achieve 120 km, 500 km and 1200 km cloud interconnection range enabling coherent DSP 800G ZR, 800G ZR+, and 600G ZR pluggable modules respectively [\[63\]. M](#page-25-17)oreover, based on latest forecasts, 800G ports switches are expected to overpass 400G in demand by 2025, while big-data DC companies such as Google, Meta and Microsoft, as pioneer ones, are forecasted to immediately incorporate the 800G switching technology in their DCNs [\[64\]. D](#page-25-18)espite the relatively immature stage of current 800G technology for DCNs, market demands have already forced for the 1.6T and 3.2T transmission technology investigation to meet the ever-expanding needs for massive data transmission within DCNs with unparalleled efficiency and reliability. Indeed, in late 2022 the 1.6T standard was presented by IEEE P802.3dj Task Force [\[65\], w](#page-25-19)hile the relative for 3.2T is anticipated in the near future. Fig. [1](#page-5-0) shows the timeline of DCN link and interconnection data rate evolution. Finally, as the DCNs bandwidth constantly increases according to Fig. [1](#page-5-0) and the distance between an end-user/IoT device and the processing network in edge DCs is becoming shorter, data can be transferred faster providing more instantaneous application service.

#### <span id="page-4-15"></span><span id="page-4-14"></span><span id="page-4-4"></span><span id="page-4-1"></span>B. TRAFFIC

Traffic flows and their dependencies are among the main networking challenges when managing cloud and distributed workloads, according to the latest Cisco report [\[66\]. W](#page-25-20)ith the massive increase of traffic volume in cloud/edge DCs due to multiple data-intensive applications, efficient DCN design requires a more exploratory investigation of traffic flows characteristics.

<span id="page-4-10"></span><span id="page-4-9"></span><span id="page-4-8"></span><span id="page-4-7"></span><span id="page-4-6"></span>The term flow refers to an amount of data that has to be transferred by a source to a destination server within a DCN, related with a specific application task. A data flow is divided into a number of packets by the source server network interface card (NIC), each of which is transmitted independently as a different part of the flow. The adoption of a data flow implies that the respective packets have a sequential and logical correlation among them. Within a DCN, multiple flows originated by different pairs of communicating sourcedestination servers and corresponding to different application tasks are transferred, requiring diverse levels of quality of service (QoS). Moreover, different flows can be concurrently transmitted by a single source server to different destination servers, while each flow corresponds to a different application task of the source server, adding dynamicity to DCN traffic. <span id="page-5-3"></span>Data flows are closely related to the applications running within a DCN. The main applications in modern DCs are listed in [\[70\]](#page-25-24) and are categorized in the following classes, as shown in Fig. [2:](#page-5-1) online and real-time applications, computing applications, and storage back-up applications. In the online and real-time applications class belong time-sensitive applications such as video streaming, real-time communication, online gaming, AR/VR/IR, IoT, Industry 4.0 etc. that have strict latency constraints. Hence, the respective traffic flows must be served with the highest priority within the DCN. In the computing applications class belong bigdata applications like Hadoop, ChatGPT, ML/AI training etc. that require high availability of computing resources such as central processing units (CPUs)/GPUs and memory. The respective traffic flows are required to face low packet loss at the computing resources; therefore they can be served with medium priority. Finally, in the storage back-up applications class belong applications such as cloud storage, database update, and virtual machine (VM) migration that require high network bandwidth, while they are not sensitive to delays. Hence, the relative traffic flows can be served with low priority.

Within a DCN, traffic patterns strongly depend on the specific applications run in the DC. Knowledge of the DCN traffic patterns is pivotal for the DCN performance optimization in order to design efficient MAC and switching protocols that can ensure the required QoS level in terms of low latency. Nevertheless, for most of the big-data companies, traffic patterns are often assumed as privacy-sensitive and proprietary resulting to insufficient knowledge of traffic characteristics within DCNs. #### 1) LOCALITY

<span id="page-6-2"></span>Concerning DCN traffic locality, Cisco has reported that almost 75% of the traffic that enters a DCN is served by the DC resources and it is consumed within the DC, while almost 8% is directed to another DC and 17% is sent back to the end-user [\[78\]. I](#page-25-32)n other words, almost three quarters of the traffic that enters a DC follows the east-west communication scenario, as opposed to the rest one quarter that follows the north-south communication scenario exiting DCN after completion. This trend, which remains approximately the same with the traffic increase [\[79\], r](#page-25-33)equires high DCN performance to serve the ever-increasing traffic demands and to provide ensured low end-to-end latency response.

<span id="page-6-3"></span>Within a DCN, traffic locality also depends on the specific running applications. As previously discussed based on Microsoft reports, the intra-rack to inter-rack traffic ratio varies between 10% - 90% and 40% - 60% for university and private enterprise DCs, while it reaches 80% - 20% for commercial cloud DCs [\[68\]. I](#page-25-22)n the latest case, the high intrarack to inter-rack traffic ratio is because DC operators of cloud storage applications locate into the same rack those servers that usually exchange high amount of traffic between them.

The dependency of traffic locality on the running DC applications has also been verified by Facebook, although it reports that the vast majority of traffic stays within the intracluster domain instead of the intra-rack one. In specific, for Hadoop 80.9% of traffic is exchanged inside a cluster, while for web and cache services the respective percentages are 56.3% and 13% [\[69\]. O](#page-25-23)n the other hand, the traffic exchanged in the intra-rack domain is significantly less; 13.3% for Hadoop, 12.1% for web services and 0.2% for cache services. The overall DC traffic locality results are presented in Table [2.](#page-7-0)

#### 4) BURSTINESS

Since the prevalent transport protocol in cloud/edge DCs and generally in Internet is TCP due to the dominance of web services, e-mail, file transfer etc., DCN traffic often has highly bursty characteristics. Burstiness of TCP traffic is caused by transport control procedures in software when scheduling a large data window transmission at once like TCP slow start [\[86\]. M](#page-25-40)oreover, inherited hardware offloading features like Large Send Offload (LSO) and Microsoft Interrupt Moderation may contribute to high burstiness in DCNs, by reducing CPU utilization at high rates and processing packets in batches [\[87\].](#page-25-41)

<span id="page-8-2"></span><span id="page-8-1"></span>Bursty traffic is responsible for link congestion at the top-of-rack (ToR) switching tier of a DCN (see Fig. [4,](#page-11-0) in Section [III\)](#page-10-0), while traffic burstiness fluctuation is becoming smother when moving to the higher DCN switching tiers of aggregation and core [\[72\]. F](#page-25-26)inally, highly bursty traffic causes high buffers occupancy and consequent high dropping rate and data loss probability at the DC resources. As the level of traffic burstiness increases, both the total packet latency and task completion time increase too [\[14\],](#page-24-13) [\[88\].](#page-26-0)

<span id="page-8-3"></span>Based on the aforementioned discussion in paragraphs I) to 4), it is concluded that existing DCNs traffic matrixes have high diversity, fluctuation and burstiness while they are almost unpredictable. This fact makes hard the traffic engineering and transmission scheduling within existing DCNs, restricting the achievable level of performance improvement. Knowledge of real flows characteristics such as size and arrival rate can help DCN engineers to design effective MAC and switching schemes.

According to measurements in its DCN topology, Microsoft has reported that link utilization in the core DCN switching tier (see Fig. [4,](#page-11-0) in Section [III\)](#page-10-0) is higher than in the lower aggregation and ToR DCN switching tiers, while it decreases when moving from a higher to a lower tier [\[71\],](#page-25-25) [\[72\]. D](#page-25-26)espite low link utilization, congestion -expressed by the loss ratio- is noticed at the lower DCN tiers since congestion is mainly affected by traffic burstiness [\[72\].](#page-25-26)

### <span id="page-9-1"></span>C. MAIN DCN DESIGN OBJECTIVES

It is worth noting that a DCN does not only interconnect servers within the DC domain, but it is also responsible for the orchestration of all network operations for data transmission, switching, and on time data delivery in general. High data-intensiveness and traffic diversity put in the agenda the following demanding objectives for an effective DCN design, as Fig. [3](#page-10-1) presents:

#### 1) PERFORMANCE EFFECTIVENESS

Data-consuming cloud/edge applications running in DCNs set tight performance requirements in order to guarantee a deterministic data delivery, in terms of low and bounded endto-end latency in the order of few µs, low jitter in the order of ns, high DCN throughput (aiming to reach links capacity), and extremely low packet loss and dropping rate, while the required QoS level strongly depends on the specific application, as previously discussed. To cope with such stringent requirements, DC operators gradually upgrade the DCN links capacity up to 800G nowadays targeting to the 3.2T era in the near future, as discussed in Section [II-A.](#page-4-0) Also, they incrementally substitute the electrical switching infrastructure by optical switching one providing hybrid electrical/optical solutions in existing DCNs, while the all-optical switching technology is preferred for new DCNs deployment with additional cost, targeting to fast optical switching in the order of ns, as it is discussed in Section [III.](#page-10-0) Finally, it is anticipated that they will gradually adopt effective access schemes to serve the highly heterogeneous traffic in the intra- and interrack domain, properly exploiting the increasing DCN link bandwidth, as it is described in Section [IV.](#page-14-0) It is worth noting that the rate of DCN traffic increase is higher than the rate of adoption by DC operators of effective technology solutions, such as optical switching and efficient access schemes, resulting to decreasing DCN performance. For example, Google has announced that during previous years 2004-2012, the bisection bandwidth in its DCs increased 125% per year, since there was available bandwidth to be shared among communicating pairs of servers. This trend has dramatically changed the last years due to the tremendous traffic expansion in Google DCs, reaching only 26% [\[55\].](#page-25-9)

As a required attribute of a modern DCN, scalability does not just concern the addition of new hardware (CPUs/GPUs and memory) without affecting the existing servers communication, but also require suitable switching and routing protocols to be effectively adapted to the expanded DCN domain. In the light of scalability requirement, switching and access protocols are summarized in Section [III](#page-10-0) and [IV](#page-14-0) respectively.

### 3) RECONFIGURABILITY

High traffic dynamicity and burstiness in DCNs impose realtime network resources reconfiguration in order to guarantee the required QoS level for each application [\[90\]. A](#page-26-2)s previously discussed, within a DCN, link utilization varies depending on unpredictable offered traffic conditions. Hence, there may be links between servers, racks or clusters overloaded due to unpredictable data-consuming communication resulting to low unacceptable QoS level, while other links may be underutilized. Thus, efficient DCN resource reconfiguration mechanisms is required to be adopted for load balancing and link utilization improvement, targeting to effective delivery of required QoS level. Time granularity is an important parameter when scheduling this target achievement. In specific, as measurements show in Microsoft DCNs, traffic pattern remains stable in a time scale of tens of ms, while it is dramatically dynamic in a time scale of hundreds of ms [\[68\]. T](#page-25-22)his convinces that DCN resources are required to be fast reconfigurable in the order of few ms.

<span id="page-10-6"></span><span id="page-10-5"></span>Network traffic management schemes have been proposed based on linear programming [\[91\],](#page-26-3) or intelligent algorithms [\[92\],](#page-26-4) [\[93\]. T](#page-26-5)heir applicability to DCNs is a matter of time scale since duration of reconfiguration processing in the order of seconds or even minutes is unacceptable for real-time DCN traffic. Therefore, heuristic reconfiguration schemes have been adopted, but with limited link utilization improvement [\[94\],](#page-26-6) [\[95\]. R](#page-26-7)egarding all-optical DCNs, diverse reconfiguration methods have been introduced too [\[96\],](#page-26-8) [\[97\],](#page-26-9) <span id="page-10-10"></span><span id="page-10-8"></span><span id="page-10-7"></span>[\[98\]. Most of the proposed optical DCN reconfiguration schemes follow the SDN-paradigm for network control [\[103\].](#page-26-15) Special investigation has been conducted for optical switches fast reconfiguration to improve DCN performance [\[9\]. Pa](#page-24-8)rticularly, optical switches based on electro-optical effects achieve ns-scale reconfigurability, while the use of semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs) along with optical switches provides lossless and fast switching in the order of ns, like in [\[104\],](#page-26-16) as it is extensively discussed in Section [III-B.](#page-11-1) Finally, network virtualization mechanisms can be enabled for the efficient DCN operation and management, where DC resources such as network capacity, storage and memory are virtually assumed to slice DCN, targeting to cloud applications stringent requirements satisfaction with limited energy footprint [\[105\].](#page-26-17)

#### <span id="page-10-12"></span><span id="page-10-11"></span>4) FAULT TOLERANCE

Under often traffic congestion conditions, possible DCN infrastructure failures have to be timely resolved -if not predicted and overcome in advance- in order to support uninterrupted DCN operation. Hence, redundant physical links can be used for traffic redirection in case of link failure. Moreover, fault-tolerant switching schemes can be applied, such as hybrid circuit/packet switching approaches for electrical/optical DCNs improving network resilience [\[106\].](#page-26-18) In any case, the avoidance of a single point of failure condition is pursued [\[107\].](#page-26-19) Finally, suitable DCN topologies can be adopted to provide DCN robustness [\[108\].](#page-26-20)

### A. ELECTRICAL SWITCHING

Traditional DCNs are equipped with electrical switches in a hierarchical topology of (usually) a three-tier switching escalation (ToR, aggregation and core switching tiers), as Fig. [4](#page-11-0) shows. Servers are organized into racks, while each of them hosts some tens of servers. Each server is connected through an electrical copper cable or optical fiber to the ToR electrical switch for further inter-rack communication. ToR switches are connected through optical fibers to higher tier electrical aggregation switches, which are further connected through optical fibers to higher tier electrical core switches. This means that in the higher switching tiers data are transmitted in the optical domain while they are processed and switched in the electrical domain, incurring O/E/O conversions. Such a DCN architecture based on electrical switches suffers by high power consumption and bandwidth bottleneck at higher tiers, while this trend increases as DC scales-up [\[109\].](#page-26-21)

### <span id="page-11-5"></span><span id="page-11-1"></span>B. OPTICAL SWITCHING

<span id="page-11-11"></span>Unlike electrical switching, optical switching is transparent to bit rate and data format, providing high flexibility. This is because it does not require any O/E/O conversion at the switch ports since the switching is performed in the optical domain. In addition, it consumes considerably less power per switched bit, as compared to the electrical switching [\[121\].](#page-26-33) Moreover, it is worth noting that optical switching provides a more independent relation between the port data rate and the port count (supporting the port count increase without sacrificing the high port data rate), as compared to the electrical switching (which conditions the port data rate and count with the number of interconnected switches). This independence can provide large-scale optically switched DCN topologies avoiding limitations of hierarchical electrical switching such as oversubscription [\[24\].](#page-24-23)

<span id="page-11-12"></span><span id="page-11-10"></span><span id="page-11-9"></span><span id="page-11-8"></span><span id="page-11-7"></span>Based on the aforementioned advantages, optical switching reaches confoundedly higher switch bandwidth as compared to the electrical switching, while it can be improved even more using WDM and SDM techniques [\[122\].](#page-26-34) As an immediate result, optical switching can guarantee considerably lower latency and task completion time, while it allows for simpler DCN architectures. For these reasons, optical

### 1) OPTICAL CIRCUIT SWITCHING

<span id="page-12-1"></span><span id="page-12-0"></span>To meet the bandwidth-demanding performance requirements of modern DCNs, optical circuit switching (OSC) for DCNs is explored by diverse research projects [\[43\],](#page-24-42) [\[44\],](#page-24-43) [\[123\],](#page-26-35) [\[124\],](#page-26-36) [\[125\]](#page-26-37) and implementations [\[21\], w](#page-24-20)hile continuous attention is attracted by academia. This is because OCS can provide essential switching bandwidth enhancement with significant power consumption reduction in scalable DCs, providing even two times more cost and power efficient DC solutions [\[126\],](#page-26-38) as Table [6](#page-13-0) presents. Despite the performance limitations discussed in the following, OCS technology is currently preferred by big-data companies' DCs in order to move towards the optical switching era. For example, in 2022 Google replaced the entire spine block by MEMS-based optical circuit switches in order to reduce power consumption and benefit from data transparency [\[21\].](#page-24-20) For the OCS switch implementation different optical switching technologies can be used: either MEMS-based [\[21\],](#page-24-20) [\[43\],](#page-24-42) [\[44\],](#page-24-43) [\[125\]](#page-26-37) or AWG-based [\[123\],](#page-26-35) [\[124\].](#page-26-36)

As in circuit switching in classic electrical networks, OCS requires an optical circuit (referred as optical path) establishment prior to the actual data transmission and its release after communication conclusion. A three-way handshake setup control messaging is required between the source and the destination switch for the optical circuit establishment [\[127\],](#page-26-39) reserving certain bandwidth for a long time period exclusively for their communication. In this way, no further routing information is needed since all data from the source switch will be routed to the destination switch through the dedicated optical path.

Although set-up procedure provides a pre-defined optical path of guaranteed QoS between the source and the destination switch, it requires long set-up time (in ms order) which may be longer than path actual occupation time for transmission and propagation, especially in highly bursty traffic DCNs. For example for a short burst transmission, the optical path occupation time period (from its transmission by the source switch till its reception by the destination switch) may be much shorter than the required optical path set-up time. Therefore, in such bursty DCN traffic conditions, OCS provides fruitless time consumption for set-up, resulting in low bandwidth utilization. Moreover, the absence of commercially available optical random access memory (RAM) and optical buffer deteriorates the switching performance even more, especially under high burstiness traffic conditions, resulting in higher data dropping probability. In addition, since OCS exclusively assigns a dedicated optical path between two specific switches by avoiding any statistical data multiplexing over it, it considerably restricts the level of maximum bandwidth utilization. Although OCS is widely used in optical networks besides DCs (e.g. reconfigurable optical add/drop multiplexers (ROADMs) are assumed as optical circuit switches [\[128\]\)](#page-26-40), its applicability to future DCNs should first effectively face the aforementioned challenges in order to handle the bursty and traffic-boosting DCN conditions. Towards this direction, a comparative study of optical switches impact under different OCS integration levels (in core and/or aggregation switching tiers) is given in [\[129\].](#page-26-41)

### <span id="page-12-4"></span>2) OPTICAL PACKET SWITCHING

Optical packet switching (OPS) was aspired to be the optical analogue of electrical packet switching (EPS) which has become dominant because of its simplicity in traditional telecom networks and in nowadays electrically switched DCNs. However, OPS technology is not yet a viable solution for DCNs due to the absence of optical RAM for buffering data in the optical domain.

<span id="page-12-5"></span>OPS handles DCN traffic on a per-packet basis. According to the OPS functionality, an optical switch -by a packet arrival- extracts the packet header and converts it in the electrical domain (O/E conversion) to process it and define the output switch port according to the destination information carried by the header. Then, the packet payload is routed to the defined switch output port without any O/E/O conversion, providing high throughput and low latency depending on traffic congestion [\[130\],](#page-26-42) as Table [6](#page-13-0) shows. However, an engineering demand arises: the packet payload is required to be properly delayed and stored at a switch buffer as long as its header is being processed for routing purposes. Nevertheless, the immature technology of optical buffers and delay lines cannot support such a functionality yet. Despite technology immaturity, the research community has paid important efforts to adopt OPS in future DCNs by addressing effective contention resolution scenarios [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[133\].](#page-27-2)

<span id="page-12-6"></span><span id="page-12-2"></span>Based on the per-packet transmission scheme, OPS can support statistical data multiplexing over the same wavelength or using WDM technique, allowing for effective bandwidth sharing among different packet flows. Therefore, flexibility can be provided, especially under bursty traffic conditions. In order to meet the OPS stringent time requirements for switching control, fast optical switches in the order of ns switching reconfiguration time have been explored benefiting from the arrayed waveguide grating router (AWGR) [\[134\],](#page-27-3) [\[135\]](#page-27-4) and SOA [\[136\],](#page-27-5) [\[137\]](#page-27-6) technology, as it is discussed at the end of Section [III.](#page-10-0) In this framework, OPS contention resolution schemes require fiber delay lines (FDLs) or appropriate wavelength conversion with the cost of high power consumption, as presented in Table [6.](#page-13-0)

#### 4) TECHNOLOGIES FOR OPTICAL SWITCHING

Several technologies have been proposed for optical switching in DCNs. Among them, MEMS, liquid crystal on silicon (LCoS) and beam steering have been commercially available, while optical switches based on MEMS seems to be the most mature technology.

<span id="page-13-4"></span><span id="page-13-3"></span>Free space MEMS-based switches can be found either in a two-dimensions (2D) or three-dimensions (3D) implementation. 2D-MEMS switching is based on crossbar topology where micro-mirrors are properly used to steer optical beams to the output switch ports, while 3D-MEMS switching is based on two stages of 2D micro-mirror arrays to steer the optical beams. On the one hand, 2D-MEMS provide short switching (reconfiguration) time in the sub-µs order, while they provide low cross talk and insertion loss [\[142\],](#page-27-11) [\[143\],](#page-27-12) but limited port count up to 64. On the other hand, 3D-MEMS can support large-scale cross-connection [\[144\],](#page-27-13) up to 576 port counts [\[145\],](#page-27-14) but they provide long switching time in the order of tens of ms [\[146\].](#page-27-15) The support of a few hundreds of switch ports with 3D-MEMS can be realized [\[147\],](#page-27-16) but it is a matter of cost due to the added complexity. MEMS-based switching technology is the dominant current trend in order to enhance switching capability by saving energy and increasing transparency in existing DCNs. For this reason, Google has recently replaced spine blocks by MEMS-based optical circuit switches in its Jupiter DCN, accepting a few weeks reconfiguration period and few ms switching time [\[21\].](#page-24-20)

<span id="page-13-10"></span><span id="page-13-9"></span><span id="page-13-8"></span><span id="page-13-7"></span><span id="page-13-6"></span><span id="page-13-5"></span><span id="page-13-2"></span>Apart from 3D-MEMS, there are also other optical switching technologies that provide relatively slow switching time varying from ms to µs. In specific, optical switches based on LCoS technology respond with switching time of tens of ms [\[147\],](#page-27-16) [\[148\].](#page-27-17) Moreover, free space optics switching based on piezoelectric actuators [\[149\]](#page-27-18) provides switching time in the order of tens of ms too, with commercially available port count of 384. In addition, micro-ring resonator (MRR) technology is used for optical switching using the thermo-optic effect, with switching time of few µs [\[150\].](#page-27-19) Mach-Zehnder

![](_page_14_Picture_1.jpeg)

<span id="page-14-4"></span>interferometer (MZI) has also been used [\[151\],](#page-27-20) [\[152\],](#page-27-21) while MZI-based optical switches is compactly fabricated using silicon photonics technology. Depending on the material, MZI-based optical switches have tens or hundreds of µs switching time for silicon or silica glass correspondingly. Although switching time can be reduced exploiting physical cladding [\[153\]](#page-27-22) or heater driving [\[154\]](#page-27-23) dedicated methods, it cannot be lower than a few µs. Further switching time decrease in the order of some tens of ns can be achieved by using silicon photonics and electro-optic effect instead of thermo-optic one [\[155\],](#page-27-24) [\[156\],](#page-27-25) [\[157\],](#page-27-26) while polarization dependencies [\[158\]](#page-27-27) should be effectively faced.

<span id="page-14-8"></span><span id="page-14-7"></span>Moreover, optical switching based on wavelength routing has been proposed since it is simple enough. Indeed, wavelength routing requires wavelength selective (WS) components like AWGs [\[155\],](#page-27-24) [\[159\]](#page-27-28) to interconnect switch ports by changing the incoming wavelength to a different outcoming one, often using tunable lasers. Despite the gradual optical switching integration, the providing switching time by the commercially available free-space optical switches is still in the order of ms, thus short-lived bursty traffic cannot be effectively served. It is obvious that future DCNs have to provide fast optical switching reconfigurability in the order of ns to support data-intensive traffic of high burstiness, providing high scalability. ### <span id="page-14-0"></span>**IV. BANDWIDTH ALLOCATION IN OPTICAL DCNs**

In order to effectively serve the bursty and highly variable traffic in DCs, several bandwidth allocation schemes suitable for optical DCNs have been introduced by academia, for both the intra- and inter-rack DCN domain. It is worth noted that a bandwidth allocation scheme for intra-rack DCNs determines the transmission rules (i.e. intra-rack MAC protocol) followed by the servers of the same rack for their communication, while that one for inter-rack DCNs defines the network resources (i.e. wavelengths, switches ports, lightpaths etc.) allocation for the communication among different ToRs. In both cases, bandwidth allocation schemes aim to effectively utilize the available bandwidth determining effective access and switching rules for the communicating servers/ToRs, expecting to reach optimal performance, i.e. low end-to-end latency in the order of few µs, high throughput, and low data dropping rate due to buffers' overflow.

### <span id="page-14-2"></span>A. BANDWIDTH ALLOCATION FOR INTRA-RACK COMMUNICATION

<span id="page-14-11"></span>Although in most of the existing intra-rack DCNs the communication between servers is implemented over electrical copper cables and the intra-rack switching is performed by electrical switches, there is an intense research investigation about MAC protocols dedicated for optical intra-rack servers' communication, as promising solutions for near future efficient optical intra-rack DCNs [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[163\],](#page-27-32) [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\],](#page-27-36) [\[168\],](#page-27-37) [\[169\],](#page-27-38) [\[170\].](#page-27-39) Table [7](#page-17-0) (upper part) presents a survey of relative optical intra-rack DCN MAC protocols. As it is shown, in the optical intra-rack DCN domain servers' interconnection is performed by a passive optical interconnect (POI), i.e. either by a AWGR [\[163\]](#page-27-32) or by a passive optical coupler [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\],](#page-27-36) [\[168\],](#page-27-37) [\[169\]](#page-27-38) or by a pair of passive optical combiner and splitter [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[170\],](#page-27-39) to reduce power consumption. Since the AWGR-based solution is heavily dependent on the specific used wavelengths [\[163\],](#page-27-32) it essentially restricts network flexibility and effectiveness. For this reason, POIs based on a passive optical coupler or on a pair of optical combiner and splitter are preferred for the intra-rack servers' interconnection to resolve the above drawback [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\],](#page-27-36) [\[168\],](#page-27-37) since they are wavelength independent.

According to Table [7,](#page-17-0) MAC protocols for optical intrarack (and inter-rack) DCNs are categorized according to: 1) the access control scheme (centralized or distributed), 2) the transmission strategy (synchronous or asynchronous), 3) the MAC protocol mechanism for contention resolution

![](_page_15_Picture_1.jpeg)

(pre-transmission coordination (PTC), carrier sensing (CS), based on control messages (CM) or based on announcement and confirmation messages (A&CM)), 4) the success of total collision avoidance (yes/no), 5) the offered traffic assumption (packet-based/flow-based), and 6) the offered traffic size and interarrival time distributions.

In specific, regarding the access control in the optical intrarack DCN domain, it can be performed either distributely by each separate server [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[168\],](#page-27-37) [\[169\]](#page-27-38) or centralized by a central controller usually hosted at the ToR infrastructure or at a dedicated central server [\[163\],](#page-27-32) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\].](#page-27-36) In the distributed access control case, each server is responsible to run the MAC algorithm in order to define its own transmission rights according to the mutually followed by all servers access rules. On the contrary in the centralized access control case, the central controller acts as an SDN controller and it is responsible: a) to collect from all servers their control information about their intention for data transmission, b) to centrally run the MAC algorithm based on the received control information in order to define the data transmission rights of each separate server, and c) to announce to all servers the allocated data transmission rights by sending to them appropriate control information. It is evident that the centralized access control scheme suffers from the risk of a single-point of failure [\[69\]](#page-25-23) and it lacks network flexibility, while it requires longer time to provide data transmission rights to each server. On the other hand, the distributed access control scheme provides a completely server-based bandwidth allocation algorithm running, eliminating the risk of a single-point of failure and mitigating delay overhead [\[69\]. T](#page-25-23)herefore, although the SDN paradigm is often proposed for next generation optical DCNs for central/administrative control provision, the distributed access control scheme gains ground in latest intra-rack DCN solutions, as Table [7](#page-17-0) shows.

Irrespective of the access control scheme, MAC protocols for optical intra-rack DCNs may follow either synchronous [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\]](#page-27-36) or asynchronous transmission strategy [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[163\],](#page-27-32) [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[168\],](#page-27-37) [\[169\].](#page-27-38) In the former case, it is assumed that time axis is divided in contiguous time periods, called as cycles or slots, while the rack servers can attempt transmission only at the beginning of a time cycle. On the contrary in the latest case, a server may attempt transmission at any time instant. It is worth mentioning that the synchronous transmission strategy may achieve higher throughput as compared to the asynchronous one, since it allows the adoption of efficient MAC protocols such as PTC to totally avoid collisions [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\],](#page-27-36) but it unavoidably necessitates a time synchronization mechanism to be followed by all servers increasing both the capital expenditure (capex) and the operating expenses (opex). On the other hand, the asynchronous transmission strategy is more flexible and less costly since no synchronization mechanism is required [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[163\],](#page-27-32) [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[168\],](#page-27-37) [\[169\].](#page-27-38)

Regarding the contention resolution mechanism proposed for optical intra-rack DCN MAC protocols, the PTC mechanism has been adopted by both synchronous [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\]](#page-27-36) and asynchronous [\[163\]](#page-27-32) WDM access (WDMA) transmission strategies irrespectively of the distributed [\[131\]](#page-27-0) or centralized [\[165\],](#page-27-34) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\]](#page-27-36) access control scheme, in order to totally avoid data loss due to collisions. This is because the PTC mechanism allows servers to announce in advance their intention for data transmission i.e. by transmitting appropriate control information prior to the actual data packet transmission. In this way, all servers (for the distributed access control scheme) or the central controller (for the centralized access control one) collect the transmission requests in advance and are able to appropriately coordinate the data transmissions guaranteeing collisions elimination. On the other hand, the contention resolution mechanism based on the A&CM strategy [\[164\]](#page-27-33) was proposed almost a decade ago for an asynchronous transmission intrarack MAC protocol, aiming to reduce the number of data collisions but without managing their total avoidance. On the contrary, the contention resolution mechanism based on the effective control messages (CM) exchange among servers prior to the data transmission in order to provide collision-free communication rises as a promising solution especially for asynchronous transmission strategies, despite the nature of the transmission that lacks synchronization and incommodes transmission coordination [\[168\].](#page-27-37) Finally, carrier sensing (CS) mechanisms have also gained ground for distributed access control and asynchronous transmission intra-rack MAC protocols. Although they apply effective transmission scheduling strategies in order to reduce data collisions, they reach limited performance due to the unavoidable collisions effect [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[169\].](#page-27-38) Therefore, based on the aforementioned discussion about contention resolution, it is concluded that in optical intra-rack DCNs effective contention resolution and total collisions elimination can be achieved by adopting appropriate pre-transmission control coordination mechanisms such as PTC, but paying the unavoidable delay overhead cost.

In addition, key role to the effective intra-rack MAC protocol design plays the realistic assumption of the DCN traffic characteristics. As discussed in Section [II-B](#page-4-1) and especially in Table [3](#page-7-1) and Table [5,](#page-9-0) according to real traffic measurements acquired by Microsoft's [\[68\],](#page-25-22) [\[72\]](#page-25-26) and Facebook's [\[69\]](#page-25-23) DCs as well as according to synthetic DC traffic patterns [\[77\],](#page-25-31) DCN traffic (packets or flows) is characterized by high variability in interarrival time (providing different levels of burstiness), size, and duration depending on the specific DC applications served. Therefore, intra-rack MAC protocols that are designed to serve traffic of variable size packets (64/1500 Bytes [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[165\],](#page-27-34) 200/1500 Bytes [\[163\],](#page-27-32) 200/1400 Bytes [\[168\]\)](#page-27-37) or flows (mice/elephant [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\]\)](#page-27-36) adopt a more realistic assumption about the offered traffic as compared to those MAC protocols that assume conventional traffic of a single packet size (1024 Bytes [\[164\],](#page-27-33) 1500 Bytes [\[169\]\)](#page-27-38), providing more effective performance results. Hence, the consideration of traffic variability in size, interarrival time and duration is of critical importance for the effective intra-rack MAC protocol design and applicability.

Since the vast majority of MAC protocols for optical intrarack DCNs have been introduced with limited experimental validation, their performance evaluation is mainly based on simulation results. Therefore, the distributions considered for the offered traffic characteristics, i.e. interarrival time, size, and duration, are critical enough for the MAC protocol performance assessment. In specific, the DCN traffic burstiness can be effectively simulated by Pareto distributed ON/OFF transmission periods duration over the network [\[163\],](#page-27-32) [\[168\].](#page-27-37) On the contrary, exponentially distributed traffic interarrival times [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\],](#page-27-1) [\[164\],](#page-27-33) [\[166\],](#page-27-35) [\[167\],](#page-27-36) [\[169\]](#page-27-38) give a less realistic traffic assumption since they provide conventional traffic patterns that they do not conform to modern DCs traffic variability and burstiness. Finally, other distributions such as lognormal and Weibull can be used to simulate different traffic classes in order to provide services diversity [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\].](#page-27-1) It is obvious that different traffic priority QoS categories assumption enhances the validation of DCN simulation model results [\[131\],](#page-27-0) [\[132\]](#page-27-1) and are preferred for modern DCNs performance evaluation. ### B. BANDWIDTH ALLOCATION FOR INTER-RACK COMMUNICATION

Unlike optical intra-rack DCNs which mainly use POIs, optical inter-rack DCNs employ optical switches. Interrack bandwidth allocation schemes aim to effectively (re)configure the switches resources in order to route the lightpaths carrying the inter-rack traffic from the source to the destination ToR.

<span id="page-16-1"></span>The first DCN prototypes employing hybrid electrical/optical switching technology propose MEMS-based optical switches with long reconfiguration time (in µs or ms order) for the optical switching part [\[43\],](#page-24-42) [\[44\],](#page-24-43) [\[171\],](#page-27-40) [\[172\],](#page-27-41) [\[173\].](#page-28-0) Most of them follow the SDN paradigm requiring significant processing overhead with additional processing delay [\[43\],](#page-24-42) [\[44\],](#page-24-43) [\[172\],](#page-27-41) [\[173\].](#page-28-0) Therefore, they are only suitable for traffic with low variability such as that of cloud data storage DCNs [\[172\].](#page-27-41) On the contrary, all-optical DCN prototypes pursue quicker switching time and lower energy footprint. Some of them use MEMS-based switches reaching switching time in the order of µs [\[47\],](#page-25-1) [\[125\],](#page-26-37) while others use WS switches (WSS) with the cost of limited flexibility and latency performance [\[174\],](#page-28-1) [\[175\],](#page-28-2) [\[176\].](#page-28-3) Moreover, other all-optical inter-rack DCN prototypes employ AWGR-based ToRs interconnection aiming to reach faster performance, but their performance and scalability are limited due to AWGRs dependance on the used wavelengths and port radix [\[45\],](#page-24-44) [\[134\],](#page-27-3) [\[135\],](#page-27-4) [\[177\],](#page-28-4) [\[178\].](#page-28-5) In this framework, Sirius prototype has been introduced by Microsoft research group employing tunable lasers and AWGR wavelength routing mechanisms[\[124\].](#page-26-36) Recent DCN prototypes and experimental testbeds exploit the SOA-based optical switching paradigm to provide fast switching time in ns order [\[92\],](#page-26-4) [\[179\],](#page-28-6) [\[180\].](#page-28-7) Finally, optical passive coupler-based disaggregated <span id="page-16-5"></span>inter-rack DCN prototypes have been introduced in [\[124\]](#page-26-36) and [\[181\]](#page-28-8) achieving sub-µs scale synchronous transmission scheduling and up to 93% throughput.

Regarding the MAC protocols and bandwidth allocation schemes for ToR-to-ToR communication in optical interrack DCNs, the synchronous transmission strategy is mainly followed [\[133\],](#page-27-2) [\[182\],](#page-28-9) [\[183\],](#page-28-10) [\[184\],](#page-28-11) [\[185\],](#page-28-12) [\[186\],](#page-28-13) [\[187\],](#page-28-14) [\[188\],](#page-28-15) as Table [6](#page-13-0) (lower part) presents. This means that extra synchronization cost is required to be spent in order for the ToRs transmissions to be appropriately coordinated in the interrack DCN domain, totally avoiding collisions [\[133\],](#page-27-2) [\[182\],](#page-28-9) [\[183\],](#page-28-10) [\[184\],](#page-28-11) [\[185\],](#page-28-12) [\[186\],](#page-28-13) [\[187\],](#page-28-14) [\[188\].](#page-28-15) This is achieved by additionally using suitable contention resolution mechanisms such as PTC [\[133\],](#page-27-2) [\[182\],](#page-28-9) [\[183\],](#page-28-10) [\[184\],](#page-28-11) [\[185\],](#page-28-12) [\[186\]](#page-28-13) or routing and wavelength assignment (RWA) algorithms based on wavelength switching matrixes (WSM) [\[187\],](#page-28-14) [\[188\],](#page-28-15) as Table [6](#page-13-0) shows. Moreover, in the inter-rack DCN domain the optical interconnection among ToRs can be realized using either WSS [\[182\],](#page-28-9) [\[183\],](#page-28-10) [\[184\],](#page-28-11) [\[185\],](#page-28-12) [\[186\]](#page-28-13) or AWG [\[133\],](#page-27-2) [\[187\],](#page-28-14) [\[188\]](#page-28-15) reaching low-latency (in µs order) performance. In any case, the ToRs' access control may be performed either centrally following the SDN paradigm [\[182\],](#page-28-9) [\[183\],](#page-28-10) [\[184\],](#page-28-11) [\[185\],](#page-28-12) [\[186\]](#page-28-13) or in a distributed way [\[133\],](#page-27-2) [\[187\],](#page-28-14) [\[188\],](#page-28-15) each with the advantages and disadvantages discussed in Section [IV-A.](#page-14-2) Finally, it is worth noting that the extended server-to-server communication along the whole intra- and inter-rack optical DCN path can be also explored, like in [\[133\]](#page-27-2) and [\[188\]](#page-28-15) where distributed control, synchronous transmission and WSM contention resolution strategies for diverse traffic priority classes are assumed (while for each case the benefits and drawbacks have been discussed in Section [IV-A\)](#page-14-2), reaching low latency in µs order.

Although the aforementioned experimental prototypes of fast optically switched DCNs are still only research-based solutions and not commercially available yet, there is an accelerated interest by both industry and academia about them targeting to even lower optical switching time in the ns scale and lower end-to-end latency in the few-µs scale to enhance the performance of near future optical DCNs.

#### B. ML/AI IMPACT ON DC POWER CONSUMPTION

<span id="page-18-4"></span><span id="page-18-0"></span>Since almost 50% of the energy is consumed by servers and network equipment within a DC [189], the advent of ML/AI era provides forecasts for explosive increase that reaches even to 160% of DCs power demands due to the extremely high workloads [199]. In this line, energy demand of DCs serving AI traffic has been forecasted to more than triple during the decade 2020 to 2030, reaching more than 1000 TWh with annual increase of almost 200 TWh [200]. To understand the reason why this will happen, let's think for example for a single ChatGPT query that requires 2.9 Wh as compared with 0.3 Wh required for a Google search, according to International Energy Agency (IEA) [201] and Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) [202] reports. Therefore, while in existing DCs a rack typically consumes 10-14 kW, this value is expected to rise to 40-60 kW in racks hosting multiple power-hungry GPUs dedicated to serve AI traffic [203]. This is because GPUs consume up to 15 times more power than the conventional CPUs, requiring much more cooling. For this reason, the DCs total power consumption in USA is considered to reach 35 GW by 2030, almost double as compared to 17 GW in 2022 [204]. Similar predictions have been adopted for the 27 countries of European Union (EU-27), according which DCs consumed 1.8%-2.6% of the total energy consumption in EU-27 in 2022, reaching 45-65 TWh [205]. Furthermore, based on the prediction that by 2025 65% of the EU-27 DCs will be hyperscale ones to serve AI traffic, while enterprise DCs will be limited even more and will account for 35% [205], the DCs total power consumption level is expected to rise even more. In this content, big data companies have seen their energy footprint to significantly increase the last years. For example, Meta (Facebook) DCs energy consumption has quadrupled from 2016 to 2020 reaching 7.2 TWh [206], while in Google DCs the energy consumption increase per year was 22% from 2017 to 2021 reaching 18.6 TWh [207]. Similar trends have been observed for Amazon and Microsoft DCs for the same time period [208]. For this reason, renewable energy procurement has already been incorporated mainly from solar and wind energy to move towards sustainable DCs [201].

## <span id="page-18-9"></span><span id="page-18-6"></span>C. DCN ARCHITECTURE IMPACT ON POWER CONSUMPTION

<span id="page-18-10"></span>Under the light of the energy footprint reduction demand, the impact of different DCN architectures on the total DCN power consumption is of crucial importance. In the following, a comparison of some of the most commonly used DCN architectures, such as hierarchical tree-tier, fat-tree, BCube and DCell is provided regarding their energy footprint since they use different number of switch tiers and links among the switches. Table 8 gives the power consumption of the above mentioned DCN architectures as a close mathematical formula [209], taking into account only the power consumed by the switches and the servers that have additional ports, while the power consumption of servers hardware, cooling, lighting etc. is neglected. In Table 8 the parameters E and  $E_{SW}$  stand

for the power consumption of a single server and a switch respectively. Moreover, the power consumption is given as a function of the number n of ports that a server or switch can have and the number k of the DCN architecture levels/tiers. For a fair comparison among the different DCN architectures and for the calculation of the power consumption they provide according to Table 8 formulas, commercially available electrical switches have been adopted, while optical switching technology has not been incorporated.

<span id="page-18-5"></span><span id="page-18-3"></span><span id="page-18-2"></span><span id="page-18-1"></span>In order to understand the close mathematical formulas of Table 8, let's study separately each DCN architecture. For each architecture, Table 8 also gives -apart from the power consumption- the total number  $N_{sw}$  of switches and  $N_s$  of servers. First, the hierarchical tree-tier DCN architecture of k tiers has a single switch at the upper tier (core). All switches (both at the upper and the intermediate tiers) have the same number n of ports. The total number of switches in the architecture is  $N_{sw}$  given in Table 8, whilst they consume power equal to  $E_{sw}N_{sw}$ . The servers are hosted at the tree leaves, each connected to a single switch. Therefore, the total number of servers is  $N_s = n^k$  while their total power consumption is  $En^k$ . Second, the fat-tree topology, also known as Clos topology, has three tiers. The upper tier (core) has  $(n/2)^2$  switches, each with n ports [209]. The medium tier has n pods, each with two sub-tiers of n/2 switches i.e. each pod has n switches of n ports each. The higher sub-tier switches are connected to the core switches, whilst the lower sub-tier switches are connected to the servers. This means that each pod has nhosts. The total number of servers in the architecture is  $N_s$  =  $n^{3}/4$  while their total power consumption is  $E(n^{3}/4)$  [209]. Third, DCell DCN architecture is recursively constructed. The smallest DCell<sub>0</sub> block has a single switch of n ports and connects n servers. In the architecture, switches are hosted only at the lower tier. This means that servers contribute in routing while each of them has k + 1 ports. Finally, BCube DCN architecture is also recursively constructed. BCube<sub>k</sub> block is constructed by n BCube $_{k-1}$  blocks and  $n^k$  switches each with n ports. Therefore, the architecture can host a total number  $N_s = n^{k+1}$  of servers, while the total number  $N_s$  of switches is given in Table 8.

<span id="page-18-8"></span><span id="page-18-7"></span>Based on the formulas of Table 8, in small-scale DCs of up to 2500 servers, BCube and DCell architectures consume almost the same power. Nevertheless, for large-scale DCs with more than 5000 servers, DCell architecture achieves higher energy efficiency than BCube one. For example, for the BCube architecture with 4096 servers the power consumption has been calculated 73.7 kW, while for the DCell architecture with many more servers, i.e. 5256 servers, the power consumption is much lower, i.e. 55.2 kW [209]. This is because in BCube architecture, servers are not only hosts but they also act as relay nodes for each other, while they can forward traffic between any pair of servers through multiple short paths providing efficient one-to-one and many-to-many communication. For this reason, they require additional network interfaces providing in this way higher power consumption, especially as the number  $N_s$  of servers increases.

### <span id="page-19-12"></span><span id="page-19-2"></span>D. DIRECTIONS FOR DCN POWER CONSUMPTION REDUCTION

Since optical network technology arises as the dominant one for next generation DCNs, an intensive interest has been invested by both academia and industry to improve their energy efficiency, while guaranteeing high bandwidth, low latency, and high reliability.

### <span id="page-19-3"></span>1) DEEPENING OF OPTICAL/ELECTRICAL SWITCHING INTEGRATION

<span id="page-19-14"></span><span id="page-19-13"></span><span id="page-19-9"></span><span id="page-19-6"></span><span id="page-19-4"></span>Crucial role for the DCN power consumption decrease plays the deepening of optical/electrical switching integration, i.e. the replacement of electrical switches that require power consuming O/E/O conversions by optical switches, with an unavoidable capex increase. The initial predictions were about even 75% of energy saving by transitioning towards the all-optical DCNs that keep signal in the optical domain throughout the network [\[221\].](#page-29-9) Energy efficient DCN design approaches adopt the gradual reduction of the number of active components. In this regard, in 2022 Google replaced in Jupiter DC the entire spine block by MEMS-based optical circuit switches, achieving 41% power consumption reduction along with transparency increase [\[21\]. I](#page-24-20)n this direction, hybrid optical switching schemes can be followed in optical DCNs, using on the one hand OCS technology for long data transfers delivering high energy efficiency due to the low circuit reconfiguration needs and on the other hand OPS technology for short data transfers with higher switching and energy demands [\[222\].](#page-29-10)

#### 2) SILICON PHOTONICS SWITCHING TECHNOLOGY

<span id="page-20-3"></span>Given the requirement for energy efficiency in conjunction with low cost and high bandwidth, the technology preferred for large scale optical switch fabrics is that of silicon photonics fabricated in high-volume complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) compatible foundries [\[223\],](#page-29-11) [\[224\].](#page-29-12) In silicon-integrated optical switches the MRR- and MZIbased technologies are dominant. They can both use either electro-optic (E-O) or thermo-optic (T-O) phase shift mechanism [\[225\].](#page-29-13) The E-O phase shift mechanism reaches higher energy efficiency and switching speed, whilst the T-O one can provide smaller size optical switch [\[226\].](#page-29-14) On the one hand, the MRR structure reaches low power consumption, wavelength selectivity and small size. Since it is a mature technology, it has been recently proposed for diverse large scale silicon-integrated optical switches [\[227\],](#page-29-15) [\[228\].](#page-29-16) In opposition to the MRR structure, the MZI one provides higher optical bandwidth whilst it is less sensitive to temperature variations. For this reason it has been introduced in latest studies for silicon-integrated optical switches [\[229\],](#page-29-17) [\[230\],](#page-29-18) [\[231\],](#page-29-19) [\[232\].](#page-29-20) In recent research, the technology for silicon-integrated optical switches further incorporating phase change materials (PCM) gains ground, in order to achieve higher energy efficiency in DCs, smaller size and lower insertion loss [\[233\],](#page-29-21) [\[234\],](#page-29-22) [\[235\].](#page-29-23) The most commonly used PCMs in siliconintegrated optical switches are VO<sup>2</sup> and Ge2Sb2Te<sup>5</sup> (GST), while their phase can be changed by heating, electrical and optical triggers [\[236\],](#page-29-24) [\[237\],](#page-29-25) [\[238\].](#page-29-26) In a silicon-integrated optical switch with PCMs, the structure used is either MRR or waveguide. ### <span id="page-20-21"></span><span id="page-20-20"></span><span id="page-20-19"></span><span id="page-20-18"></span><span id="page-20-14"></span><span id="page-20-0"></span>**VI. FUTURE DIRECTIONS IN OPTICAL DCNs RESEARCH**

<span id="page-20-16"></span>Despite recent research results and prototypes about many aspects of optical DCNs deployment, there are still open research topics that require further investigation in order to reach high DCN effectiveness to serve the bursty and highly variable DC traffic. Although there are recent optical components technology evolutions that can provide improved optical switching reconfigurability in the ns order, the total performance enhancement of optical DCNs cannot be guaranteed if other networking requirements such as flexibility, scalability and differentiated service of variable and

<span id="page-20-17"></span>

![](_page_21_Picture_1.jpeg)

priority-based DC traffic are not additionally supported. The most challenging open research topics for next-generation optical DCNs as well as their limitations are summarized in the following paragraphs.

### A. NS SWITCHING CONTROL

It is worth mentioning that the discussion in Section [III-B](#page-11-1) about recent fast optical switching achievements in the ns scale only refers to the actual fast optical path switching between two specific ports of the optical switch. In other words, it is assumed that the decision about the switching between these specific switch ports has been taken in a previous time instant and it is not counted in the switching time calculation. It is obvious that this decision requires the suitable processing of control information about the destination of the switched data. In order for the total optical switch response time to be restricted in ns scale for fast switching, the switch control for destination information processing must be also performed within a few ns [\[260\].](#page-30-4) Up to now, the technology for switching control and required information processing is not mature enough to provide them within a few ns in a scalable and efficient way. This restriction gets worse the realization of optically switched DCNs with packet granularity since they additionally require synchronization in the ns scale. Thus, fast and efficient switching control in ns scale remains an open research request.

### B. FAST CLOCK DATA RECOVERY

<span id="page-21-1"></span>In order for packet-based bursty traffic optical DCNs to achieve efficient performance in terms of latency, fast CDR within few ns is required to be provided by the optical receivers. If fast CDR is not supported, the total DCN performance deteriorates since throughput reduces and latency increases, especially under short-size bursty traffic conditions as in modern DCNs [\[261\].](#page-30-5) Nowadays, CDR in ns scale is achieved by burst-mode receivers [\[262\]](#page-30-6) only in low data rates. Since near future optical DCNs will enable high data rate operation at 800 Gbps or even higher per wavelength, as mentioned in Section [II-A,](#page-4-0) fast CDR in ns scale for such high data rates is required, being a hot challenge for optical networks engineers.

### C. OPTICAL BUFFERING

As mentioned in Section [III-B,](#page-11-1) OPS is not currently a realistic analogue of EPS in optical DCNs since no optical buffering is available due to technology restrictions. Therefore, optically transmitted traffic within an optical DCN cannot be effectively stored in the optical domain upon its arrival at an optical switch, as long as the appropriate control information processing is taking place for switching control. This fact results to high data loss rate and dropping probability especially under highly bursty traffic, as mentioned in Section [III-B.](#page-11-1) Although some alternatives such as FDLs and wavelength conversion techniques have been introduced to resolve the high dropping rate, they have limited applicability in large-scale DCNs due to the increasing complexity and high queuing delay (that burdens even more the end-to-end latency), respectively. Thus, optical buffering is a pivotal challenge for efficient optical DCNs.

#### D. BANDWIDTH & ACCESS CONTROL EFFICIENCY

The lack of optical buffering to store packets in the optical domain without requiring O/E/O conversion in conjunction with the absence of fast switching control and CDR as well as with the stringent synchronization requirement especially for bursty traffic service, have a substantial effect on the total performance of nowadays optical DCNs. This is because they impose strict packet contention resolution mechanisms and bandwidth allocation schemes to achieve optimum traffic congestion control and transmission performance due to technology limitations. As already discussed in Section [IV,](#page-14-0) efficient access control and resource allocation schemes aim to provide guaranteed high DCN bandwidth utilization, extremely low end-to-end latency (in a few µs) and task completion time, while ensuring for low (if not zero) packet loss and data dropping probability, taking into account the traffic burstiness, variability and QoS priority classification in modern DCNs. Therefore, it remains an open research topic.

#### <span id="page-21-0"></span>E. SELF-DRIVEN DCN RECONFIGUARBILITY

As mentioned in Section [II-C,](#page-9-1) high reconfigurability and adaptability to the variable traffic conditions is required for next generation optical DCNs, in order for them to efficiently serve the intensive bursty traffic. In specific, this flexibility is required for both the optical switches reconfiguration and the bandwidth allocation schemes on-time adaptability according to the varying traffic demands. ML/AI can significantly support such challenging achievements. In other words, both optical switch and network resources reconfiguration can benefit from ML/AI techniques to provide effective switching and access control and management in the optical domain. In this way, self-driven optical DCN reconfiguration according to the varying DC traffic needs is achieved, avoiding the currently required human intervention.

<span id="page-21-8"></span><span id="page-21-7"></span><span id="page-21-6"></span><span id="page-21-5"></span><span id="page-21-4"></span><span id="page-21-3"></span><span id="page-21-2"></span>In this direction, multiple ML techniques have been already developed to enhance DCN decision-making ability and intelligence. They aim to forecast various DCN performance parameters values as a-priori knowledge in order to improve DCN automated reconfiguration ability in case of DCN scaling up and ever-changing traffic conditions which provide high complexity and difficulty in network optimization. This is achieved by adjusting appropriate learning strategies to grasp DCN traffic characteristics in advance and to appropriately reconfigure DCN to provide optimum performance (maximum bandwidth utilization, and minimum latency and data loss), as well as power efficiency. More specifically, DCN design and operation can benefit from such ML/AI-based algorithms about flow prediction [\[263\],](#page-30-7) [\[264\],](#page-30-8) flow classification [\[265\],](#page-30-9) load balancing [\[266\],](#page-30-10) [\[267\],](#page-30-11) [\[268\],](#page-30-12) resource management [\[269\],](#page-30-13) [\[270\],](#page-30-14) [\[271\],](#page-30-15) routing optimization [\[254\],](#page-29-42) [\[266\],](#page-30-10) [\[272\],](#page-30-16) [\[273\],](#page-30-17) network topology [\[274\],](#page-30-18) congestion control [\[258\],](#page-30-2) [\[262\]](#page-30-6) etc. to optimize DCN

<span id="page-22-16"></span><span id="page-22-15"></span>![](_page_22_Picture_1.jpeg)

performance. Despite the considerable efforts, there are still many challenges for optical DCNs intelligence improvement, especially for online real-time dynamic reconfiguration ability under heterogeneous traffic and applications.

<span id="page-22-2"></span>For the learning model training, suitable previous data knowledge and relative datasets should be acquired. In this framework, the adoption of a knowledge and intelligence higher management DCN plane [\[276\],](#page-30-19) could be beneficial to determine the optimum reconfiguration scheme for high performance, low latency, low energy consumption, and high fault tolerance. For example, an optical DCN reconfiguration procedure has lately been proposed in [\[277\]](#page-30-20) exploiting ML techniques based on monitored datasets. Moreover, self-supervised learning techniques have been explored for reconfigurable optical DCNs [\[278\],](#page-30-21) while transfer learning as a part of self-supervised learning technique has also been investigated [\[279\].](#page-30-22) Concluding, although ML/AI is mainly used for DC traffic pattern prediction [\[280\]](#page-30-23) for optimization purposes, strong emphasis could be placed to use ML/AI to additionally adopt self-driven optically reconfigurable DCNs according to the constantly increasing and highly varying traffic demands.

#### <span id="page-22-4"></span><span id="page-22-3"></span>F. SPACE DIVISION MULTIPLEXING IN DCNs

<span id="page-22-7"></span>The explosive increase of traffic in DCNs due to ML/AI dominance has already intensively posed under investigation the demand of expanding DCN capacity. Although WDM technique is currently used in optical DCNs as a well-known technology, the research effort mainly on SDM incorporation in DCNs is expected to continue at pace in the near future to provide more mature technologies to enhance even more capacity. SDM technique is currently used in DCNs exploiting the commercially available fiber ribbons or bundles [\[281\]](#page-30-24) which can be obtained by packing together thousands of single mode fibers (SMFs) [\[282\].](#page-30-25) In this way, high capacity, special efficiency and compatibility with WDM technology can be achieved [\[283\],](#page-30-26) while cabling complexity is significantly reduced. Despite capacity boost, SDM technique based on fiber ribbons or bundles provides bulky cables which are hard to manage in a dense DCN environment, in order to serve the explosive DCN traffic. To overcome this issue, multi core fibers (MCFs) [\[284\],](#page-30-27) [\[285\]](#page-30-28) and few-mode fibers (FMF) [\[283\]](#page-30-26) can be used in DCNs implementing the SDM scenario. Although MCF capacity is linearly proportional to the number of cores in the fiber, MCF effectiveness is restricted by the inter-core crosstalk (IC-XT), i.e. the undesired high coupling between optical signals that propagate in adjacent cores and can occur if the principal and the undesired IC-XT signals match in phase [\[286\].](#page-30-29) In this case, optical signal-tonoise (OSNR) decrease is provided resulting in high power demand [\[287\].](#page-30-30) For this reason, only uncoupled MCFs [\[288\]](#page-30-31) and weakly coupled MCFs [\[289\]](#page-30-32) can be exploited in shortreach applications such as DCNs, since strongly coupled MCFs [\[290\]](#page-30-33) provide high IC-XT even in a few meters fiber length [\[291\].](#page-30-34) In a MCF, the core number increase (in order <span id="page-22-18"></span><span id="page-22-17"></span><span id="page-22-1"></span><span id="page-22-0"></span>to reach higher capacity) results to MCF cladding diameter increase too. This fact is critical enough for DCNs design where high special efficiency is required. Although some efficient MCF designs suitable for DCNs have been proposed (e.g. a MCF with 100 cores within a 320 µm cladding diameter, providing IC-XT < 25 dB for 1 km fiber length [\[292\],](#page-30-35) and another MCF demonstration of more than 100 spatial channels each carrying hundreds of WDM channels in a single fiber with capacity > 10 Pbps[\[293\]\)](#page-30-36), there is still an intensive interest to achieve even higher core density and lower IC-XT, in smaller cladding diameter and for longer fibers for efficient DCN implementations. Nowadays MCF-based SDM technology is assumed to be the most efficient and feasible one to increase capacity for short reach DCNs, since IC-XT can be considered not severe over short links (< 1 km). Since MCFbased SDM technique is achieved to be compatible to energy efficient integrated technologies such as VCSELs [\[294\],](#page-30-37) [\[295\],](#page-31-0) while it can be used with silicon photonic on-board transceivers [\[296\],](#page-31-1) it is a promising candidate to meet the demands for high capacity, cost and power efficient DCNs despite the short-reach implementation restriction and the consequent small DCN scale. In this direction advocates the evolution of optical switches that can support MCF links with SDM switching suitable for DCNs [\[297\],](#page-31-2) [\[298\],](#page-31-3) as well as of SDM MUX/DEMUX modules [\[283\].](#page-30-26) On the other hand, MCF amplifiers technology is still under research. Finally, efficient MCF-based SDM DCNs can be envisioned with the exploitation of effective transmission techniques such as scheduling transmission in opposite directions (bi-directional transmission) over adjacent cores to suppress IC-XT [\[299\],](#page-31-4) [\[300\],](#page-31-5) along with appropriate routing and spectrum allocation (RSA) algorithms for different DCN topologies on the top of SDM and WDM [\[301\],](#page-31-6) [\[302\]](#page-31-7) to increase DCN capacity up to even 300 times as compared to the WDM solution.

### <span id="page-22-21"></span><span id="page-22-20"></span><span id="page-22-19"></span><span id="page-22-5"></span>G. DISAGGREGATION IN DCNs

<span id="page-22-23"></span><span id="page-22-22"></span><span id="page-22-14"></span><span id="page-22-13"></span><span id="page-22-12"></span><span id="page-22-11"></span><span id="page-22-10"></span><span id="page-22-9"></span><span id="page-22-8"></span><span id="page-22-6"></span>In order to meet the demands of serving the explosive traffic, DC operators gradually enhance the DC resources total capacity by increasing the number of networking, memory, storage and computing components. However, the memory and CPU utilization in DCs is low enough. Measurements have revealed that DC clusters utilize only 10%-30% of the total CPU capacity during 80% of the time, whilst the average memory utilization is only 50% during 55% of the time [\[303\].](#page-31-8) In other words, despite the high cost and power consumed for the constantly increasing number of DC resources, they cannot be fully utilized. This is because traditional DCs follow the server-centric paradigm according which the available resources per server are limited to the boundaries of the mainboard tray. Nevertheless, the ratio of resources demands for memory to those for CPU may vary by 3-4 orders of magnitude, being higher than the available resources in a server tray [\[304\]](#page-31-9) and providing resource underutilization. For example, if in a server the whole CPU capacity is allocated to a single task along with a part of the

<span id="page-23-6"></span><span id="page-23-5"></span><span id="page-23-2"></span>memory resources, then the remaining memory is wasted [\[305\],](#page-31-10) providing resource fragmentation [\[306\]](#page-31-11) which accounts for even 85% of the total DCN cost [\[307\].](#page-31-12) It is evident that DC resources wastage is getting worse with DC scaling. In order to enhance DC performance, the resourcecentric paradigm has attracted research interest, according which server resources (memory, xPU, storage and accelerators) are disaggregated into standalone components [\[308\],](#page-31-13) [\[309\],](#page-31-14) [\[310\],](#page-31-15) [\[311\].](#page-31-16) According to the disaggregated DC (DDC) approach, all servers resources provide a common pool of accessible resources across the whole DCN, from which a resource can independently from the others be allocated to a task. In this way, DDCs can offer high flexibility for resource allocation, while resource utilization can be improved even up to 34% and power consumption up to 40% [\[312\].](#page-31-17) In the DDC architecture, a great challenge arises: high bandwidth and low latency interconnect network is required to ensure the memory-to-CPU ultra-high speed communication. This is because DDC traffic is exchanged among more nodes (the disaggregated resources) than in traditional directattached components DCs providing high traffic congestion, while higher latency overhead is provided. In nowadays computer architecture, the interconnection among multiple resource types poses different latency requirements. For example, the communication between CPU and storage can be implemented in ms scale, while that of CPU and memory must be realized much faster in ns scale [\[313\].](#page-31-18) As a promising solution to meet these challenges arises optical networking technology. Although optical transmission technology can support high (but not unlimited) bandwidth in Tbps scale, optical switching technology is not mature yet to provide such low switching latency. Despite recent research prototypes that have met ns scale switching demands [\[310\],](#page-31-15) [\[314\],](#page-31-19) [\[315\],](#page-31-20) [\[316\],](#page-31-21) the challenge to fully utilize the advantages of fully DDCs in a power efficient way with low latency and high bandwidth still calls for further advances.

### <span id="page-23-4"></span><span id="page-23-0"></span>**VII. CONCLUSION**

ML/AI advent has enabled several new cloud/edge applications that impetuously gain ground in every-day life, such ChatGPT, requiring enormous datasets to be exchanged and processed in a flash inside DCs globally. Moreover, multiple emerging data-intensive delay-sensitive user applications, such as social networks, online gaming etc., are co-hosted in DCs providing a tremendous traffic explosion within DCNs. These extremely intensive traffic-boosting conditions in existing DCNs drive new tendencies in DCN operation and design in order to support the end-users preferences without additional apparent complexity. Optical technology in transmission and switching is projected to effectively serve the rapidly increasing traffic in DCNs.

<span id="page-23-7"></span>This paper gives a comprehensive review on the main characteristics of existing DCNs while it emphasizes the optical technology that enables next generation DCNs to provide rich performance in terms of low end-to-end latency in µs scale. In specific, this paper presents the recent technology evolution on optical DCN links and it outlines the expected relative evolutions in the near future towards the Tbps data rate era. In addition, it extensively explores the DCN traffic characteristics of high burstiness and variability. Based on both measurements in existing big-data companies' DCs and published simulation DCN traffic predictions, it discusses the close dependance of traffic size, interarrival time and locality on the specific applications running in each DCN, deriving useful directions for efficient optical DCN design. Since, the rapid traffic increase and burstiness in DCNs can be effectively served by optical switching technology as compared to the electrical one that is currently used in most of DCs globally (despite the introduction of few optical switching prototypes), this paper focuses on optical DCN switching. In specific, it provides an extensive overview of the optical switching technologies and techniques that are currently used in existing DCNs and of those which are expected to enable advances in new emerging data-intensive cloud/edge applications in next generation optical DCNs. Furthermore, bandwidth allocation schemes and access control mechanisms have been extensively discussed for both intra- and inter-rack optical DCN domain, aiming to reach efficient contention resolution, high bandwidth utilization and low end-to-end delay. Moreover, power demands of modern DCNs is further discussed, while diverse DCN architectures and optical switching technologies are compared regarding their energy footprint. Finally, this paper outlines the technical challenges and open research topics towards efficient optical DCN performance in the near future. Since multiple data-boosting time-sensitive user applications constantly emerge, optical technology is expected to play crucial role in cloud/edge DCs with adaptability, reconfigurability and intelligence enabled by ML/AI, providing fast and reliable service to the end users, while keeping cost and power efficient DCN operation.
