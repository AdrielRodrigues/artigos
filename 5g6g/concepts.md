## XR and Metaverse Foundations

### Extended Reality (XR) and Metaverse over Mobile Networks
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications, ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain | gen: 150e9191c176 -->
Extended Reality (XR) is the umbrella for Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR) and Mixed Reality (MR), which together form spatial computing: the digitization of human and object activity in 3D space. These sit on the Reality-Virtuality Continuum (RVC), with AR lying between Real Reality and VR and MR as the middle ground where virtual and physical objects interact in real time. VR fully replaces the real world with synthetic environments built on displays, graphics, tracking and databases, and needs tight multi-user synchronization to avoid latency-induced discomfort; industrial adoption is hindered by latency and rendering complexity. AR overlays digital elements on the physical world and is categorized into marker-based, marker-less, projection-based and superimposition-based types, with open problems in precise mapping and tracking of virtual objects in complex urban scenes; MR relies on computer vision to perceive boundaries and lighting. Experience quality is described through Quality-of-Interaction (QoI), a subset of Quality-of-Experience (QoE), and 5G's eMBB and uRLLC are bottlenecks for the most demanding use cases such as holographic communication and real-time haptics. XR in 6G is characterized by two demands: extreme bandwidth for high-fidelity graphics and ultra-low latency to prevent motion sickness. 6G is expected to enable spatial computing, meaning real-time mapping of physical spaces integrated with digital overlays, as well as remote telepresence. XR is applied in education and cultural heritage. In smart culture, representative use cases include hybrid museum visits using AR QR codes, holographic projections of artists for remote concerts, and virtual reconstructions of ancient landmarks (e.g., the CHRONOS application for Ancient Greece). The 'Internet of Cultural Things' (IoCT) combines IoT sensors, cameras and XR to provide personalized visitor paths, multisensory interaction and improved accessibility. The Metaverse is conceptualized as an evolution of the Internet: a persistent, computer-generated, immersive 3D pool of XR spaces where humans and avatars interact, differing from plain cyberspace by its depth of immersion and persistence. It builds on a progression from Web1 (static pages) and Web2 (platform-controlled social media) toward decentralized environments in which NFTs represent digital ownership, and it integrates B5G/6G, cloud/edge computing, AI and blockchain, with lifelike recreations of historical sites, global participation and an autonomous creator economy based on DeFi and NFTs. Integration with IoT and the Tactile Internet lets it react in real time to physical gestures. Reported use cases include marketing (in-game branded clothing and billboards), blockchain dApps and peer-to-peer virtual economies, virtual tourism, virtual real estate tours that let buyers customize interiors from 360-degree captures by cameras and drones, browser-based P2P communication, hybrid VR workspaces (e.g., Virtuworx), medical and surgical training simulations, AI-driven diagnostic training, and smart cities using 6G IoT sensors and digital twins to optimize traffic and energy. Simulations have also been proposed to make climate change tangible to the public. Representative projects and players include Decentraland (a DAO-governed virtual world for real estate and NFT sales), Meta's Oculus ecosystem, Enjin (an NFT SDK provider), Silks (blockchain horse racing), the University of Miami XR Initiative (nursing and autism training), VReality (real estate), MetaMed (telehealth), MetaDrive (autonomous-driving simulation via reinforcement learning) and Edverse (blockchain education), alongside Epic Games (Unreal Engine) and Microsoft (Mesh). Such applications require massive bandwidth, ultra-low latency and high reliability, and the 'sense-decide-act' loop needed for immersion favors distributed edge-based architectures over centralized cloud computing. Private 6G networks may be needed for mission-critical industrial tasks. Network support covers high-resolution content delivery, synchronized multi-user activities, offline sharing of 3D objects, real-time XR streaming for education, and blended XR conferences that unify remote and in-person participants.

### 3GPP XR Support in 5G-Advanced (Rel-17/18 XRM)
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications | gen: fa7fefa2e4b6 -->
3GPP Releases 17 and 18, including the XR and Media services (XRM) Work Item, add XR-specific enhancements to the 5G system. PDU Set-based QoS lets the network treat groups of packets that jointly carry one media unit (e.g., a video frame) as a single entity, improving delivery precision; uplink-downlink coordination reduces latency across the interactive loop; and energy-saving mechanisms such as optimized Discontinuous Reception (DRX) cycles are aligned with the periodicity of media streams (e.g., 60 FPS) to suit the intermittent traffic pattern. A proposed architecture adds XR-specific functions: a 5G-XR Application Function (AF), a 5G-XR Application Server (AS) and a 5G-XR Client in the User Equipment, communicating over standardized interfaces (N6, N3) or the PC5 sidelink. 5G-Advanced also offers microsecond-level timing accuracy and fine positioning, supporting device tracking for XR.

### XR Performance Requirements and 5G Feasibility Limits
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications, ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends | gen: aa68a2d6798d -->
Evaluations against XR requirements show that current 5G meets many targets in favorable conditions but falls short in dense deployments and for the multi-sensory demands of the Metaverse. Standard XR audio needs about 36 Mbps, which 5G handles, but lossless high-fidelity audio is projected at 1.6 Gbps. Full-view 72K x 36K video would need about 1 Tb/s raw or ~1 Gb/s compressed; user satisfaction declines above roughly 30 Mbps in dense areas, although algorithms such as AIPAT can reach 40–50 Mbps with low delay. Motion-to-photon latency must stay under 20 ms to avoid motion sickness; URLLC reaches sub-10 ms in optimal conditions, but high user density raises buffering delay to 50–100 ms. High-fidelity haptic feedback requires about 0.1 ms latency, beyond what 5G delivers. Holographic telepresence requires Tbps-class rates: a standard hologram may need 0.5–2 Gb/s, while full-size human holograms need up to 4.32 Tb/s. At the AI layer, a PersonalizedAI layer driven by real-time emotional and mental analytics is estimated to require transmission bit rates of ~238 Gbps, exceeding 5G capability. Reliability requires about 99% of frames delivered within the latency budget, and coexisting eMBB traffic can cut XR capacity by up to 80% unless Inter-cell Interference Coordination (ICIC) or stricter packet delay budget (PDB) settings are used. Capacity is only 2–8 UEs per cell at 30–45 Mbps in FR1 (sub-6 GHz) versus 20–35 UEs in FR2 (mmWave), while HD VR streaming at 200–2000 Mbps is unsustainable. XR devices target power below 3 W; 5G modems use about 0.5 W but draw more under low-latency dense traffic, which DRX optimization mitigates. 5G-Advanced reaches 1–10 µs timing accuracy through over-the-air synchronization and PTP, and uplink carrier-phase positioning can give sub-centimeter accuracy and under 1 degree orientation precision at 28 GHz. Beyond these gaps, 5G also cannot support the projected proliferation of IoT devices, ultra-massive connectivity in dynamic environments, or resilient edge AI for decentralized intelligence. The capacity, power and latency-under-load gaps motivate 6G, with research on PDU-set scheduling, Radio Environment Maps (REMs) and Superposition Coding (SPC) in massive MIMO.

### Tactile Internet and Holographic Telepresence
<!-- sources: ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain, tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond | gen: 5bad84a513bb -->
The Tactile Internet requires a round-trip delay below 1 ms so that haptic feedback feels instantaneous (high-fidelity haptics are cited at about 0.1 ms, the latency target also given for 6G). Unlike conventional content-oriented networks, it is built on control-based communication whose response time of roughly 1 ms matches human tactile perception; services differ widely in their needs, with IoT devices tolerating about 100 ms at low data rates, while autonomous vehicles and AR need about 1 Gb/s with sub-1 ms latency. 5G uses SDN/NFV to reduce latency, but the speed of light limits transmission distance to roughly 150 km for such budgets. AI-based predictive models are proposed to mask this latency by predicting haptic feedback before it arrives. A feasibility testbed connected an access network in Daejeon to a core network (KOREN) over 278 km, using ROADMs and 100 Gb/s DP-QPSK coherent transceivers. An inverted pendulum emulated a remotely controlled robot, requiring millisecond-order control loops between the controller in Seoul and the pendulum in Daejeon, while uncompressed 4K UHD video at 6 Gb/s was sent simultaneously to emulate robotic vision. Total latency was dominated by fiber propagation delay (about 1.3 ms for long-haul and about 0.3 ms for access). With a high-capacity, low-latency TDM-PON prototype the pendulum stayed balanced and the video was clear; when latency or capacity were intentionally degraded, the pendulum lost balance and the video froze, showing that both high capacity and low latency are needed in the access network. Holographic Telepresence (HT) requires Tbps-class rates and latency under 1 ms: a standard hologram may need 0.5–2 Gb/s, while full-size human holograms need up to 4.32 Tb/s. Holographic communication transmits 3D images, so ultra-low latency is needed to avoid perceptual lag; it depends on specialized holographic capture and display devices and on significant edge computing resources for real-time rendering. URLLC is the primary enabler for high-stakes applications such as remote surgery, with 6G THz links and edge computing providing the needed capacity and proximity; in Healthcare 4.0, telesurgery is performed through haptic robots over sub-millisecond 6G Tactile Internet links, and the Internet of Medical Things adds high-definition telemedicine and continuous monitoring through implantable sensors. In the Internet of Robotic Things (IoRT), collaborative robotics and teleoperation rely on low latency for haptic feedback, while AI-driven systems let robots learn from their environment and coordinate complex tasks autonomously. Haptic interaction is also envisioned in immersive services such as virtual property tours, where a Tactile Internet component carries the haptic stream while FeMBB carries 4K UHD video. Network slicing is expected to reconcile the low-latency haptic stream with the high-bandwidth video stream within the same session.

### AI Layers and Generative AI for the Metaverse
<!-- sources: ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, edge_computing_enabled_large-scale_traffic_flow_prediction_with_gpt_in_intelligent_autonomous_transport_system_for_6g_network | gen: 25cc90b11e6b -->
AI is mapped onto a seven-layer Metaverse architecture: AIInfra (hardware and software such as GPUs and MEMS enabling AI execution); AIInt (intelligent interfaces for accessibility, e.g., brain-computer interfaces for disabled users); AICont (AI-driven smart contracts supporting democratization and anomaly detection in blockchain transactions); AIVWorld (autonomous generation of realistic 3D environments); AIART-E (generative AI such as GPT and DALL-E for economic enrichment through NFT creation); SocialAI (Explainable AI to reduce bias and detect hate speech); and PersonalizedAI (hyper-personalization from real-time emotional and mental analytics). Transformers and deep learning enable natural-language interaction and realistic 3D rendering. In learning paradigms, the field is moving from task-dependent supervised learning toward self-supervised and reinforcement learning, to build autonomous, life-like agents that interact with humans without constant human labeling. Computer vision, using semantic segmentation and 3D pose estimation, supports lifelike avatars, holographic images and 'digital humans', and cognitive-theory-based optimization of 3D spaces is proposed to speed learning in virtual classrooms. To avoid the cost of manual 3D modeling, generative models (GANs, diffusion models) and Neural Radiance Fields (e.g., Instant NeRF) convert 2D images or text descriptions into high-fidelity 3D assets automatically. Generative pre-trained models are also being repurposed beyond language: a GPT-2 backbone can be applied to spatio-temporal forecasting such as traffic flow by tokenizing time series through patching and channel-independence, keeping most pre-trained parameters frozen to retain general representation knowledge, and fine-tuning only Layer Normalization parameters. These AI workloads, especially LLMs, need large bandwidth and stable links, which ties the AI layers to 6G requirements. Open challenges include bias-free AI, integration with Industry 5.0, and protecting intellectual property against easy digital counterfeiting.

### Sustainability of the Metaverse
<!-- sources: ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends | gen: 55a6649a254f -->
The Metaverse has socio-environmental implications beyond technology. Environmental benefits cited include replacing physical goods (e.g., denim) with digital assets for avatars, reducing water consumption and CO2 emissions; replacing discretionary air travel with virtual meetings and concerts, lowering the carbon footprint of the conference and exhibition industry; and digitalizing events and products to move toward a waste-free circular economy. Digital twins support sustainable industrial and urban 'meta-worlds' by simulating systems to optimize energy use before physical deployment. 6G is designed to be more energy-efficient than earlier generations. Risks include increased energy consumption by data centers, for which regulation is advocated, and the energy cost of blockchain, motivating a shift from Proof-of-Work to Proof-of-Stake. Energy-efficient AI models that do not drain device batteries are an open research need. On the social side, the authors argue for democratization (equal access) over mere decentralization, warn that 'unconscious bias' in AI models can lead to digital discrimination by race or gender, and point to a digital divide, since current VR hardware is concentrated in North America and Western Europe. Reported benefits include democratized healthcare access, play-to-earn economic models, and reduced ecological footprints via virtual offices.

## 5G Core, Edge and Slicing

### 5G Service-Based Architecture (SBA) and Network Slicing
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications, ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport, resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey | gen: 15b268aaed40 -->
The 5G Service-Based Architecture is a modular, cloud-native core design in which stateless Network Functions (NFs) communicate over HTTP/2-based service interfaces as producers and consumers. 5G is framed as a networks-as-a-service model, avoiding one-size-fits-all architectures: use cases ranging from massive broadband and high-definition media to ultra-reliable machine-type communication (uMTC) and tactile Internet have conflicting QoS requirements for latency, jitter, reliability and bandwidth. Network slicing, combined with Network Function Virtualization (NFV), builds on the architecture's modularity by creating virtual, logical networks on shared infrastructure, each tailored to a service profile such as eMBB (high throughput), uRLLC (ultra-reliable low latency) or mMTC, which allows XR traffic to receive dedicated treatment. A Network Slice is a logical end-to-end network delivered under a Service Level Agreement (SLA); once activated it becomes a Network Slice Instance (NSI), composed of several Network Slice Subnet Instances (NSSIs), one per technological domain. Management is hierarchical: the Communication Service Management Function (CSMF) translates service needs into network requirements, the Network Slice Management Function (NSMF) manages NSIs, and the Network Slice Subnet Management Function (NSSMF) handles individual NSSIs. Four stakeholders take part, often with conflicting goals and in different administrative domains: Infrastructure Providers (InPs), Mobile Network Operators (MNOs), Slice Tenants (STs) and end-users. The slice lifecycle has four phases: Preparation (design and onboarding), Commissioning (creation and resource allocation), Operation (supervision and modification) and Decommissioning (termination). The enabling technologies are NFV (VNFs/CNFs on VMs or containers), SDN (control/data plane separation with centralized programmability) and Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC), which moves delay-sensitive functions such as the UPF closer to the edge. A slice spans multiple domains: the wireless radio (RAN, with RU/DU/CU functional splits), the access, metro and core transport networks, and geographically distributed data centers hosting the core (EPC in 4G-derived deployments), so the transport network must be able to serve each slice's distinct requirements, for example by placing latency-sensitive slices on the shortest paths. In 6G, network slicing is regarded as critical for the Metaverse because its services have conflicting requirements, for example ultra-low latency for haptics versus high bandwidth for video, which must be served concurrently on shared infrastructure. Blockchain-based proposals use smart contracts to replace network slicing brokers, allowing secure, automated provisioning of virtual slices for different users, and use blockchain to coordinate License Shared Access (LSA) spectrum sharing and the billing of crowdsourced infrastructure. SBA also follows an API-driven approach that exposes network capabilities to third parties, including XR application developers, who can request QoS or access network information programmatically. The reliance on APIs and virtualized NFs also shapes the security surface of the core, requiring strong authentication and encrypted communication between functions.

### Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC)
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications, ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, edge_computing_enabled_large-scale_traffic_flow_prediction_with_gpt_in_intelligent_autonomous_transport_system_for_6g_network, edge_intelligence_in_softwarized_6g_deep_learning-enabled_network_traffic_predictions, low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain | gen: 80543f0cbc36 -->
Multi-Access Edge Computing integrates edge servers with the 5G core by placing the User Plane Function (UPF) and Local Area Data Networks (LADN) near the base station, which shortens the end-to-end data path and lowers latency. A common formulation is a three-tier architecture: mobile users as data sources, edge servers for local processing, and a central server for coordination. Edge nodes are resource-constrained, with limited storage and computation relative to growing IoT traffic volumes, so edge AI is typically delivered as containerized microservices on compact edge boxes close to the traffic source, orchestrated with Kubernetes and Kubeflow. Cloud computing offers vast resources but often cannot meet strict latency requirements, which motivates offloading tasks to nearby edge servers; in cloud-edge optical network formulations, the candidate destinations for an offloaded request are the edge server of the local region, edge servers of adjacent regions, and cloud servers, with the choice balancing latency against regional computing load. In 6G, the infrastructure is split into IoT (massive connectivity with multi-modal cellular/satellite access), edge (decentralized processing for real-time decisions) and cloud (centralized high-power computing for big data analytics and disaster recovery). Middleware uses MEC nodes for context-aware data caching: predictive analysis preloads data based on user location, behavior and application requirements, which reduces load on the core network. MEC underpins several 6G applications: in the Internet of Vehicles it supports real-time collision avoidance alongside URLLC; in the Internet of Medical Things edge servers give near-real-time processing for critical diagnostics; in the Internet of Drones it enables local obstacle avoidance; in the Industrial IoT it reduces response times for safety-critical operations; and in holographic communication it provides the significant resources needed for real-time rendering. For XR, MEC enables real-time analytics behind AR overlays (for example in museums) and edge caching of high-demand XR assets, and it supports energy-aware offloading of computationally heavy rendering from headsets to the edge, which would otherwise drain mobile device batteries. Caching proposals for AR/VR include in-network content caching, both centralized and distributed, that stores popular content such as property tours at the edge; a pricing model for cache offloading in which edge nodes share cached content, relieving pressure on the cloud core; and Content-Centric IPv6, which replaces legacy location-based routing by decoupling content from location so requests are served from the nearest edge router. In a reported evaluation, 6G eRLLC with such caching improved caching efficiency by 43% (lower packet miss probability) under high traffic. Edge AI extends this by moving inference and prediction closer to the user to satisfy uRLLC requirements; examples include CNN-LSTM combinations for channel prediction and traffic forecasting that keep connections stable, LSTM encoder-decoder models that forecast multivariate traffic statistics from µ-box measurements to support automated resource scaling, and Field of View (FOV) prediction at the edge, which can reduce bandwidth requirements by up to 80% by delivering only the content the user is likely to view. Edge clusters can also host model training: in large-scale traffic flow prediction, training is distributed across edge clusters, each handling one road-network subgraph with resources proportional to its size, relieving central server bottlenecks and improving training efficiency, while techniques such as parameter transfer between neighboring clusters and attention-head pruning suit limited edge hardware. Decentralized intelligence and MEC are presented as necessary to scale to millions of concurrent users, with future work aimed at moving AI from cloud to device and at energy-efficient models. MEC also introduces a new attack surface: edge resources run on virtualized infrastructure where compromised hosts can affect service availability, and edge repositories holding user data are exposed to privacy attacks. Because XR capacity per cell remains limited in dense deployments, edge placement reduces distance-related delay but does not by itself resolve radio-side constraints.

### Network Data Analytics Function (NWDAF)
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications | gen: e95a130a0104 -->
The Network Data Analytics Function is the 5G core entity that applies AI/ML to network data, moving management from reactive to proactive operation. For XR deployments in crowded venues such as museums, NWDAF can predict network load in high-traffic areas, optimize resource allocation through intelligent network slicing, and dynamically adjust QoS parameters to minimize packet loss and delay. These capabilities complement the radio resource management needed to sustain XR quality as user density grows, and AI-driven predictive analytics is identified as a priority for future research.

### Digital Twin Networks (DTN)
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications, ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey | gen: 2f3fc4e3047d -->
Digital Twin Networks provide real-time virtual replicas of physical networks, used for predictive maintenance and optimization by testing configurations and anticipating faults on the twin before acting on the real network. In the XR and Metaverse context, digital twins extend to immersive representations of physical assets and environments, enabling sustainable industrial 'meta-worlds' in which assembly lines and urban systems are simulated to optimize energy use and reduce waste before physical implementation. The THz bandwidth provided by 6G is cited as an enabler of digital twins and immersive XR in Industry 4.0 AR/VR scenarios, where robots (for example using reinforcement learning for in-site monitoring) can have their status viewed through AR interfaces. In smart cities, 6G IoT sensors feed a digital twin used to optimize urban traffic and energy flow. In network slicing, Network Digital Twins are proposed as a scalability enabler: because E2E resource-management solutions span RAN, transport and core domains and are costly or risky to trial on live infrastructure, a twin allows them to be validated virtually before physical deployment. Open challenges include ownership disputes over digital representations and the need for ultra-low-latency synchronization between the physical system and its twin.

### Smart City Layered Communication Architecture and 5G Optical Requirements
<!-- sources: a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure | gen: a654cd687c0d -->
Smart city IoT integration is commonly described with a four-layer communication model. The Device/Sensing Layer comprises sensors and actuators that collect physical data and require low latency and long battery life. The Communication Layer consists of transport and access networks supporting eMBB, mMTC and URLLC, using DWDM for long distances and PON or point-to-point links for fronthaul/backhaul. The Data Layer organizes, filters, analyzes and stores the collected data to enable decision-making. The Application Layer is the interface with citizens, delivering services such as smart healthcare, energy and governance. Optical fiber is favored as the medium because of its high bandwidth, low attenuation and immunity to electromagnetic interference, and urban traffic growth is driven largely by video streaming (over 70% of Internet traffic). 5G imposes stringent requirements on the optical transport: traffic volume expected to be 100 times higher than 4G, with end-user data rates up to 10 Gb/s; end-to-end delay of a few milliseconds, and below 1 ms for time-critical applications such as healthcare and traffic safety; spectral efficiency at least three times that of 4G; and minimized power consumption across sensing and transmission devices. Fixed spectrum allocation cannot meet these demands, motivating flexible optical networking.

## End-to-End Network Slicing and Orchestration

### End-to-End Network Slicing Across RAN, Transport and Core Domains
<!-- sources: resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey, tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies | gen: b50776c68a83 -->
End-to-end (E2E) network slicing treats the Radio Access Network (RAN), Transport Network (TN) and Core Network (CN) as deeply interdependent domains, so that a solution feasible in one may fail E2E because of resource exhaustion in another; computing resources, for example, may be shared between RAN and CN functions. The pressure that a slice places on the transport network depends on its type: mMTC-type slices (low volume, high reliability) primarily load the fronthaul, URLLC-type slices (high volume, latency below 1 ms) primarily load the aggregation layer (about 55% of traffic in the cited analysis), and eMBB slices (high throughput) primarily load the midhaul and backhaul. The literature is organized into three classes. Single-domain frameworks manage only the RAN, TN or CN and lack visibility into other domains: admission of a slice in one domain without checking the others leads to QoS violations during operation, RAN allocation ignores whether the TN has enough bandwidth, and protecting one domain leaves the E2E service exposed to attack vectors elsewhere. Cross-domain frameworks combine two domains. RAN+CN work shows that joint admission control and allocation outperform disjointed approaches at the price of higher combinatorial complexity, but orchestration typically ignores TN congestion and VNF migration schemes often ignore radio resources such as Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs). RAN+TN work reduces over-provisioning relative to TN-only solutions but usually lacks CN VNF placement and O-RAN compliance. TN+CN work commonly uses Stochastic Network Calculus (SNC) for latency estimation and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) for heterogeneous service design, yet overlooks RAN resources, making E2E latency estimates inaccurate. Including User Equipment (UE) feedback such as CQIs improves allocation decisions, although user-level isolation among multiple active slices on one device remains open. True E2E frameworks incorporate RAN, TN and CN simultaneously under a high-level orchestrator. Generic E2E models represent nodes and links abstractly and miss domain-specific details such as PRB allocation, while dedicated E2E solutions use RL (for example SARSA) or joint optimization for admission control, and Queueing Theory or DRL (for example PPO) for joint radio, power and bandwidth allocation. Reconfigurability at the E2E level is sparsely studied; generic heuristics can redefine inter-domain delay budgets, but full dynamic reconfiguration needs hierarchical orchestration to avoid response times that degrade QoS. Most works labelled E2E are in fact cross-domain solutions with oversimplified models, and E2E research is considered to be in its infancy, with full automation as the main gap. E2E frameworks are the most reliable but also the most complex to implement, and AI/ML is regarded as essential for managing this complexity in the transition from 5G to 6G. Further directions include hierarchical (recursive) slicing, in which a tenant slices its allocated NSI further, and UE-level slicing. Within the access segment, slicing can be obtained by virtualizing and disaggregating the OLT into a physical part (ports and cards) and a logical part (DBA types and bandwidth profiles), so that exclusive logical resources can be allocated to specific applications on demand, a flexibility that fixed-hardware TDM-PONs lack and that serves diverse services sharing one ODN.

### Slice Admission Control, Resource Allocation and VNF Placement
<!-- sources: resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey | gen: 7580ec688af5 -->
Slice resource management comprises several functions: Admission Control (AC), which decides whether a slice request is feasible; Resource Allocation (RA), which assigns specific resources to an accepted slice; VNF placement, which chooses the location of VNFs/CNFs and reserves interconnection capacity; and reconfigurability, which scales or adjusts resources during the operation phase. These are usually written as a generic optimization problem, min/max f(x) subject to linear or non-linear constraints, and are typically combinatorial and NP-hard because of discrete variables such as VNF placement and the intersection of feasible regions across domains. Solution methodologies fall into three families. Optimization-based methods include closed-form (Queueing Theory), relaxation-based (convex approximation) and metaheuristics such as Genetic Algorithms. Machine learning covers supervised learning for prediction, unsupervised learning for pattern recognition, Reinforcement Learning and Deep RL (DRL) for automated decisions in dynamic environments, and Federated Learning for privacy-preserving decentralized training. Game Theory models strategic interactions between rational stakeholders such as MNOs and tenants, and is used primarily for pricing resources between InPs and MNOs. For AC, optimization approaches often map the problem onto multiple knapsack problems but ignore historical request data, whereas DRL adapts better to dynamic demand at the cost of needing large datasets. For RA, methods range from genetic algorithms for C-RAN design to model-free DRL for flexible deployment. VNF placement research balances latency and cost against resource utilization; DRL enables dynamic VNF migration, but the lack of hybrid strategies that consider RAN and CN VNFs together makes many solutions unrealistic. Reconfiguration is handled reactively through heuristics or proactively through traffic prediction (DES, neural networks); DRL gives better resilience but is often confined to one domain, producing over-provisioning in one area and under-utilization in another. In E2E settings, the central challenge is reducing the state-action space of the joint problem without losing accuracy.

### Multi-Domain Slice Orchestration and Trust (Blockchain, Multi-Agent DRL)
<!-- sources: resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey | gen: 5e6e83ea8b38 -->
Orchestration coordinates the interactions among management entities across technological domains. In single-domain systems it relies on hierarchical abstraction, with SDN controllers reporting to a domain orchestrator, and its main obstacle is the lack of full programmability across hardware vendors. Cross-domain orchestration leans heavily on DRL but often ignores transport congestion. In E2E designs, generic approaches focus on interoperability through standard interfaces such as OSM and the NFV Orchestrator (NFVO), and on blockchain to establish trust in multi-InP environments. Dedicated E2E solutions use multi-agent DRL, in which domain orchestrators report to a central Network Slice Management Function (NSMF); techniques such as safety DRL, which constrains exploration to avoid harmful actions, and imitation learning are used to accelerate convergence. On the trust side, most E2E security proposals are generic frameworks that use blockchain and smart contracts to negotiate and enforce SLAs among stakeholders (InPs, MNOs, tenants and end-users) belonging to different administrative domains; the EU project 5GZORRO is an example of trust mechanisms of this kind. Remaining gaps include integrated intra- and inter-domain security that accounts for non-virtualized resources such as radio power, and slice isolation to prevent inter-slice interference and attacks such as DDoS, where optimization models struggle with the trade-off between detection accuracy and resource efficiency. Securing Southbound Interfaces with Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) has also been proposed. A recurring requirement is standardized APIs that give trust and visibility across administrative domains.

### Open RAN, RIC and Zero-Touch Automation for Slicing
<!-- sources: resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey | gen: 9012e8d235d7 -->
Open RAN (O-RAN) promotes lower-level RAN functional splits (RU, DU, CU) to enhance flexibility and virtualization, and its RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) with xApps and rApps is identified as a route to more flexible E2E slicing, since control logic for radio resources can be deployed as software applications. Many cross-domain slicing frameworks lack compliance with O-RAN standards, which is cited as a limitation. For lifecycle management, integrating ETSI Zero-touch network and Service Management (ZSM) with O-RAN is proposed to achieve zero-touch automation of the slice lifecycle (preparation, commissioning, operation and decommissioning), complemented by standardized APIs for visibility across administrative domains and by Network Digital Twins for testing solutions before deployment. Experimental validation is supported by research projects and testbeds. EU initiatives (Horizon 2020 and the 6G SNS JU) focus on AI/ML for resource allocation and on trust mechanisms such as 5GZORRO, while the US PAWR program provides city-scale platforms, POWDER and Colosseum, used for O-RAN slicing experiments. Most testbeds are single-domain; cross-domain examples rely on ONAP or Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) control planes. Hyperstrator is noted as a modular, open-source E2E slice orchestrator, though it simplifies the transport network with Open vSwitch (OVS) containers rather than real optical switches. A call for open-source datasets and frameworks aims to reduce reliance on outdated synthetic data.

## 6G Vision and Architecture

### 6G Vision, THz Communications and New Service Classes
<!-- sources: ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond, hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization, machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies | gen: c03baa4ec3e1 -->
6G is envisioned as 'connectivity with intelligence', shifting from a data-centric to a human-centric approach and from mere connectivity to interactivity, in the context of the Internet of Everything (IoE). Wireless generations progressed from voice (1G) to multimedia (4G) and high-capacity connectivity (5G); 5G's mmWave raises speeds but remains insufficient for multi-sensory Metaverse requirements, and 5G's eMBB and uRLLC hit bottlenecks for demanding AR/VR such as holographic communication and real-time haptics. Commercial 5G deployments struggle with skyrocketing traffic and real-time service demands, with global mobile traffic projected at 5016 exabytes per month by 2030. In ultra-dense heterogeneous networks the capacity bottleneck shifts from the RAN to the transport network, so 6G goals translate directly into x-haul requirements. Quantitative 6G targets relative to 5G include peak data rates of about 1 Tbps (some visions cite up to 10 Tbps; 10–100x greater than 5G), latency reduced from 10 ms to below 1 ms (some visions cite round-trip latency of about 0.1 ms), spectrum expanded up to 1000 GHz, support for mobility up to 1000 km/h, reliability improved to 99.99999%, and massive connectivity of roughly 10^6 sensors/km². These are pursued with Terahertz (THz) waves (0.1–10 THz), 'smart radio environments' that use Intelligent Reflecting Surfaces (IRS) to control signal reflection through induced phase changes, and integrated space-air-ground-sea networks (SAGSIN) that extend coverage non-terrestrially, including via satellites. On the spectrum side, attention is given to FR3 (7–24 GHz) as a balance between capacity and propagation, and to THz bands (100 GHz–3 THz) for rates up to 1 Tbps; THz suffers extreme air loss and therefore requires ultra-dense infrastructure, which in turn increases the number of x-haul links, with fronthaul demands of 120–250 Gbps at latency of 50 µs or less expected in ultra-dense scenarios. Edge cloud placement shortens x-haul distance and overall latency, and the architecture is expected to evolve toward a 'network of subnetworks' using AI for predictive maintenance and dynamic resource allocation. Among the enabling technologies, THz communications are expected to deliver multi-terabit rates, Massive MIMO to raise spectral efficiency, and holographic beamforming to produce highly directional signals; AI-driven beamforming and network orchestration adapt the system dynamically to changing demand. In the terrestrial (land) architecture, 6G uses higher frequency bands (millimetre-wave and THz) together with Massive MIMO, advanced beamforming and ultra-dense deployments of small cells in urban areas, with AI/ML applied to dynamic resource allocation and interference management and an emphasis on 'green' sustainable design to limit the carbon footprint of high-density networks. Ultra-dense heterogeneous networks are also meant to provide seamless coverage and massive IoT integration. The transition is driven by the proliferation of IoT devices and demand for ultra-low latency and massive connection capacity, and the resulting traffic is non-linear, highly dynamic and prone to unpredictable surges, which defeats classical statistical prediction and motivates dynamic spectrum and wireless resource management so that operators can handle traffic spikes and reduce latency. Targeted services include Extended Reality (XR), IoE and holographic-type communication. THz bands are essential for high-fidelity indoor VR, 3D imagery, digital twins and zero-error short-range communication, but face challenges such as absorption by water molecules; immersive fidelity is expected to combine mmWave, MEC and THz. Compared with 5G's eMBB, uRLLC and mMTC, which are mostly limited to rudimentary XR, 6G introduces evolved service classes: uMBB/FeMBB (further-enhanced or ultra-massive broadband, with higher rates than eMBB), eRLLC (extreme reliable low-latency communication), uMTC (ultra-massive connectivity for dense sensor networks), uHPC (ultra-high-precision and reliability for mission-critical tasks) and e3DC (extended 3D coverage through non-terrestrial networks). A complementary classification extends the 5G triad into Computation-oriented Communications (CoC), Contextually agile eMBB (CAeC) and Event-defined URLLC (EDURLLC). These classes underpin applications such as Healthcare 4.0 (remote telesurgery with haptic robots) and Industry 4.0 (digital twins and immersive XR); in a virtual-tour case study, FeMBB provided 1 Tbps peak rates for 4K UHD streaming alongside Tactile Internet haptics, and was reported to cut inter-packet latency variation (jitter) by about 60% versus 5G eMBB, while eRLLC with in-network caching improved caching efficiency by 43% (lower packet miss probability) under high traffic. Channel modeling combines satellite channels for stability with ultra-massive MIMO described through Geometry Based Stochastic Models (GBSM). Remaining technical issues include a baud-rate mismatch between high-speed 6G cores and low-line-rate end devices, which creates flow-rate control problems, and the lack of universal standards, since many current 6G designs are proprietary. 6G is also designed to be more energy-efficient than previous generations, although advanced technologies such as THz and dense deployments increase power consumption, which calls for AI-based power management. Open research includes optimizing SAGSIN architectures for reliability during satellite handovers. On the optical side, the three 5G scenarios (eMBB for high-bandwidth data, uRLLC for mission-critical tasks, mMTC for IoT) require higher bandwidth to accommodate massive MIMO and wider spectrum, lower latency and precise synchronization for C-RAN/CoMP, and fine-grained network slicing for optimized QoS. Future 6G requirements extend these demands, for instance multi-Tb/s CPRI-equivalent rates per wavelength in the fronthaul (e.g., via DA-RoF with 400G-ZR coherent transceivers) and hard, deterministic-latency slicing in the transport network.

### Space-Air-Ground-Sea Integrated 6G Architecture
<!-- sources: review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain | gen: 58a810318003 -->
The proposed 6G architecture targets pervasive connectivity across four dimensions: space, air, land and sea/undersea, forming a space-air-ground-sea integrated network. In space, global coverage relies on satellite constellations in LEO, MEO and GEO orbits, which trade off latency against capacity. Key requirements are advanced inter-satellite links for direct data exchange, phased array antennas for adaptive beamforming, and cross-layer optimization; because power is scarce in orbit, energy efficiency is a primary design driver, alongside robust encryption and seamless integration with terrestrial networks. The airborne segment aims at ultra-fast in-flight connectivity for passengers and enhanced aircraft-to-aircraft (A2A) communication for collision avoidance. It also enables real-time remote pilot assistance, predictive maintenance through connected sensors, and improved surveillance and navigation, with high bandwidth and low latency needed for precise air traffic management and emergency search-and-rescue. The terrestrial segment uses millimetre-wave and Terahertz bands, Massive MIMO, advanced beamforming and ultra-dense small-cell deployments in urban areas, with AI/ML for dynamic resource allocation and interference management and a strong emphasis on sustainable ('green') design to reduce the carbon footprint of dense networks. In the sea and undersea domain, radio frequency signals attenuate rapidly in water, so a hybrid approach is used: acoustic communication for long distances at low rates and optical communication for short distances at high rates. Satellite integration is needed for remote oceanic connectivity. The undersea system supports underwater IoT sensor networks for environmental monitoring and resource management, and requires specialized energy-harvesting techniques and high-reliability links. Across these domains, the integration is meant to extend coverage non-terrestrially and complement the terrestrial network. Open research includes reliability during satellite handovers and consistent regulatory policy across jurisdictions. Space-air-ground-sea integration also widens the attack surface and the range of IoT entry points that must be secured.

### AI-Native Autonomous 6G Networks (Self-X and Pervasive AI)
<!-- sources: ai_and_6g_into_the_metaverse_fundamentals_challenges_and_future_research_trends, an_ai-driven_intelligent_traffic_management_model_for_6g_cloud_radio_access_networks, coalition_of_6g_and_blockchain_in_ar-vr_space_challenges_and_future_directions, edge_computing_enabled_large-scale_traffic_flow_prediction_with_gpt_in_intelligent_autonomous_transport_system_for_6g_network, edge_intelligence_in_softwarized_6g_deep_learning-enabled_network_traffic_predictions, hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain | gen: d57850f9ff71 -->
The relation between AI and 6G is a two-way feedback loop: 6G enables pervasive AI, and AI optimizes 6G networks. On the first side, 6G provides infrastructure (including SAGSIN) for ubiquitous intelligence and reduces inference latency by distributing AI across the end-edge-cloud continuum, allowing millions of users to share immersive spaces. On the second, 6G networks are expected to be Self-X: self-healing, self-optimizing and self-organizing, with Zero-Touch Management replacing manual configuration to handle massive complexity without human intervention. 6G is described as 'AI-native', incorporating several kinds of AI: tiny AI for constrained devices, federated AI for privacy-preserving distributed training, collective AI in which multiple agents cooperate, and semantic-oriented AI that conveys meaning rather than raw bits. The AI-native vision describes a hyper-flexible architecture that integrates AI, Software-Defined Networking (SDN), Network Function Virtualization (NFV) and cloud-native computing; DevOps practices decompose hardware-bound services into microservices at the network edge so operators can better meet latency and data-rate KPIs. Existing networks are not optimized for AI, however, and deploying AI services can deplete resources, while edge nodes face limited storage and compute, unlabeled raw time-series data and abrupt traffic shifts. At the network layer, AI-enhanced functions include ML-based channel estimation, modulation recognition and traffic classification and prediction; intelligent routing that avoids congestion and Radio Resource Management (RRM) with dynamic spectrum sharing; AI-driven fault management (self-healing), predictive mobility handover, and detection of intrusions or botnets; and energy optimization, where ML predicts low-activity periods and dynamically adjusts power levels. Edge intelligence for traffic prognosis (for example LSTM-based forecasting of flow statistics over a 10-hour horizon, deployed through Kubeflow) is presented as a step toward automated resource scaling driven by predicted traffic. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) manages resources dynamically; in a layered 6G architecture it occupies the information plane, where it performs intelligent resource allocation and context awareness alongside the communication and service planes. Federated Learning (FL) trains models across devices without compromising user privacy. Edge AI predictors such as CNN-LSTM support channel prediction and traffic forecasting. Proactive prediction is the operational core of this vision: a hybrid Random Forest–GRU–attention predictor of wireless resource allocation is reported to enable adaptive scheduling and energy-saving smart scaling, with a projected 40–60% reduction in network congestion events, improving QoS and operational efficiency by anticipating traffic spikes rather than reacting to them. A concrete closed loop appears in AI-driven 6G Cloud RAN traffic management: a gradient-boosted predictor forecasts RAN load, a management unit classifies traffic and maps it to resources, and the cloud services unit feeds observed resource usage and job types back as new training samples that refine the predictor. This lets the network anticipate congestion and reserve or consolidate capacity proactively rather than reacting after degradation; in simulation such a loop improved bandwidth utilization by up to 17-18% and cut job execution latency by up to 18-27% relative to baseline schedulers, though prediction and analysis overhead leaves it slower than an idealized optimal allocation. Pervasive AI also serves vertical applications such as intelligent transportation: an LLM-based spatio-temporal predictor (a GPT-2 backbone with a spatio-temporal module) can be trained across edge clusters, each handling a subgraph of the road network, to forecast traffic flow on more than 1000 roads, illustrating AI distributed across the edge rather than concentrated in a central server. Integrating AI into 6G also raises challenges of interoperability (standardized protocols across diverse AI modules), ethics (algorithmic bias and privacy) and security of the AI models themselves. Open directions include Zero-Touch Management at scale, moving AI from the cloud to the device, self-supervision to address scarce labeled data, energy-efficient models that do not drain device batteries, and online or reinforcement learning to adapt to abrupt traffic surges.

### 6G Intelligent Autonomous Transport Systems (6G-IATS)
<!-- sources: edge_computing_enabled_large-scale_traffic_flow_prediction_with_gpt_in_intelligent_autonomous_transport_system_for_6g_network | gen: 94211e755457 -->
6G-enabled Intelligent Autonomous Transport Systems (6G-IATS) are transport systems in which 6G connectivity and distributed computing support autonomous operation and traffic management. A key service is large-scale traffic flow prediction, defined as forecasting flow for more than 1000 roads, which underpins routing, congestion control and planning. The task is difficult because the volume of data generated by large transport systems strains centralized processing, and because accurate forecasts require capturing complex spatio-temporal correlations across the network, including recent, daily-periodic (rush hours) and weekly-periodic patterns. This motivates edge-based generative AI: segmenting the road network into subgraphs and training generative models on edge clusters near the data sources, as in the STGLLM-E architecture, which was evaluated on London and Manchester highway datasets. Proposed extensions include external factors such as weather and accidents.

### Security Threats in Virtualized 5G/MEC Networks
<!-- sources: 5g_6g_architecture_evolution_for_xr_and_metaverse_feasibility_study_security_and_privacy_challenges_for_smart_culture_applications, machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain | gen: 8c1c5f891f65 -->
Virtualized 5G and MEC deployments expand the attack surface beyond standard ICT threats such as malware and phishing. In virtualization, a compromised VM host can undermine the availability of the services it runs; Trusted Platform Modules (TPM) are proposed to establish chains of trust that preserve integrity. API-centric operation requires robust authentication (token, cookie or key-based), input sanitization against injection, and encrypted communication using mTLS or OAuth 2.0. Private 5G networks face protocol-specific attacks: RRC attacks can tamper with subscriber identity or cause denial of service; NAS vulnerabilities enable location tracking; flaws in the GTP protocol allow man-in-the-middle attacks on real-time environment data; and Diameter exploits can hijack user sessions. Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) sharing is proposed as a mitigation. The threat view extends to 6G, where the larger attack surface is attributed to AI integration, massive IoT and non-terrestrial links. Infrastructure vulnerabilities include weak isolation between network slices, IoT devices acting as entry points, and physical attacks on critical hardware. Cyber threats include sophisticated DDoS attacks, ransomware, zero-day exploits, and AI-driven manipulation of network decisions; AI models themselves are exposed to adversarial attacks that can corrupt inference or training. Operational risks include supply chain compromise through malicious components and the difficulty of keeping regulatory policies consistent across jurisdictions. ML-based defenses complement these measures. Decision trees combined with eXplainable AI (XAI) improve trust in intrusion detection systems, LSTMs (with the Nadam optimizer) and CNNs classify and detect malware traffic with high accuracy, and CNNs combined with Stacked Autoencoders (SAE) are used for intrusion detection. Non-parametric Bayesian methods assist IoT authentication, Gaussian Mixture Models enhance physical-layer security, and DRL provides robustness against jamming, with multi-agent RL enabling collaborative spectrum sharing for defense.

## Passive Optical Networks (PON)

### PON Multiplexing Variants (TDM-, OCDM-, TWDM-PON)
<!-- sources: a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure, enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond, intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services, tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies, xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: a3332dc18ccb -->
PON is a point-to-multipoint architecture made up of an optical line terminal (OLT), an optical distribution network (ODN) and optical network units (ONUs), and variants differ in how they separate users' signals. Access networks, unlike core networks that prioritize cost per bit, must maximize capacity at low cost per user, and are bound by an "outside plant" constraint: electrical power, optical amplifiers and dispersion-compensating fibers are generally not allowed in the passive ODN. Research therefore uses the O-band for both upstream and downstream to limit chromatic dispersion, and avalanche photodiode (APD) receivers to improve the power budget. PON is regarded as the most cost-effective fiber solution for x-haul, with PtMP deployments reported to reduce backhaul costs by up to 60%, and the variants have been evaluated against NG-PON2 requirements. TDM-PON shares a single wavelength by assigning time slots to ONUs; it is cost-effective, but suffers from security vulnerabilities (the shared medium exposes traffic) and from latency introduced by the Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA) process run in the OLT, which is problematic for 5G's tight delay budgets and keeps upstream latency a bottleneck; conventional fairness-based scheduling cannot meet Tactile Internet latency requirements. A further source of latency in TDMA-based PONs is that all traffic, including ONU-to-ONU traffic, must traverse the OLT and its electronic switching. In the XGS-PON (10 Gb/s symmetric) system model, an OLT at the central office serves multiple ONUs through passive splitters, and upstream bandwidth is organized into four Transmission Container (T-CONT) types: fixed (T-CONT-1), assured (T-CONT-2), non-assured (T-CONT-3) and best-effort (T-CONT-4). Upstream traffic is scheduled by polling: each ONU sends a Report frame stating its queue occupancy, and the OLT answers with grants carried in the Bandwidth Map (BWmap). Data that arrives after a Report but before the corresponding grant waits at least one more cycle, and periodic quiet windows opened for ranging newly activated ONUs can interrupt constant-bit-rate flows. TDM-PON is evolving from GPON through 50G-PON toward VHSP (100–200 Gbps by about 2035). A TDM-PON prototype based on IEEE 50G-EPON with 25 Gb/s per wavelength used an FPGA-based OLT and 25G bidirectional optical sub-assembly (BOSA) modules. It measured a downstream sensitivity of –24 dBm at a BER of 10^-3, remained stable over 50 hours, and carried commercial IPTV and file transfers at about 8 Gb/s (limited by the IP network interface). Optical Code Division Multiplexing PON (OCDM-PON, or OCDMA-PON) uses coherent or incoherent optical encoders and decoders to assign each user a distinct code, offering stronger network security, better bandwidth efficiency than TDM and asynchronous access, although it is currently considered impractical because photonic encoders/decoders are immature. WDM-PON assigns a dedicated wavelength per ONU, giving low latency and high scalability, but expensive transceivers make it less viable for some 5G scenarios. OFDM-PON (orthogonal subcarriers generated by DSP) offers high spectral efficiency downstream but is unsuitable upstream because of the lack of coordination among ONUs. Further proposals include SDM-PON, which uses multi-core fibers to create parallel spatial channels and avoids complex MIMO-DSP if weakly coupled cores are used; NOMA-PON, which applies power-domain multiplexing with Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC) to increase user capacity in 6G fronthaul; and PDM-PON, which uses polarization states to reach 100 Gbps or more per wavelength. Hybrid architectures such as TWDM-PON combine time- and wavelength-division multiplexing to capture the benefits of each, and TWDM-PON is singled out as the most promising solution for mobile fronthaul because of its balance of cost, capacity and flexibility in handling both time and wavelength resources. As candidates for 5G fronthaul and backhaul, PON technologies are expected to evolve toward 100 Gb/s and beyond through tunable transponders and new modulation formats. Within the ITU-T higher speed PON (HSP) project, 50G-PON is designed to replace or augment XG(S)-PON while reusing existing ODNs. To overcome the greater sensitivity requirements and dispersion penalties of 50 Gb/s signals, it employs DSP-based channel equalization, LDPC codes, low-chirp transmitters and semiconductor optical amplifiers. Beyond these, Very High Speed PON (VHSP) initiatives explore coherent transmission and digital subcarrier multiplexing (DSCM) as the next step. Three mechanisms address 5G x-haul latency: Cooperative Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (CoDBA) shares RAN scheduling information with the PON scheduler, eliminating the ONU-OLT negotiation latency; accelerated DBA scheduling allows multiple bursts per ONU within each 125-µs frame, cutting the maximum waiting time from 125 µs to roughly 16 µs; and a Dedicated Activation Wavelength (DAW) carries discovery and ranging on a separate wavelength, removing the ranging window so that low-latency traffic is not interrupted.

### WDM Passive Optical Networks (WDM-PON)
<!-- sources: a_bidirectional_wdm-pon_free_space_optical_fso_system_for_fronthaul_5_g_c-ran_networks, a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure, enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond | gen: f356d07ff210 -->
WDM-PON assigns a dedicated wavelength to each user over a shared passive fiber infrastructure, providing high-capacity, logically point-to-point connections between the Central Office (CO) and remote nodes such as Radio Remote Heads (RRHs) or Optical Network Units (ONUs). Compared with dedicated point-to-point fiber, PON allows resource sharing and lower cost, and wavelength separation avoids the bandwidth contention of time-shared PONs. The dedicated wavelengths and low power insertion loss make WDM-PON well suited to 5G fronthaul. To support cell densification, research focuses on tunable transponders that enable 'colorless' ONUs, which are not tied to a fixed wavelength, at 25 Gb/s and above. In a C-RAN fronthaul demonstration, a multi-wavelength comb source generated 16 channels spanning 1550.92 nm to 1556.96 nm, each carrying 20 Gbps downstream (320 Gbps in total). The channels were modulated with Mach-Zehnder modulators (MZM), amplified by Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFA), and sent over 10-20 km of SMF before reaching the ONUs. Upstream traffic reuses the downstream wavelengths, so no separate upstream wavelength plan or per-ONU laser is needed. Reported results showed that downlink performance was relatively insensitive to SMF length in this range, while the OOK uplink degraded more with added fiber length. A separate C-band front-haul proposal is a 'semi-active' WDM-PON in which active equipment is required at the DU/CU sites but not at the RU sites. It uses 20 wavelength pairs for bidirectional (BiDi) transmission, with cyclic arrayed waveguide gratings (AWGs) having a 2.6 THz free spectral range to multiplex the downstream and demultiplex the upstream channels, and it provides high reliability through type-B protection.

### Wavelength Reuse with RSOA in PON Upstream
<!-- sources: a_bidirectional_wdm-pon_free_space_optical_fso_system_for_fronthaul_5_g_c-ran_networks | gen: 392c0cd1cf9a -->
Wavelength reuse lets the upstream signal in a PON use the same wavelength as the downstream signal, removing the need for an expensive, wavelength-specific optical source at each Optical Network Unit (ONU). A Reflective Semiconductor Optical Amplifier (RSOA) at the ONU receives the downstream light and remodulates it with upstream data, acting as both modulator and amplifier. In a hybrid WDM-PON/FSO fronthaul, the RSOA generated a 5 Gbps NRZ on-off keying (OOK) upstream signal from the downstream carrier. A variable optical attenuator (VOA) set the input power so the RSOA operated in the gain saturation region, which suppresses residual downlink modulation on the returning seed wavelength and reduces Rayleigh backscattering noise. The upstream signal returned through the FSO link and SMF to PIN receivers at the Central Office. Because the RSOA is saturated, uplink BER was less sensitive to OSNR variations, and no significant crosstalk with downstream traffic was observed, since the two directions use different modulation formats and operating regimes.

### Channel Bonding in TDM-PON (Multi-Wavelength ONUs)
<!-- sources: tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond | gen: 3acc63620aef -->
Channel bonding lets an ONU use several wavelengths at once so that its capacity exceeds that of a single wavelength, which matters because access networks must reach 50 Gb/s and beyond per user while the ODN stays passive. In a prototype based on IEEE 50G-EPON with 25 Gb/s per wavelength, bonding is implemented at packet level in the multipoint reconciliation sublayer (MPRS), so that, for example, two wavelengths serve a 50G ONU. Because frames of the same flow travel on different wavelengths and can arrive with different delays, the receiver relies on envelope alignment buffers and EPAM markers to resynchronize and reorder the frames. In tests, bonding increased upstream throughput from 8.3 Gb/s to 16.7 Gb/s. Bonding is paired with a low-latency DBA so that the added capacity does not come at the cost of delay. For future 100 Gb/s and beyond ports, channel bonding is regarded as a complement to better modulation formats: aggregating multiple wavelengths can exceed 100 Gb/s even when the receiver sensitivity and chromatic dispersion of a single high-baud-rate channel limit the power budget.

### Coherent PON and Digital Subcarrier Multiplexing (DSCM)
<!-- sources: intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services, tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond | gen: 3fcce0423bd5 -->
For 100 Gb/s and beyond per port, the main challenge is keeping the power budget despite poor receiver sensitivity and chromatic dispersion at high speeds, and two physical-layer families are compared. Coherent detection, using DP-QPSK or DP-QAM, offers superior sensitivity and dispersion compensation, but its cost remains a barrier for ONU deployment. Direct detection, using NRZ-OOK, PAM or DQPSK combined with DSP, is simpler and cheaper but struggles with dispersion and baud-rate limits. Channel bonding of multiple wavelengths supplements either approach to exceed 100 Gb/s. Very High Speed PON (VHSP) initiatives explore coherent transmission combined with digital subcarrier multiplexing (DSCM), in which the optical carrier is divided into multiple digitally generated subcarriers (SCs) that can be allocated to different ONUs, services or links. In a DSCM-based coherent PON, specific subcarriers can be reserved for intra-PON ONU-to-ONU communication, keeping them distinct from regular OLT-ONU PON traffic. Subcarrier interleaving is used to manage bidirectional transmission on a single fiber, so that upstream and downstream (or the two directions of a direct ONU-to-ONU link) occupy interleaved subcarrier slots. The scheme coexists with legacy intensity-modulation direct-detection (IM-DD) PONs through wavelength separation, making it backward-compatible with deployed infrastructure. Because the different subcarriers are received together at a coherent receiver, the power imbalance between subcarriers arriving from different transmitters is a key design constraint, with a maximum allowable imbalance of about 10 dB. Tx/Rx DSP for such coherent transceivers contributes roughly 31 µs of processing latency per Tx/Rx pair in first-order latency models.

### Converged Coherent Metro + PON Architecture
<!-- sources: intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services | gen: 0d5bc536b4fa -->
Converged architectures extend coherent transmission from the metro core to the access edge without O-E-O conversion at intermediate nodes, using ROADMs. Three connection types are defined in this setting. Intra-PON: direct ONU-to-ONU links within one PON tree via taps. Pre-metro: communication between an ONU and a node located before the metro segment (e.g., a pre-metro DU), implemented with three taps that drop signals at that node. Inter-PON: all-optical links between ONUs in different PON trees, realized through optical splitters in the central optical matrix of the metro network. The first-order link budget for these cases includes the Optical Signal-to-Noise Ratio (OSNR) penalties of metro traversal (for example, a 4 dB penalty for a 17 dB OSNR). For pre-metro links, a main DU tap split ratio of 2/98% is found to be optimal for minimizing the power imbalance between the RU-DU link and the DU-OLT link.

### All-Optical ONU-to-ONU (Intra-PON) Communication via Asymmetrical Taps
<!-- sources: intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services | gen: 8f2d554dae2a -->
In conventional TDMA-based PONs all traffic must traverse the Optical Line Terminal (OLT), so communication between two ONUs in the same tree is hairpinned through the OLT's electronic switch, incurring O-E-O conversion and switching delay. A proposed remedy is to modify the optical distribution network (ODN) by adding asymmetrical splitters ("taps") that create direct all-optical paths between ONUs, supporting both intra-PON (within one tree) and inter-PON (between trees) connectivity. Four configurations are described. One-to-One (single stage): two asymmetrical taps and a short fiber patch-cord inside the splitter cabinet link two ONUs directly. One-to-Few (single stage): taps combined with a symmetrical splitter let one ONU (e.g., a Distributed Unit, DU) reach several others (e.g., Radio Units, RUs). Two-stage splitting, intra-stage: the same approach connects ONUs within the same second-stage cabinet. Two-stage splitting, inter-stage: ONUs in different second-stage cabinets are connected by modifying the first-stage splitter or adding a point-to-point fiber between cabinets. The approach needs only passive components and is combined with DSCM coherent PON, which assigns dedicated subcarriers to the direct traffic.

### PON Optical Link Budget and Power Imbalance Analysis
<!-- sources: intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services | gen: f1a6244ccfd9 -->
Feasibility of tap-based ONU-to-ONU links is assessed with a first-order link budget against the ITU-T N1 class (29 dB maximum loss), together with a maximum allowable power imbalance of 10 dB between subcarriers at the receiver. For One-to-One links over 40 km, an optimal tap split ratio of 20/80% needs only about 1.3 dB of additional margin beyond the N1 limit; for 20 km, 10/90% is optimal with roughly 0.75 dB extra margin. A One-to-Few configuration with one ONU reaching four others over 40 km remains feasible with about 2 dB additional margin. Statistical analysis using log-normal ODN loss distributions derived from real-world data identifies 10/90 and 20/80 as the most stable split ratios for keeping power balance. In two-stage splitting, intra-stage communication is feasible with minimal extra margin (0.5–2.2 dB), whereas inter-stage communication suffers significantly higher loss and requires much larger margins or specific split ratios to remain viable.

### Low-Latency Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA) and Cooperative DBA in PON
<!-- sources: tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond, xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: ac739501e4ac -->
Conventional TDM-PON DBA relies on fairness-based scheduling in which ONUs report queues and the OLT grants upstream slots, an approach that cannot meet the strict latency of Tactile Internet applications. A low-latency alternative uses differential Quality of Service, dividing uplink capacity into four tiers: Gold, Silver, Bronze and Best Effort. The Gold class uses a static cycle-based method, being served every 250 µs, which guarantees latency for time-critical tactile services. In a 50G-EPON prototype, Gold traffic kept latency below 0.4 ms regardless of load, while lower tiers exceeded 10 ms under high load. Such advanced DBA is considered sufficient for midhaul and backhaul, but fronthaul needs further reductions. Cooperative DBA (Co-DBA) addresses this: the OLT and the mobile equipment exchange traffic patterns through a Cooperative Transport Interface (CTI), so that the OLT allocates bandwidth proactively from actual mobile traffic demands instead of reacting to ONU requests, removing the request-grant negotiation delay. Co-DBA requires high-precision time synchronization, accurate to several microseconds, with IEEE 1588 or SyncE as candidates, and implies complex synchronization between the BBU and the OLT. Related mechanisms include accelerated DBA scheduling with multiple bursts per ONU per 125-µs frame. A standard-compliant alternative that avoids the BBU-OLT coupling is predictive DBA for XGS-PON. In conventional DBA, data that arrives at an ONU after its Report frame but before the next Gate/BWmap grant must wait at least one full cycle, which pushes upstream delay beyond the C-RAN fronthaul budget of about 300 µs; reactive schemes handle bursty traffic poorly. The Traffic Prediction-based Enhanced Residual Bandwidth Utilization (TP-ERBU) algorithm runs in the OLT and combines two ideas. First, a fourth-order weighted moving average estimates the additional data arriving during this waiting period, so the grant covers predicted as well as reported bytes. Second, instead of a constant maximum allocation limit A_max per ONU, it keeps a dynamic per-ONU limit A_i^t: ONUs whose predicted demand exceeds A_max are marked overloaded and receive a higher limit in the next cycle, lightly loaded ONUs have their limit reset to A_max, and the bandwidth left unused at the end of a cycle is pooled and divided uniformly among the overloaded ONUs in the following cycle. Time and space complexity are both O(N) in the number of ONUs. Compared with gGIANT (grouped assured bandwidth, which improves utilization but misses C-RAN latency requirements) and Optimized RR (a round-robin scheme that redistributes excess bandwidth but still struggles with bursts), simulations with 16 RRHs/ONUs over 10 km (RTT of 120 µs) and Option 7.2/7.3 splits report improvements of 20.59% in packet delay, 38.33% in channel utilization, 25.00% in packet loss, 5.71% in jitter and 15.56% in throughput against Optimized RR. For Option 7.3 the mean upstream delay stays under 300 µs up to a load factor of 0.8, against 0.6 for Optimized RR and 0.4 for gGIANT; packet loss falls by up to 40% versus gGIANT and throughput rises by up to 30%, with the largest gains at high load, where buffers are most likely to overflow.

### Lightweight Moving-Average Traffic Prediction versus ML for Real-Time DBA
<!-- sources: xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: 2f80af0dfae8 -->
Traffic prediction can shorten upstream delay in PON fronthaul by letting the OLT grant bandwidth for data that will arrive during the waiting period between an ONU's Report and the corresponding grant. Machine-learning and deep-learning predictors are accurate, but they typically need GPU inference, scale poorly (often quoted as O(N^2) in the number of ONUs), react slowly to sudden traffic variations and are difficult to reproduce, which limits their practicality inside a real-time scheduler. A lightweight alternative is a fourth-order weighted moving average: the OLT takes the arithmetic mean of the previous four cycles together with the differences between consecutive cycles, and assigns higher weights to more recent observations so the estimate adapts quickly to bursts. The resulting prediction P_i^t of additional data for ONU i is added to the reported queue when the grant is computed. All operations (polling, prediction, grant assignment) scale linearly, giving O(N) time and space complexity and no need for specialized hardware. In a comparison against a TensorFlow-based deep-learning predictor running on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, the moving-average predictor needed 0.01 ms per prediction versus 0.15 ms (a 93.33% reduction), while mean packet delay was 0.25 ms versus 0.28 ms, so the simple predictor was at least as good in delay while being far cheaper to compute. This is used to argue that, for schedulers operating within a sub-300 µs fronthaul budget, simple predictors are preferable to deep models when traffic is bursty and the decision must be made every DBA cycle.

## Fronthaul, X-haul and RAN Transport

### Centralized RAN (C-RAN) Fronthaul
<!-- sources: a_bidirectional_wdm-pon_free_space_optical_fso_system_for_fronthaul_5_g_c-ran_networks, a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure, an_ai-driven_intelligent_traffic_management_model_for_6g_cloud_radio_access_networks, elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport, enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond, intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies, xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: 4e64956a1b4b -->
Traditional Distributed RAN (D-RAN), with baseband processing at each antenna site, is too costly and power-hungry for the dense small-cell deployments of 5G and coordinates inter-cell interference poorly. The shift to Cloud-RAN (C-RAN) centralizes baseband processing in Base Band Units (BBUs) pooled at a Central Office (CO) or edge data center, while simple Radio Remote Heads (RRHs), also called Radio Remote Units (RRUs), Remote Units (RUs) or Remote Radio Frequency Units (RFUs), at cell sites handle the radio functions; the fronthaul is the transport segment linking the two. Modern architectures further divide the functions into Radio Units (RU), Distributed Units (DU) and Centralized Units (CU). A third variant, Fog RAN (F-RAN), integrates C-RAN with fog computing and AI at the edge to improve QoS through caching and real-time processing. The term x-haul is used collectively for the transport layers of such architectures: in the functional-split view it comprises front-haul (RU to DU), mid-haul (DU to CU, linking gNBs/eNBs, e.g. over X2) and back-haul (CU to 5G core, e.g. over S1), with typical distance limits of under 10 km, 40 km and 80 km respectively (data center interconnection typically under 120 km) and latency requirements that range from about 100 µs for CPRI/eCPRI to 10 s for mMTC; these differing distance and capacity demands dictate different modulation, detection and multiplexing technologies per segment. The degree of centralization determines transport pressure: moving more functions to the BBU raises the capacity and reliability required of the fronthaul regardless of actual user traffic (for example about 162 Gbps per sector for a 32x32 mMIMO configuration), so x-haul capability must be considered jointly with the functional split. Low-layer splits such as Split 8 can require up to 1.25 Tb/s in 6G, while Split 7.x options relax this, and URLLC targets call for one-way transport delays below about 250 µs. The two defining challenges of C-RAN fronthaul are the massive bandwidth requirement and a stringent latency constraint, typically below 300 µs. Pooling improves resource use and enables Coordinated Multipoint (CoMP) transmission, but it places three main demands on the access transport network: capacity (massive data rates for digital and analog Radio-over-Fiber (RoF) and massive MIMO), latency (strict synchronization and timing for centralized radio coordination), and dynamicity (pairing RRUs with BBUs in the pool as traffic fluctuates). Fully centralized deployments are ideal for pooling and coordination but impose very high bandwidth and low-latency demands on the fronthaul, so partial centralization is often adopted in practice to reduce the required capacity. Point-to-point (P2P) fiber links are limited to distances under 20 km and are insufficient for the broader traffic demands of 5G, while pure fiber fronthaul is also costly and sometimes infeasible because installing cable at every urban site faces cost and geographical barriers; high densification of small base stations additionally calls for wavelength or time multiplexing to avoid excessive fiber counts, and convergence with FTTH networks is suggested as a cost-reduction strategy in urban areas. Point-to-multipoint PON architectures are therefore favored, since fiber sharing (split ratios up to 1:256) reduces fiber usage; given the massive existing PON footprint, moving X-haul onto shared PON infrastructure is more scalable than dedicated point-to-point fiber, provided latency bottlenecks such as OLT hairpin switching are resolved. One cost-effective option is 10-Gigabit-capable Symmetric PON (XGS-PON), valued for its symmetry, simplicity and passive optical distribution network. In this mapping a centralized BBU pool connects to an OLT at the central office, and each RRH attaches to an ONU behind a passive splitter; the OLT, which sits next to the BBUs, can then predict RRH traffic and issue upstream grants proactively. Its main obstacle is upstream scheduling delay: the DBA cycle alone can exceed the fronthaul latency budget even when Option 7.x splits have already reduced the bandwidth need, and the quiet windows used to range newly joining ONUs can interrupt constant-bit-rate fronthaul flows. A proposed hybrid approach places the BBU at the CO and the RRHs at the Optical Network Units (ONUs) of a WDM-PON, using a combined single-mode fiber (SMF) and Free Space Optical (FSO) link. This design supports 20 Gbps downstream and 5 Gbps upstream per wavelength channel, for 320 Gbps aggregate downstream capacity, over up to 20 km of SMF plus roughly 700 m of free-space path, offering a lower-cost alternative to full fiber deployment. WDM front-haul schemes target aggregated capacities such as 300 Gb/s per fiber (twelve 25-Gb/s channels). In the C-band, a semi-active WDM-PON uses 20 BiDi wavelength pairs. In the O-band, two schemes are discussed: L-WDM extends LAN-WDM from eight to twelve channels using cyclic AWGs and optical circulators for bidirectional transmission, keeping the dispersion penalty below 1 dB over 10 km of standard SMF; M-WDM doubles the number of CWDM channels by shifting existing channels by ±3.5 nm, but suffers higher dispersion penalties than L-WDM and needs mitigation for 25-Gb/s NRZ signals. Elastic Optical Networks address the C-RAN requirements by offering the spectral granularity to carry analog and digital RoF signals on a single fiber and, through S-BVTs and software-defined reconfiguration, enabling any-to-any RRU-BBU assignment, which is essential for BBU pooling and improved CoMP. Grooming at the optical layer without O-E-O conversion further reduces end-to-end latency and energy consumption relative to electronic grooming. Seen from the BBU-pool side, the centralized BBUs act as control stations for the antennas and radios and generate heterogeneous traffic (from 6G, IoT and UAV users) that is handed to a cloud platform. That platform is commonly organized into three functional units: a traffic analysis unit that predicts probable congestion against pre-estimated thresholds, a traffic management unit that segregates traffic into states and selects scheduling and mapping strategies, and a cloud services unit that executes requests through storage, computing and networking services and returns usage feedback. This virtualization and customization creates a tension between customers, who want minimal latency, and providers, who want maximal bandwidth utilization at low operating cost; proactive traffic management on the cloud side is one way to reconcile them.

### X-haul Latency Budget and Functional Splits for 5G/6G
<!-- sources: intra-pon_communication_based_on_dscm_coherent-pon_for_ultra-low_latency_5g-6g_x-haul_services, tdm-pon-based_optical_access_network_for_tactile_internet_5g_and_beyond, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies, xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: 7e61feaa49b3 -->
Transport requirements depend on the chosen CU/DU/RU functional split. The 3GPP splits range from Option 1 (fully centralized) to Option 8 (fully distributed), and low-layer splits such as Split 8 demand enormous bandwidth (up to 1.25 Tb/s for 6G), exceeding current very high speed PON capabilities, which makes the intra-PHY Option 7.x series, balancing fronthaul capacity against control flexibility, more viable compromises. Fronthaul bandwidth depends on cell bandwidth, number of sub-carriers, modulation order, MIMO layers, I/Q sample size and antenna ports. Within the split-7 family, Option 7.1 transmits frequency-domain I/Q symbols, gives high performance but demands the maximum bandwidth and minimum latency (on the order of 4 Gbps in the cited configuration); Option 7.2 reduces the bandwidth relative to 7.1 by multiplexing signals from multiple antenna ports; and Option 7.3 carries bits rather than I/Q symbols, because modulation/demodulation moves to the RU near the antennas, lowering the capacity demand to about 134 Mbps for specific configurations at the price of greater RU complexity. Fronthaul rates scale with antenna count and sampling rate: a 32x32 MIMO mmWave setup at 3.5 GHz can require a CPRI rate of about 162 Gbps per sector. Massive MIMO and CoMP in ultra-dense deployments are the main drivers of the combined need for high bandwidth and ultra-low latency. For URLLC, end-to-end latency targets are as low as 0.5 ms, so the transport network must target one-way propagation delays below 250 µs. The fronthaul round-trip time must stay well below the Timing Advance window, typically targeting 200–500 µs with a timing accuracy of ±130 ns; for C-RAN over PON the commonly used uplink budget is below 300 µs. Projections are given for Small Cell, Macro Cell and Ultra Dense scenarios, and for 6G ultra-dense environments fronthaul demands are expected to reach 120–250 Gbps with latency of 50 µs or less. A first-order latency model accounts for fiber propagation (5 µs/km), Tx/Rx DSP processing (about 31 µs) and electrical switching delays (20–30 µs). A more detailed uplink model for PON fronthaul sums the transmission time of each frame, propagation delay, compression/decompression delay, FEC encoding/decoding delay and the queuing delay in the ONU buffer. In the analyzed case of synchronized phases, where CPRI frames reach the ONU simultaneously, the queuing delay is the dominant variable component, since it depends on how long data waits for an upstream grant; it is therefore the target of scheduling improvements, whereas the other terms are largely fixed by distance and hardware. Advanced DBA with differential QoS is considered sufficient for midhaul and backhaul, but fronthaul requires further latency reductions. Cooperative DBA (Co-DBA), in which the OLT learns mobile traffic patterns through a Cooperative Transport Interface and allocates bandwidth proactively, targets this case, and depends on time synchronization accurate to several microseconds using IEEE 1588 or SyncE. Prediction-based DBA that stays within the XGS-PON standard is an alternative, shortening the queuing term without BBU-OLT signaling. Direct all-optical ONU-to-ONU links reduce latency substantially: intra-PON from about 287 µs to 131 µs (~54% reduction) by removing the OLT switch and one set of Tx/Rx conversions; pre-metro from about 387 µs to 131 µs (~66%) by bypassing metro hops and OLT processing; and inter-PON from about 437 µs to 281 µs (~35%).

### Optical OFDM for Fronthaul Modulation
<!-- sources: a_bidirectional_wdm-pon_free_space_optical_fso_system_for_fronthaul_5_g_c-ran_networks, a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure | gen: ac8bd811bf48 -->
Optical Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) divides the data across many orthogonal subcarriers, which eliminates interference between subcarriers, increases spectral efficiency over On-off keying (OOK), and resists multipath fading, intersymbol interference (ISI), fiber dispersion, and atmospheric fading. In OFDM-PON, the orthogonal subcarriers also allow flexible spectrum allocation and 'optical grooming,' in which many small connections are aggregated into a single transmitter. Its main drawback is potential frequency offset caused by carrier mismatch. In a WDM-PON/FSO fronthaul downlink, 16-QAM intensity-modulated OFDM with 512 subcarriers and a 1024-point FFT carried a 40 Gbps PRBS stream as 20 Gbps per wavelength channel, modulated with Mach-Zehnder modulators and recovered by a PIN photodetector and quadrature demodulator. This made the downlink relatively insensitive to SMF length over 10-20 km, and BER improved with rising OSNR. The 5 Gbps NRZ OOK uplink, by contrast, was significantly more affected by increasing SMF length, since OOK is more susceptible to fiber impairments than OFDM. Combining OFDM downstream with OOK upstream thus trades off spectral efficiency and resilience against the simplicity and low cost of the RSOA-based upstream transmitter.

### Digital-Analog Radio-over-Fiber (DA-RoF)
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond | gen: f7ab7e8f01f7 -->
Analog radio-over-fiber (A-RoF) transports the radio waveform directly over fiber, but it is limited by SNR and error-vector magnitude (EVM), which makes high-order modulations such as OFDM-1024QAM difficult to support. Hybrid Digital-Analog RoF (DA-RoF) addresses this limitation: it uses cascaded probabilistic constellation shaping (PCS)-n-QAM stages to digitally approximate the waveform, and pulse code modulation (PCM) to represent the remaining approximation errors. The technique trades spectral efficiency, which is reduced by about 50% relative to A-RoF, for an SNR gain of more than 10 dB, lowering EVM to levels able to support 1024-QAM. DA-RoF is compatible with CPRI-style fronthaul, and by integrating 400G-ZR coherent transceivers it can scale to multi-Tb/s CPRI-equivalent bit rates per wavelength, which is relevant to future 6G fronthaul requirements.

### Wireless X-haul Technologies (mmWave, Microwave, THz, Satellite)
<!-- sources: x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies | gen: 979a341e9c59 -->
Wireless x-haul options are cost-effective alternatives or complements to fiber, which remains the gold standard for bandwidth and reliability but is limited by high deployment cost; copper (Ethernet/DSL) is considered outdated for 5G/6G demands. Wireless links are generally weather-dependent, which constrains availability and latency guarantees. Millimeter-wave links (30–300 GHz) offer data rates of roughly 1–2 Gbps but have short reach and require strict line-of-sight. Microwave links (6–60 GHz) reach farther, up to about 50 km, but have lower spectral efficiency and are susceptible to rain and fading. THz links (300 GHz–3 THz) have the potential for 100 Gbps and more, but their range is severely limited, below 100 m even with directional antennas. Free Space Optics (FSO) uses lasers through the air; it is license-free and high-capacity and can reduce deployment cost by about 50% relative to fiber, but it fails in fog or heavy rain. Satellites, notably LEO and high-throughput satellite (HTS) systems, provide connectivity for sparsely populated areas where terrestrial infrastructure is unavailable. The overall conclusion is that x-haul must be tailored to the specific RAN deployment: wireless solutions (mmWave, FSO, THz) offer cost advantages in certain contexts, while fiber optics remain the future-proof solution.

### Free Space Optical (FSO) Communication and Atmospheric Turbulence
<!-- sources: a_bidirectional_wdm-pon_free_space_optical_fso_system_for_fronthaul_5_g_c-ran_networks, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies | gen: c01a75e352fe -->
FSO transmits optical signals through the atmosphere between line-of-sight terminals, offering license-free spectrum, high bandwidth, and fast, flexible deployment. It is used as a last-mile link or wherever laying fiber is impractical, and as an x-haul option it is reported to reduce deployment costs by about 50% relative to fiber. Its main impairment is the atmosphere: link availability fails in fog or heavy rain, and atmospheric turbulence causes intensity scintillation and fading that reduce reliability, along with wavelength-dependent atmospheric absorption. Turbulence is commonly modeled with the Gamma-Gamma distribution, whose parameters alpha and beta capture large- and small-scale turbulence cells, and the refractive index structure parameter Cn^2 sets the turbulence strength from weak to strong. A quasi-static 'frozen channel' model treats the fading as constant over a symbol frame while varying between frames. In a simulated hybrid SMF-FSO WDM-PON fronthaul (Optiwave), bit error rate degraded as FSO distance increased. At the forward error correction (FEC) limit of about 3.8 x 10^-3, the downlink reached roughly 700-890 m depending on wavelength, with shorter wavelengths (such as lambda_16) performing worse than longer ones (lambda_1), and the uplink reached roughly 950-1000 m. The uplink's longer reach is attributed to the gain characteristics of the RSOA.

### Poisson-Pareto Burst Process (PPBP) Traffic Modeling for Fronthaul
<!-- sources: xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: 3075fd277e57 -->
The Poisson-Pareto Burst Process (PPBP) is a traffic model in which bursts arrive according to a Poisson process and each burst has a heavy-tailed, Pareto-distributed duration, with the traffic rate being the superposition of the concurrently active bursts. It reproduces the on-off nature of mobile user activity and the long-range dependence (self-similarity) observed in real 5G fronthaul traffic, which makes it suitable for stressing schedulers with bursty uplink load. In evaluations of Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation for XGS-PON-based C-RAN, PPBP traffic is injected at each RRH to emulate uplink data under different load factors. ## Elastic Optical Networks (EON)

### Elastic Optical Networks (EON) and Flexible Grid
<!-- sources: a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure, elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport, low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks | gen: f5732d269728 -->
Conventional WDM/DWDM networks are circuit-switched systems that establish lightpaths on a fixed grid defined by ITU-T G.694.1 (typically 12.5, 25, 50 or 100 GHz channel spacing), using transponders and (R)OADMs built on wavelength selective switches (WSS). They are bound by the wavelength continuity constraint, which requires a lightpath to keep the same wavelength on every link. Mandatory guardbands and the fixed channel width waste spectrum when demand does not match the grid size, reconfiguration often requires hardware changes because modulation is tied to specific transponder types, and electronic traffic grooming at the IP/MPLS layer forces optical-electrical-optical (O-E-O) conversions at intermediate nodes that add latency and power consumption. These rigidities cannot match the rigid-to-variable service mix of 5G. Elastic Optical Networks (EON) replace fixed wavelengths with a flexible grid in which the spectrum is discretized into narrow frequency units called sub-carriers or frequency slots; using techniques such as Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM), multiple adjacent sub-carriers are aggregated into a channel tailored to each connection's bandwidth demand, subject to spectrum contiguity (sub-carriers must be adjacent) and continuity/consistency constraints (the same slots must be used on every link of the path). In cloud-edge offloading models the EON serves as the transport for offloaded traffic, with spectrum divided into 12.5 GHz frequency slots and modulation formats ranging from BPSK to 16-QAM setting the slots needed per request. Enabling hardware includes bandwidth-variable transponders (BVTs) and sliceable BVTs (S-BVTs), whose software-configurable modulation formats (BPSK, QPSK, x-QAM) trade optical reach against spectral efficiency and which can be split into multiple flows; bandwidth-variable optical cross-connects (BVOXCs), which replace WSS-based nodes to provide flexible spectral switching windows; and digital signal processing (DSP), which adjusts bit rate, FEC and modulation according to distance and demand. Reported benefits are the elimination of guardbands between sub-carriers, fewer transponders through S-BVT virtualization, hitless software-defined reconfiguration of lightpaths to follow time-varying traffic, optical-layer grooming that removes O-E-O conversions (reducing latency and energy use), and versatility in supporting diverse services through different modulation formats. Migrating from fixed grids to EON is regarded as indispensable for providing the capacity, reliability and energy efficiency that a massive number of 5G-connected devices require, and implementing it raises challenges in routing, fragmentation, survivability and control that must be addressed to limit Capex and Opex.

### Routing and Spectrum Allocation (RSA) and Spectrum Fragmentation in EON
<!-- sources: a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure, elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport, elevating_optical_networks_machine_learning_approach_for_optimal_resource_scheduling_and_performance_boost, low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks | gen: d24749d5bfa4 -->
In Elastic Optical Networks, Routing and Spectrum Allocation (RSA) is the problem of choosing a path and a block of frequency slots for each lightpath request, subject to two constraints: spectrum contiguity (the allocated sub-carriers must be adjacent) and spectrum continuity (the same slots must be used on every link of the path). Modulation format selection is coupled to the problem because it sets both the spectral width required and the optical reach permitted by the physics of the link. For C-RAN, research calls for joint optimization of RRU-BBU assignment, routing, spectrum and modulation that respects strict latency and synchronization constraints, and for algorithms for dynamic lightpath establishment that balance latency and energy consumption as fronthaul and backhaul demands change over time and location. In cloud-edge offloading, RSA is additionally coupled with server selection and computing capacity: a request must be assigned a path and slots toward one server (whole processing) or two servers (partial processing), and the total computing used at a node may not exceed its capacity V_e nor spectrum usage on a link exceed the |F| available slots. A typical heuristic realization uses K-Shortest Path (KSP) routing with First Fit spectrum allocation, with complexity dominated by O(K × |S| × |L|), while the objective jointly minimizes average E2E latency and the number of occupied frequency slots. Dynamic setup and teardown of lightpaths of differing widths, especially under the high churn of 5G demands, leaves unused 'holes' in the spectrum, known as spectrum fragmentation; these gaps may be individually too small or non-contiguous to host new requests (non-aligned or non-continuous slot blocks), which are then blocked even though total free capacity suffices. Managing fragmentation, for instance through allocation policies or defragmentation, is therefore an open challenge alongside RSA, and the proposed direction is hitless, reactive defragmentation strategies that retune lightpaths without disrupting traffic. One allocation-side approach is the Fragmentation-Function-Fit (FFF) algorithm, which minimizes a Global Spectrum Fragmentation (GSF) evaluation function η(c). This function takes into account the total number of free subcarriers, the number of subcarriers required by each request (N_m), the probability that such a request is generated (P_m) and its duration (t_d(m)); a higher η(c) indicates greater global fragmentation, and FFF iteratively allocates resources so as to keep this value low. When combined with shortest-path routing, FFF prioritizes high-rate traffic flows, which consume the most spectrum, to minimize their blocking probability. In simulation it produced lower blocking probabilities than a scheme without the fragmentation-aware step at equivalent load, at the cost of higher computational complexity and longer running time. Both problems are tied to machine-learning-based traffic prediction, which aims to anticipate uncertain mobile traffic patterns to guide resource allocation.

### Bandwidth Variable Transponders (BVT/S-BVT) and Adaptive Modulation
<!-- sources: elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport | gen: c1ac821bc36c -->
Bandwidth Variable Transponders (BVTs) are the key transmission elements of Elastic Optical Networks. Supported by digital signal processing (DSP), they let software adjust the bit rate, forward error correction (FEC) and modulation format (e.g., BPSK, QPSK, x-QAM) of a lightpath according to path distance and traffic demand. This creates a trade-off between optical reach and spectral efficiency: lower-order formats such as BPSK and QPSK tolerate longer distances but use more spectrum per bit, whereas higher-order QAM formats pack more bits into fewer sub-carriers but only over shorter reaches. In contrast, fixed-grid transponders are tied to a specific modulation level, so changing it often requires hardware replacement. Sliceable BVTs (S-BVTs) extend the concept by virtualizing a single transponder into multiple independent flows, each of which can be directed to different destinations with its own spectral width and modulation, so that one device can serve several lightpaths. This reduces the total number of transponders needed and, in an IP-over-EON core, also the number of IP routers and BVOXC ports, lowering CapEx and OpEx. In C-RAN access transport, S-BVTs together with software-defined reconfiguration allow flexible, any-to-any RRU-BBU connectivity and the simultaneous carriage of analog and digital RoF signals on one fiber, and they enable hitless adjustment of lightpaths as traffic varies.

### Software-Defined Elastic Optical Network (SDEON) for Virtualized EPC Transport
<!-- sources: elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport | gen: ffe25ea82f25 -->
A Software-Defined Elastic Optical Network (SDEON) integrates an Elastic Optical Network with a Software Defined Networking (SDN) controller and is proposed for the 5G core transport segment, where aggregate data rates are expected to grow roughly 1000x relative to 4G, requiring Tbps transmission, and where traffic is highly unpredictable owing to user mobility and 'tidal' phenomena such as flash crowds. The motivating problem is the 4G Evolved Packet Core (EPC), whose reliance on proprietary hardware prevents elastic scaling; in particular, the tight coupling of control and user planes in the SGW and PGW yields poor resource utilization because their scaling needs differ (CPU-bound versus I/O-bound), which motivates virtualizing EPC functions as VNFs in distributed data centers. The SDEON supports this virtualized EPC in several ways. High capacity: adaptive modulation and variable spectrum widths reach Tbps rates more efficiently than inverse multiplexing over fixed-grid DWDM. Cost reduction: sliceable bandwidth-variable transponders (S-BVTs) reduce the number of required IP routers and BVOXC ports through virtualization, lowering CapEx and OpEx. VNF optimization: the controller uses global network knowledge to place VNFs optimally and to establish QoS-aware lightpaths, for example assigning latency-sensitive slices to the shortest paths. Prior work on mobile-core SDEON architectures has addressed load balancing and handovers, and experimental SDN testbeds have demonstrated elastic lightpath provisioning between RRUs and BBUs.

### Optical Network Challenges: Survivability, Energy and SDN Control
<!-- sources: a_review_of_optical_networking_technologies_supporting_5g_communication_infrastructure, elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport, elevating_optical_networks_machine_learning_approach_for_optimal_resource_scheduling_and_performance_boost | gen: 480f3703da5b -->
Beyond RSA and fragmentation, several open problems remain for flexible optical networks supporting 5G. Traffic prediction: machine learning, notably deep learning, is proposed to forecast uncertain mobile traffic as a function of time and location so resources can be allocated proactively. Survivability: protection and restoration schemes are needed to handle fiber or transponder failures, and differentiated survivability aims to provide different reliability levels according to the QoS requirements of each network slice. Energy reduction: efforts focus on cutting O/E/O conversions, for example through optical bypass, on intelligent sleep modes for EON devices and for antennas during low-traffic periods (relevant for both network and battery-powered sensing devices), and on new energy harvesting techniques integrated with optical bypass. Optimal resource planning is also linked to environmental sustainability, since better scheduling lowers the energy consumption and carbon footprint of communication networks. Control plane design: existing protocols such as GMPLS, OSPF and RSVP-TE could be extended, or new Software Defined Networking (SDN)-based orchestration layers designed, to manage end-to-end connectivity in a unified way across the wireless, transport and data-center domains. In Software Defined Optical Networking (SDON), a logical controller holds a global view of the network topology and resource utilization; it collects physical-layer data, feeds ML models (supervised channel-quality classification and unsupervised traffic clustering), and uses the resulting intelligence to apply adaptive spectrum scheduling algorithms. SDON is credited with flexible control and lower capital costs, but requires sophisticated algorithms to cope with volatile user demand and avoid congestion. Solving these challenges is aimed at minimizing capital (Capex) and operational (Opex) expenditure while meeting 5G performance targets.

### Fuzzy C-Means Traffic Flow Clustering for Spectrum Allocation
<!-- sources: elevating_optical_networks_machine_learning_approach_for_optimal_resource_scheduling_and_performance_boost | gen: 9e07459e3689 -->
Traditional bandwidth allocation in optical networks often ignores the individual demands of each Traffic Flow (TF). Fuzzy C-Means (FCM), an unsupervised soft-clustering algorithm in which each sample holds a degree of membership in every cluster, is used to partition TFs into groups with similar traffic patterns. ## Long-Haul and Transport Optics

### Probabilistic Constellation Shaping and Capacity-Approaching FEC
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond | gen: c152bb054abf -->
Reaching the Shannon limit in long-haul transmission depends on both forward error correction (FEC) and constellation design. High-performance codes such as CFEC, CFEC+ and OFEC, along with low-density parity check (LDPC) codes with 20-25% overhead, have been demonstrated, and soft-decision (SD) implementations now operate within about 1.4 dB of the SD Shannon limit for 16-QAM signals. Probabilistic constellation shaping (PCS) closes the remaining gap by optimizing the distribution of constellation points. Probabilistic Amplitude Shaping (PAS) is the main implementation: it combines a distribution matcher with systematic binary FEC encoding, which keeps the DSP simple and remains compatible with existing QAM formats and FEC codes. Field trials show that activating PCS can substantially extend transmission reach; for example, the error-free reach of 16-QAM was extended from 1500 km to 2000 km, corresponding to an effective OSNR gain of approximately 2 dB.

### Super C+L Band Long-Haul Transmission
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond | gen: ffc460095291 -->
Increasing the capacity of a single fiber requires wider amplification bandwidth. Super C+L erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) provide a 6 THz window in the super-C band and a 5 THz window in the super-L band, which allows up to 88 Tb/s of single-fiber capacity using 800-Gb/s channels on a 100-GHz grid. Modern long-haul systems combine digital coherent detection, subcarrier multiplexing and nonlinearity mitigation with flexible modulation and coding. Current commercial capabilities include per-fiber capacities of 88 Tb/s for data center interconnection and over 26 Tb/s for transatlantic links, with reaches extending up to 15,000 km.

### Fifth Generation Fixed Network (F5G) and Fiber Sensing
<!-- sources: enabling_optical_network_technologies_for_5g_and_beyond | gen: c4244bd90ecc -->
The Fifth Generation Fixed Network (F5G) aims to extend fiber connectivity beyond the home to every device and machine. ## Optical Data Center Networks

### Data Center Traffic Characteristics (Mice/Elephant Flows, Locality, Burstiness)
<!-- sources: optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges | gen: 049e8018832b -->
Data center traffic is analyzed as flows, sequences of packets related to one task, and is highly heterogeneous. It falls into three priority classes: online/real-time applications (AR/VR, IoT) that are time-sensitive and need the highest priority; computing applications (big data, AI training) that need medium priority and low packet loss; and storage back-up, which is bandwidth-heavy but delay-tolerant and low priority. Locality is dominated by East-West traffic internal to the data center, about 75% of the total; intra-rack ratios range from 10-40% in university and enterprise data centers up to 80% in commercial clouds owing to strategic server placement. Most flows are mice flows (<10 KB), but a small share of elephant flows (>100 MB) carries the majority of transferred bytes, and AI/ML applications generate notably longer and larger flows than standard cloud services. Packet sizes are bimodal, with short control packets of about 200 B and large data packets of about 1400-1500 B; interarrival times depend on the application, with media streaming needing very low values (<12 μs). TCP slow start and hardware offloading such as Large Send Offload produce high burstiness, which causes buffer occupancy, data loss and longer task completion time, particularly at the ToR tier. Link utilization is typically higher in the core tier than in aggregation or ToR tiers, yet congestion and packet loss are more frequent at the lower tiers because of bursty patterns. Link rates have grown from 10 Gb/s (2007) to today's dominant 400 Gb/s, moving toward 800 Gb/s with coherent DSP technology and 1.6T and 3.2T targets, with DWDM replacing CWDM. These traits drive design objectives of deterministic μs latency and high throughput, scalability, millisecond-scale reconfigurability via SDN or ML/AI, and fault tolerance through redundant links and hybrid switching.

### Data Center Network Topologies and Power Consumption
<!-- sources: optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges | gen: b6e9a39220e8 -->
Cooling accounts for about 40% of total data center power, and efficiency is measured by Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE); hyperscale facilities reach a PUE of about 1.1 through liquid cooling and free cooling, such as siting in cold climates. AI workloads sharply raise energy demand: GPUs consume up to 15 times more power than CPUs, AI queries such as ChatGPT requests are far more energy-intensive than standard searches, and forecasts point to tripled energy demand by 2030. Topology affects network power: hierarchical Tree-tier architectures are the most energy-efficient because they use fewer switches and links, but suffer capacity bottlenecks; Fat-Tree balances performance and efficiency; BCube and DCell are generally less efficient because servers act as relay nodes and need more network interfaces. Reduction directions include replacing electrical switches with optical ones to remove O/E/O conversions (Google reported a 41% saving in Jupiter); silicon photonics using MRR and MZI structures, with phase change materials such as VO2 and GST giving non-volatile, energy-efficient switching; transceiver choices such as VCSELs for short reach and coherent lasers for inter-data-center links; and management via SDN-based energy-aware routing that powers down underutilized links, plus ML/AI traffic prediction to deactivate idle devices.

### Optical Switching Technologies and Paradigms in Data Center Networks (OCS, OPS, OBS)
<!-- sources: optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges | gen: 36c7c498b096 -->
Traditional three-tier data center networks (ToR, Aggregation, Core) rely on electrical switches that require frequent optical-to-electrical-to-optical (O/E/O) conversions, causing high power consumption and bandwidth bottlenecks from oversubscription and hardware limits such as switch chip pin counts; co-packaged optics is an emerging mitigation. Optical switching offers bit-rate transparency, lower power per bit and a decoupling of port count from data rate, and comes in three paradigms. Optical Circuit Switching (OCS) establishes a dedicated optical path before transmission; it provides high bandwidth and is deployed by companies such as Google using MEMS, but suffers from ms-scale setup times and poor utilization under bursty traffic. Optical Packet Switching (OPS) routes each packet individually, giving high flexibility and statistical multiplexing, but is currently non-viable at scale because optical RAM is absent. Optical Burst Switching (OBS) sends a control header ahead of the data burst, shortening setup time relative to OCS and avoiding buffers, but needs complex contention resolution to prevent dropped bursts. Enabling technologies differ in speed and scale: 2D-MEMS switch in sub-μs but have limited port counts, while 3D-MEMS support hundreds of ports but switch in ms; LCoS and piezoelectric switches operate at ms scale; micro-ring resonators (MRR) and Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZI) use thermo-optic or electro-optic effects for μs to ns speeds, with silicon photonics integration reaching ns; wavelength routing with arrayed waveguide gratings (AWG) achieves ns switching limited by tunable laser speed; and semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOA) switch in ns but are limited by amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) noise. OCS is currently used in core tiers, while all-optical networks with ns-scale switching are the longer-term target.

### Disaggregated Data Center Architecture
<!-- sources: optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges | gen: 6d934fbcf410 -->
Disaggregation follows a resource-centric paradigm in which CPU, memory and storage are separated from fixed servers into shared pools, eliminating the resource fragmentation of server-centric designs. ### Open Challenges for All-Optical DCNs (ns Control, Burst-Mode CDR, Optical Buffering)
<!-- sources: optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges | gen: 364cb1c5438d -->
Several barriers remain before all-optical packet-switched data center networks are practical. First, although physical switching can occur in ns, the decision process (header processing and control) is still too slow, and scaling control logic to ns levels is a critical open problem. Second, optical receivers must perform clock data recovery (CDR) within ns for bursty traffic at 800 Gb/s and above; otherwise throughput falls and latency rises. Third, the lack of optical RAM prevents true Optical Packet Switching; Fiber Delay Lines (FDLs) exist but are too complex and add excessive queuing delay at large scale. Fourth, since there is no buffering, contention resolution must be essentially perfect to avoid data loss, requiring more advanced bandwidth allocation and access control schemes that account for the burstiness of AI-driven traffic. These challenges sit alongside the broader directions of self-driven reconfigurability, space division multiplexing and disaggregation.

### Resource Scheduling in Optical Data Center Networks (ODCNs)
<!-- sources: elevating_optical_networks_machine_learning_approach_for_optimal_resource_scheduling_and_performance_boost, optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges | gen: eafc9c98aa31 -->
Resource scheduling in dynamic computing environments aims to optimize asset use, reduce latency and prevent bottlenecks; in optical data center networks (ODCNs), flexible optical transport carries large data volumes over long distances with minimal degradation. Bandwidth allocation in ODCNs is organized around effective access control that minimizes latency and packet loss, and it differs between intra-rack and inter-rack communication. For intra-rack communication, systems often use Passive Optical Interconnects (POI) such as couplers or splitters to reduce power. Distributed access control is preferred over centralized SDN controllers because it avoids a single point of failure and reduces delay. Synchronous strategies can totally avoid collisions through Pre-Transmission Coordination (PTC) but require costly synchronization, whereas asynchronous strategies are more flexible but rely on Carrier Sensing (CS) or Control Messages (CM). For inter-rack communication, allocation configures optical switches to route lightpaths between Top-of-Rack (ToR) switches; early hybrid electrical/optical prototypes used MEMS, while recent all-optical prototypes use SOA-based switching for ns-scale reconfiguration. Most inter-rack schemes use synchronous transmission with PTC or Routing and Wavelength Assignment (RWA) algorithms to eliminate collisions. At the network level, an SDON-based scheduling scheme uses a controller with a global view of topology and resource use, shortest-path routing, ML-based channel quality assessment, and traffic-flow clustering, with high-rate flows prioritized through a fragmentation-aware allocation algorithm to minimize blocking. The workflow collects physical-layer data into a database, trains the classifier and clustering models, and then applies the adaptive spectrum allocation. Evaluation used a 13-node NSFNET topology with three data centers and 22 links. The combined approach reduced blocking probability and improved spectrum efficiency relative to traditional methods; the DBWO-RF-based mechanism showed its advantage particularly under high load, reaching a maximum spectrum resource utilization efficiency of 84.1%, while the fragmentation-aware allocation step achieved lower blocking at the cost of longer running time. Noted limitations include a focus on high traffic flow rates, difficulty handling non-linear systems, potential overfitting to specific datasets, and the need to extend evaluation to varied traffic patterns for scalability. Because optical fabrics lack buffering, contention resolution must be near-perfect, which motivates allocation schemes that account for the burstiness of AI-driven traffic.

## Cloud RAN Resource Management and Edge Offloading

### Traffic-State Classification and Resource Mapping in Cloud RAN
<!-- sources: an_ai-driven_intelligent_traffic_management_model_for_6g_cloud_radio_access_networks | gen: 710b742c96cf -->
To reconcile low latency with high utilization, a Cloud RAN traffic management unit can classify live jobs into states according to their bandwidth demand and execution time, using configurable thresholds on each. Five states are defined: light jobs (low bandwidth, low execution time), stragglers (low bandwidth, high execution time), network hogs (high bandwidth, low execution time), heavy jobs (high bandwidth, high execution time), and average jobs (all remaining traffic). Each state receives its own scheduling and mapping strategy. Light jobs are served by First-Come First-Serve (FCFS). Stragglers are mapped to virtual nodes on physical machines (PMs) with the highest CPU and memory capacity so they can carry out their I/O operations. Network hogs use hybrid scheduling and are executed in parallel with stragglers, exploiting the resources left idle while stragglers wait on I/O. Heavy jobs are distributed across multiple high-capacity PMs to satisfy their large demands. Average jobs are sorted by deadline and priority and mapped to servers ordered by decreasing processing speed, so the most urgent requests finish soonest. Varying the bandwidth and execution-time thresholds changes the share of traffic falling into each state, showing the classification's sensitivity to these parameters. In simulations, the hog-straggler parallelism reduced job execution latency by 18.37%-27.12% relative to FCFS depending on thresholds, and bandwidth utilization improved by 17.07% over JUFO (Joint UE and Fog Optimization), 17.31% over random-fit and 18% over first-fit.

### Virtual Node and Physical Machine Resource Allocation for RAN Workloads
<!-- sources: an_ai-driven_intelligent_traffic_management_model_for_6g_cloud_radio_access_networks | gen: 71e3542e3311 -->
Resource allocation for Cloud RAN workloads can be formulated as mapping each job to a virtual node (VN) hosted on a physical machine (PM), subject to capacity constraints. A set of constraints requires that every job is assigned to exactly one VN on exactly one PM, and that the bandwidth, CPU and memory requested by the job do not exceed the available capacity of the VN or of the PM underneath it; aggregate demand above PM capacity for longer than a threshold period is what defines congestion. The allocation objective is to minimize job latency and CPU idle time, with the mapping strategy chosen per traffic state (e.g., high-capacity PMs for stragglers, multi-PM distribution for heavy jobs). When traffic is sub-normal, workloads are consolidated onto a minimum number of PMs to save resources, while predicted congestion triggers diversion to reserved PMs. Operationally, the system initializes lists of jobs, VNs and PMs and then loops over time intervals: predict the next-interval load, compute deviation, determine congestion status, classify traffic states, and apply the allocation rules. The overall time complexity is O(PMT), where P is the number of PMs, M the number of RAN requests and T the number of time intervals. Evaluations simulated IBM server configurations for PMs and Amazon VM instances for VMs on a dual Intel Xeon Silver 4114 (40 cores, 128 GB RAM) host.

### Cloud-Edge Elastic Optical Networks (CE-EON) and Partial Computation Offloading
<!-- sources: low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks | gen: b9877abc9e73 -->
A Cloud-Edge Elastic Optical Network (CE-EON) integrates three layers: IoT terminals and base stations, edge computing nodes, and cloud computing nodes, interconnected by an elastic optical transport network. It is commonly modeled as a weighted undirected graph comprising base stations, cloud nodes, edge nodes, cloud and edge fiber links, and optical switches. Motivated by the growth of IoT, 5G and cloud services, the central problem is to decide, for each user request, whether to offload it and to which server, so as to reduce end-to-end (E2E) latency while using network resources efficiently. Prior work on offloading falls into binary offloading (a task is executed entirely locally or entirely remotely), partial task offloading (the data volume is split between local and remote execution), and cloud-edge collaboration. Partial resource offloading in a CE-EON distinguishes two service types: indivisible services are processed whole, either at the local edge server, at an edge server in an adjacent region, or at a cloud server; divisible services are split by a proportional segmentation ratio λ_u, with a share processed locally and the remainder (1 − λ_u) offloaded to another region. A binary offloading decision variable M_u (M_u = 1 for local processing, M_u = 0 for offloading) is derived from the local region's computing resource utilization ratio and from E2E latency relative to the network's extremes. Requests are characterized by source, bandwidth requirement, computing resource need and traffic type, and both static scenarios (resources are held until all requests are handled) and dynamic scenarios (resources are released after processing) are studied. Evaluation uses average E2E latency, blocking probability (requests failing for lack of spectrum or computing resources), spectrum occupancy ratio and average hop count.

### End-to-End Latency Modeling for Offloaded Services
<!-- sources: low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 6021419655cf -->
End-to-end (E2E) latency of an offloaded request is the sum of transmission time from the terminal to the processing node and processing time at the server, and its modeling differs for indivisible and divisible services. Indivisible services are processed whole in one of three scenarios: at the local edge server (Scenario 1), at an edge server in an adjacent region (Scenario 2), or at a cloud server (Scenario 3), each with its own transmission and processing terms. Divisible services are split with a proportional segmentation ratio λ_u: the fraction λ_u is processed locally and the remainder (1 − λ_u) is transmitted to and processed at another region. Because the two parts run in parallel, the total E2E latency is the maximum of the local processing time and the remote transmission-plus-processing time. Minimizing this maximum yields the optimal segmentation ratio, obtained by setting local processing time equal to remote transmission plus processing time, so that neither branch becomes the bottleneck. The offloading decision M_u (local or offloaded) is determined from the local region's computing resource utilization ratio and E2E latency relative to the network's extremes. Related lines of work on E2E latency study joint communication and computing resource allocation, task segmentation strategies and SDN-based load balancing; a collaborative cloud-edge baseline instead segments tasks by normalized backhaul communication and cloud computing capability and applies KKT conditions to jointly allocate resources. Reported results show that segmentation is not always beneficial: approaches that split too many services can be slower than local processing for tasks that would finish faster locally.

### Server Importance Degree (SID) for Load-Balanced Server Selection
<!-- sources: low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 59a07133f239 -->
The Server Importance Degree (SID) is a metric used to select the destination server for an offloaded request among edge and cloud servers. It is computed as a normalization of two quantities, the E2E latency to reach and use a server and the server's available computing resources, so that a server with low latency and ample spare capacity receives a high SID. Selecting the server with the highest SID therefore trades off low latency against regional load balance, avoiding overloading servers that are close but nearly saturated while still preferring nearby capacity. In the proportional-segmentation heuristic, indivisible services are sent to the highest-SID server, while divisible services are processed locally when the local load is below a threshold and otherwise split by the optimal ratio λ_u, with the remainder sent to the highest-SID server. The all-resource-offloading baseline (ARO) also uses SID but offloads every request whole, which in simulation produced the highest blocking probability, spectrum occupancy and hop count among the heuristics, illustrating that SID alone without segmentation does not suffice. By contrast, all-local-processing (ALP) ignores remote servers and picks the local server with the most available resources, blocking requests when none suffices, so it uses the least spectrum and a single hop but its latency degrades as load grows and local servers become bottlenecks.

### ILP and Heuristic Formulations for Joint Offloading and Spectrum Allocation
<!-- sources: low-latency_partial_resource_offloading_in_cloud-edge_elastic_optical_networks | gen: 6ce47fd26aa3 -->
Joint offloading and spectrum allocation in a cloud-edge elastic optical network can be formulated as an Integer Linear Programming (ILP) model minimizing the weighted objective P(G) = αT(G) + βF(G), where T(G) is the average E2E latency and F(G) the total number of occupied frequency slots. Constraints include selection uniqueness (each request uses exactly one path and either one server for whole processing or two servers for partial processing), node selection (a server cannot be the source node), capacity (computing used at a node cannot exceed V_e, and spectrum use on a link cannot exceed the |F| available slots), and spectrum constraints (allocated slots must be contiguous in frequency and consistent across all links of the path). Because the extensive binary variable space makes the problem NP-hard, heuristics are used. Partial Resource Offloading based on Proportional Segmentation (PRO_PS) classifies requests by type, sends indivisible ones to the highest-SID server, and processes divisible ones locally under a load threshold or else splits them at the optimal λ_u. Baselines are PRO_AS (fixed 50/50 average segmentation for divisible services), the Collaborative Cloud-Edge (CCE) approach (KKT-based joint resource allocation), All-Resource-Offloading (ARO, whole offloading to the highest-SID server) and All-Local-Processing (ALP, local processing or blocking). The heuristics are dominated by K-shortest-path routing, O(K × |S| × |L|), plus First Fit spectrum allocation, while ARO and ALP are cheapest because they skip service classification and ratio calculation. In a static 6-node network with modulation formats from BPSK to 16-QAM, PRO_PS reached an average latency only 3.4% above the ILP, and its spectrum occupancy and average hops were close to the ILP's; ALP had the lowest spectrum occupancy and hops (1 hop), ARO the highest, and CCE was slower than PRO_AS. In a dynamic 14-node network with 20,000 requests, PRO_PS had the lowest blocking probability and the lowest average latency among the heuristics, with ARO worst on blocking, ALP degrading as load rose, and PRO_PS offering the better balance of performance against resource usage than CCE or ARO.

## Traffic and Time-Series Prediction

### AI-Based Traffic Prediction and Congestion Detection in C-RAN
<!-- sources: an_ai-driven_intelligent_traffic_management_model_for_6g_cloud_radio_access_networks, elastic_optical_networking_for_5g_transport | gen: 05e2b1e1b82e -->
Deep-learning and other machine-learning models are proposed to predict mobile traffic patterns as a function of time and location, so that Elastic Optical Networks can allocate just-enough spectrum to each connection and obtain statistical multiplexing gains instead of provisioning for peak demand, which is especially relevant given the uncertainty and churn of 5G traffic. In C-RAN, congestion can be anticipated by a traffic analyser that combines a machine-learning predictor with pre-estimated thresholds. One approach uses Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), an ensemble of ℓ base learners (decision trees) trained to minimize an objective consisting of a loss term, which reduces prediction error, and a regularization term that limits overfitting by penalizing tree complexity through the number of leaves and the leaf weights; a Taylor expansion of the loss is used to optimize each tree. The predictor forecasts RAN load for the next interval, and the deviation between live and predicted traffic is compared with a threshold over a time interval Δt. A traffic status value is then assigned: 1 for congestion, -1 for sub-normal traffic, and 0 for normal traffic. Congestion is formally declared when the aggregate demand for bandwidth, CPU or memory exceeds the available capacity of the physical machines for longer than a threshold time period. Anticipated congestion triggers diversion of traffic across multiple paths to physical machines reserved for heavy workloads, whereas sub-normal traffic is consolidated onto a minimum number of machines to save resources. The cloud services unit returns resource-usage and job-type feedback to refine the predictor's training samples. In evaluations on call detail record and YouTube mobile streaming datasets, the XGBoost predictor showed low mean squared error and mean absolute error and, in some scenarios (e.g., a Trentino dataset), lower estimation error than a federated meta-learning approach (FMLA-d); the resulting scheme reduced congestion by up to 10.15% compared with an online secure communication model (OSC-MC).

### LSTM Encoder-Decoder (Seq2Seq) for Multivariate Time-Series Prediction
<!-- sources: edge_intelligence_in_softwarized_6g_deep_learning-enabled_network_traffic_predictions, hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization | gen: 32085614875c -->
Network traffic at the edge is non-linear, aperiodic and sporadic, and plain Recurrent Neural Networks struggle to capture its long-term dependencies because of the vanishing gradient problem. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cells address this with a memory unit (the cell state) whose content is regulated by three gates: forget, input and output. Because error derivatives are distributed through sums during back-propagation through time, the cell can learn long-range correlations across lengthy multivariate sequences. The Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is a related recurrent cell commonly used as an alternative, and LSTM and GRU are both regarded as superior to ensemble methods for sequential temporal dependencies, serving as standard recurrent baselines. In one 6G resource allocation study, LSTM and GRU reached R^2 of 96.82% and 96.46%, and a hybrid Random Forest–GRU–attention model surpassed them while converging in 15–20% fewer epochs than LSTM. A sequence-to-sequence (s2s) encoder-decoder built from LSTM cells uses a single-layer structure: the encoder processes a set of past observations (input sequence X_i) into an encoded temporal representation, and the decoder, initialized with a repeat vector and the encoder's cell state, reconstructs the target sequence (Y_o) over a future horizon window. Training minimizes the Huber loss, which behaves as squared loss for small errors and absolute loss for large ones, switching at a threshold tau = 1, making it robust to sudden traffic shifts. In a reported evaluation on one month of multivariate flow data (43,000 records at 5-minute intervals, split 65% training / 35% testing), the model used 100 LSTM cells per layer, min-max normalization to [-1, 1], a 20-hour lookback to predict the next 10 hours, batch size 32, 40 epochs, the Adam optimizer and a learning-rate-schedule callback, implemented with TensorFlow, Keras and Scikit-learn. Training and validation losses converged after about epoch 20 without significant overfitting. Mean, minimum, maximum and standard deviation of databytes were predicted with R^2 close to 1 and RMSE close to 0, whereas total traffic bytes was predicted less accurately (RMSE 231.64, R^2 0.686).

### Hybrid Random Forest–GRU with Attention for Network Traffic Prediction
<!-- sources: hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization | gen: 3c72ab673a4b -->
Traditional statistical or rule-based traffic predictors such as ARIMA/SARIMA struggle with the non-linear, dynamic and bursty nature of 6G traffic, motivating hybrid AI models that combine ensemble learning with recurrent sequence modeling. One proposed architecture pairs a Random Forest (RF) regressor with a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) and an attention mechanism, so that RF captures stationary non-linear interactions among features and the GRU captures dynamic temporal correlations. It operates in two stages. In the first, the RF regressor is trained on the flattened time-series windows and produces a prediction ŷ_RF that serves as an auxiliary feature. This prediction is tiled across the sequence length to form X_RF and concatenated with the original sequence X into an augmented sequence X_aug. In the second stage, X_aug is fed to a GRU layer whose hidden states H are weighted by an attention layer into a context vector c, and a dense output layer converts c into the final prediction. The training pipeline is: Min-Max normalization, sliding-window sequence generation, RF training, sequence augmentation, then GRU/attention training with the Adam optimizer, MSE loss and early stopping; hyperparameters were chosen by grid search on minimum validation RMSE. The model was evaluated on a real-world testbed dataset of 399 samples with eight features (timestamp, user ID, signal strength in dBm, latency, required bandwidth, allocated bandwidth, resource allocation as target, and application type), using a 30-step window and an 80/20 train/test split with 10% of training data held out for validation. It also converged in 15–20% fewer epochs than LSTM, and loss curves showed stable training without significant overfitting. The gain is attributed to the attention layer's focus on salient time steps and the RF component's handling of non-linear dependencies that deep models alone may miss. Reported comparison points from the literature include CatBoost at 95.1% and a general neural network at 93% R². ### Attention Mechanism for Time-Series Forecasting
<!-- sources: hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization | gen: e54a5defbe9b -->
Attention layers added on top of recurrent networks let a forecaster focus on the most informative time steps of an input window rather than relying only on the final hidden state. In a GRU-based forecaster, the attention layer takes the sequence of hidden states H produced by the GRU and computes a score for each time step using a tanh activation applied with learnable weights and biases. A softmax function then normalizes these scores into attention weights α_t that sum to one, and the context vector c is obtained as the weighted sum of the hidden states, c = Σ_t α_t h_t. A dense output layer maps this context vector to the final prediction. Because the weights are learned jointly with the recurrent layer, the model can prioritize specific time steps, for instance those that precede traffic bursts, while down-weighting uninformative ones. In a hybrid 6G traffic prediction model where the GRU input is augmented with Random Forest predictions, the attention layer is credited, together with the RF component, for reducing RMSE by over 69% relative to an LSTM baseline (R^2 99.70% versus 96.82%) and for stable convergence without significant overfitting. Attention is presented as the element that, combined with ensemble and recurrent components, represents the current frontier of performance for network traffic forecasting.

### Time-Series Forecasting Evaluation Metrics and Preprocessing (RMSE, MAE, MAPE, R²)
<!-- sources: hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization, xgs-pon-standard_compliant_dba_algorithm_for_option_7x_functional_split-based_5g_c-ran | gen: f586bbaf3153 -->
Forecasting models for network traffic are typically compared with four metrics: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) and the Coefficient of Determination (R²). Predictions and targets are inverse-transformed from the normalized scale before metrics are computed, so that errors are interpretable in real-world units. The usual training objective is Mean Squared Error (MSE) minimized with the Adam optimizer, often with early stopping and hyperparameter selection by grid search on minimum validation RMSE. ### Graph Partitioning and Efficient Attention for Large-Scale Road Networks
<!-- sources: edge_computing_enabled_large-scale_traffic_flow_prediction_with_gpt_in_intelligent_autonomous_transport_system_for_6g_network | gen: 393300e1dfe5 -->
Large road networks, modeled as undirected graphs with roads as nodes, are hard to process whole, so they are partitioned into a set of subgraphs, one per edge cluster. RoadSort performs this segmentation while limiting information loss by selecting 'important' roads as subgraph centers. It extends PageRank in two ways: it adds Betweenness Centrality to identify transport hubs, and it sets the damping factor β from the actual shortest-path distance between roads instead of a constant. In ablations RoadSort preserved accuracy better than Degree Centrality and plain PageRank. Increasing the number of subgraphs beyond 10 degraded accuracy because excessive edge cutting severs spatial dependencies. Within each subgraph, standard multi-head self-attention (MHSA) over nodes has O(n^2) complexity. Square Target Board Multi-Head Self Attention (STBMHSA) instead maps roads into a 'square target board' of R regions, and each road attends to these regional representations at different granularities, reducing complexity to linear O(Rn). Each ST block pairs STBMHSA, which captures spatial relations, with a standard MHSA applied to each road's temporal sequence to capture time dependencies. STBMHSA proved both more efficient and more accurate than standard MHSA and than purely local neighborhood attention.

## Edge AI Infrastructure and MLOps

### Edge-Distributed Training with Parameter Transfer and Pruning
<!-- sources: edge_computing_enabled_large-scale_traffic_flow_prediction_with_gpt_in_intelligent_autonomous_transport_system_for_6g_network | gen: a9fb56fa7e22 -->
To avoid central server bottlenecks when training on large-scale traffic data, a separate model is deployed on each edge cluster, one per road-network subgraph. Computing resources, meaning the number of servers per cluster, are allocated in proportion to the number of roads in the corresponding subgraph. Training follows an 'inference while train' mode based on transfer learning: the first cluster's model is trained, and its parameters initialize the model of the adjacent cluster, and so on, so later clusters start from a useful state rather than from scratch. To prevent overfitting and keep models lightweight on limited edge hardware, a pruning strategy removes two attention heads from the MHSA and STBMHSA modules every three clusters. Among tested schedules, Equal Pruning-2 (removing 2 heads) gave the best balance between efficiency and accuracy. Combined with graph segmentation and a frozen GPT-2 backbone (only LayerNorm fine-tuned), this gave faster convergence and lower GPU memory use than non-frozen deep learning baselines, measured by total training time, average subgraph training time and GPU memory.

### Federated Learning and Model Compression for Edge AI in 6G
<!-- sources: hybrid_model_for_6g_network_traffic_prediction_and_wireless_resource_optimization, resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey, review_on_6g_communication_and_its_architecture_technologies_included_challenges_security_challenges_and_requirements_applications_with_respect_to_ai_domain | gen: cbdbc6227961 -->
Deploying AI for 6G network management raises three recurring challenges: scalability, privacy and real-time adaptation. Edge AI processes data near its source to reduce latency, and the 6G AI-native vision includes tiny AI for resource-constrained devices and federated AI for decentralized training, positioning both as means of cutting latency and preserving privacy. Scalability concerns the high computational cost of sophisticated models, such as hybrid ensemble-plus-recurrent predictors, on resource-constrained edge devices that have limited storage and compute. Proposed remedies are model compression and knowledge distillation, in which a smaller student model is trained to approximate a larger teacher, yielding lightweight architectures suitable for the edge. Privacy concerns arise because training traffic and resource-allocation predictors requires massive data collection from users, with associated ethical risks. Federated Learning (FL) is suggested as a solution: models are trained across devices or edge clusters and only model updates are exchanged, so raw user data never leaves its origin. Complementary privacy-preserving techniques include differential privacy. In slice resource management, FL is listed among the machine learning methods for privacy-preserving decentralized training, alongside multi-agent DRL for decentralized control and Large Language Models (LLMs) for human-friendly slice configuration as future AI directions. Real-time adaptation concerns the need for models to cope with abrupt traffic surges or anomalies that shift the data distribution after deployment. Suggested approaches are online learning, which updates models incrementally as new samples arrive, reinforcement learning, and adaptive control systems that adjust resource allocation dynamically. Future work in this direction centers on lightweight architectures for resource-constrained devices and on improved model interpretability, which is linked to regulatory compliance. Together these techniques aim to let accurate predictors, such as a Random Forest–GRU–attention model that reached R² of 99.70% on 6G resource allocation data, run proactively at the edge without centralizing sensitive data or exhausting device resources.

### Kubeflow and Kubernetes-Based MLOps at the Edge
<!-- sources: edge_intelligence_in_softwarized_6g_deep_learning-enabled_network_traffic_predictions | gen: d746463d50df -->
Kubeflow is a machine learning toolkit that runs on Kubernetes (K8s) and manages the ML lifecycle, including data preparation, model training, hyperparameter tuning and deployment, while leveraging high-performance computing capabilities to auto-scale ML workloads. In an edge deployment, a K8s cluster spans a softwarized testbed: the Provisioning and Orchestration (P+O) center at the GIST site acts as the cluster master that orchestrates AI microservices through Kubeflow, while edge hyper-converged boxes (µ-boxes) serve as worker nodes. ### eBPF-Based Traffic Data Collection and Streaming Pipeline
<!-- sources: edge_intelligence_in_softwarized_6g_deep_learning-enabled_network_traffic_predictions | gen: ea68ebb87ebc -->
Network traffic statistics for edge intelligence can be gathered with extended Berkeley Packet Filtering (eBPF), which runs sandboxed programs inside the Linux kernel. Through the IO Visor tooling, eBPF enables packet tracing with low CPU overhead, avoiding the cost of copying every packet to user space. In a reported pipeline, a custom software implementation accumulates raw packets from the interfaces of edge µ-boxes, and Apache Spark with Scala processes them into five multivariate statistical features of per-flow databytes (mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation and total). The processed records are streamed via Apache Kafka and stored in a MongoDB NoSQL database, forming a time-series dataset; the example dataset covered one month sampled at 5-minute intervals (about 43,000 records). Such a visibility pipeline supplies the otherwise scarce, unlabeled raw time-series data needed to train traffic prediction models and is operated by the Visibility center of the testbed, which feeds the Intelligence center.

## Machine Learning for Wireless and 6G

### Supervised, Unsupervised and Reinforcement Learning Taxonomy for Wireless Networks
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends | gen: 502f68701bf4 -->
Machine Learning (ML), as a subset of Artificial Intelligence, is positioned as a critical tool for 6G because it makes data-driven decisions and estimates parameters for system features too complex for conventional mathematical models. Methods fall into three paradigms. Supervised learning trains on labeled input-output data and includes Artificial Neural Networks and Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) for resource allocation and cell association (with autoencoders usable for unsupervised pre-training), k-Nearest Neighbor (a distance-based multi-class classifier that is costly on large datasets), Naive Bayes (a probabilistic model assuming feature independence, efficient for high-dimensional data), decision trees and Random Forests (information-gain-maximizing trees, with forests reducing overfitting by aggregating trees), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for pattern and image recognition via convolution and pooling layers, and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and LSTMs for sequential and time-series data, where LSTMs address the vanishing gradient problem. Unsupervised learning finds hidden structure in unlabeled data: K-means clusters by distance to centroids and depends strongly on the choice of k; Self-Organizing Maps use competitive learning for dimensionality reduction and clustering; autoencoders learn efficient codings by reconstructing their input; Principal Component Analysis projects data onto orthogonal axes of maximum variance; Hidden Markov Models and Restricted Boltzmann Machines support sequence modeling and encoding of probability distributions. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has an agent learn an optimal policy by trial and error, maximizing a reward from its environment: value-based methods include Q-learning (off-policy, greedy) and SARSA (on-policy), policy-based methods include Policy Gradient, Proximal Policy Optimization and Actor-Critic (A2C), which learns a policy (actor) and a value function (critic), and Deep RL (DRL) combines deep learning with RL for high-dimensional state and action spaces. Applications are organized by task. Supervised models predict path loss (Random Forest, kNN), perform joint user association and power allocation (semi-supervised graph/spectral RL), allocate bandwidth dynamically (ANN), support cooperative spectrum sensing (ANN, SVM), predict data rates (Random Forest), design MISO beamformers (DNN), and balance load (RNN-based APRIL model). Beam selection in mm-wave uses kNN and SVC, with DNNs and GRUs reducing beam overhead; DNN estimators outperform conventional channel estimation; ANNs and DNNs approach optimal caching performance. For fault and anomaly management, extended SVMs (Support Tucker Machine) detect outliers in IoT sensor data, SOMs outperform Fuzzy C-means and K-means in fault prediction, and 'K-Aware K-means' self-tunes k to reach 99.7% anomaly detection accuracy. Unsupervised K-means supports user selection and power allocation in NOMA, while LSTMs and CNNs handle modulation recognition and cell event classification. Discrete-time Markov chains and HMMs predict user mobility by modeling the network as a state-transition graph. Visible Light Communications (VLC) use K-means and CAPD as pre-distorters to reduce non-linearity. Security uses decision trees with XAI, LSTM and CNN traffic classifiers, non-parametric Bayesian IoT authentication, GMM physical-layer security and CNN with stacked autoencoders for intrusion detection.

### Deep Reinforcement Learning for Wireless Resource Management
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends, optical_data_center_networking_a_comprehensive_review_on_traffic_switching_bandwidth_allocation_and_challenges, resource_management_from_single-domain_5g_to_end-to-end_6g_network_slicing_a_survey | gen: d22adfa68aff -->
Reinforcement learning is used extensively for wireless resource management because it learns policies directly from interaction without requiring an accurate system model, and Deep RL (DRL) extends it to high-dimensional state and action spaces. For spectrum allocation, examples include NAAC for D2D communication and GAN-DDQN for network slicing; for resource allocation, Q-learning minimizes outage probability, and DRL optimizes power allocation under delayed CSI and maximizes SNR in IRS-assisted links. In caching and computing at the edge, MDP-based DRL and Actor-Critic models maximize cache hit rates and reduce transmission delay in MEC networks, and Multi-Agent Multi-Armed Bandit (MAMAB) approaches learn caching strategies online. Channel allocation in LTE, 5G and dense WLANs has been addressed with RL based on auction theory and MDPs to enhance throughput. For energy, Q-learning and DRL reduce consumption in cooperative networks, and Hybrid Actor-Critic (Hybrid-AC) optimizes dynamic computation offloading by balancing time and energy costs. In mobility management, offline RL reduces excess handovers, while DRL using camera images enables proactive handover timing in mm-wave systems by predicting data rate drops before they occur. In V2V links, DRL maps observations to resource allocation that minimizes interference and satisfies latency constraints. In UAV scenarios, two-stage DRL handles offline content placement and online user tracking (via DDQN) under energy constraints. For security, DRL provides robustness against jamming, multi-agent RL (MARL) enables collaborative spectrum sharing for defense, and RL with prioritized experience replay boosts secrecy rates in IRS-aided environments. In hybrid RF/VLC networks, multi-agent DQN and Q-learning optimize power allocation with better convergence than plain Q-learning, and Deep Q-learning has been applied to fault detection and diagnosis with high accuracy using fewer features. In optical data center networks, DRL and other ML/AI methods are proposed for self-driven reconfigurability, forecasting traffic and automatically adapting topology, routing and bandwidth allocation without human intervention, while traffic prediction can also be used to deactivate idle devices and save power. In network slicing specifically, DRL variants are applied across the management functions: twin-actor DDPG balances efficiency and optimality in joint RAN+CN admission control and allocation; SARSA handles E2E admission control over the massive dimensionality of E2E resources; PPO jointly allocates radio, power and bandwidth across domains; and multi-agent DRL with safety DRL and imitation learning supports orchestration, with domain agents reporting to a central slice manager. DRL is also favored for slice reconfiguration because of its resilience in dynamic conditions, though it is often restricted to a single domain. Recognized open issues are long convergence time, which can hinder performance in highly dynamic wireless environments, and, for E2E slicing, the explosion of the state-action space, whose reduction without loss of accuracy remains a challenge.

### Intelligent Reflecting Surfaces (IRS) and Smart Radio Environments
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends, x-haul_solutions_for_5g-6g_networks_overview_of_requirements_and_technologies | gen: 672cd9f27866 -->
Intelligent Reflecting Surfaces (IRS), also called Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RIS), are central to the 'smart radio environment' concept for 6G: surfaces whose elements induce controllable phase changes on incident signals, allowing the propagation channel itself to be shaped to improve coverage and link quality, alongside operation in mm-wave and THz bands. Machine learning is applied to configure them: Deep RL maximizes SNR in IRS communications, and specialized RL models using prioritized experience replay boost secrecy rates in IRS-aided environments against eavesdroppers. A key open problem is Channel State Information (CSI) acquisition. IRS elements are passive, so they cannot sense or estimate channels directly, and the training overhead is high because of the large number of elements; the authors argue ML is needed to move beyond linear correlations when inferring channels in this setting. From the transport perspective, deploying RIS introduces a new backhaul/control segment between base stations and the RIS elements, which increases control-plane latency and adds to the x-haul requirements of 6G networks.

### ML for UAV-Assisted Wireless Networks
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends | gen: 20c5098b7d29 -->
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) act as aerial base stations, caching nodes and edge trainers, and ML is applied across all three learning paradigms. Supervised methods include DNNs that predict UAV network requirements, deep learning combining clustering with CNNs for joint caching and trajectory optimization, GRU-based prediction of serving base stations for drones from their trajectory, and ensemble methods that predict Received Signal Strength (RSS). ANNs and SVMs detect GPS spoofing, jamming and intrusion attacks. Unsupervised approaches use K-means to cluster users spatially before deploying a UAV as a base station, while MLP and LSTM models predict optimal UAV locations to maximize throughput. Reinforcement learning includes two-stage DRL that optimizes offline content placement and online user tracking (via DDQN) while respecting energy constraints. An open issue is 'UAVs as Intelligent Service' (UaaIS): because UAVs have limited battery life, using them as edge trainers requires energy-efficient training and inference. GANs are also proposed as a future direction for optimizing UAV trajectories and power settings with high convergence speed.

### ML-Based Channel Estimation, CSI Prediction and Beamforming for Massive MIMO
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends | gen: c53272f0b897 -->
ML is used to reduce the overhead and complexity of channel acquisition and beamforming in massive MIMO and mm-wave systems. For channel estimation, DNN estimators give more accurate predictions than conventional algorithms, and deep supervised learning supports adaptive bit allocation in heterogeneous networks to cut feedback overhead. For CSI prediction in massive MIMO, combinations of CNNs with Autoregressive Networks (ARN) and RNNs handle channel aging, and deep supervised mapping between the space and frequency domains reduces training and feedback overhead. In mm-wave beam selection, kNN and SVC solve multi-class selection, while DNNs and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) reduce beam overhead and predict serving base stations from trajectory. Supervised DNNs are also used for MISO beamforming, and unsupervised DNNs enable fast beamforming design in single-BS MIMO systems, maximizing sum-rate while increasing computational speed. In molecular communication, unsupervised models such as DetNet and LSTMs perform channel detection under inter-symbol interference. In IRS-assisted systems, CSI acquisition remains hard because of passive elements and high training overhead, and meta-learning (MAML) is proposed to reduce the sample size needed to train DNNs on wireless channels.

### Meta-Learning (MAML) and GANs as Future Directions for 6G ML
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_beyond_5g-6g_networksstate-of-the-art_and_future_trends | gen: 068c540c3496 -->
Two techniques are identified as future trends for ML in 6G. Meta-learning aims at 'learning to learn': Model Agnostic Meta Learning (MAML) finds a sensitive parameter initialization that allows fast adaptation to new tasks from very few samples, which addresses the long convergence times and data demands of conventional models. Potential applications include reducing the sample size needed for DNN training in wireless channels and improving IoT demodulation via online meta-learning. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) rely on a minimax game between a Generator and a Discriminator to produce realistic data samples. In 6G, GANs can pre-train DRL frameworks for URLLC resource allocation, and can optimize UAV trajectories and power settings with high convergence speed. GANs are already used in combination with DRL, as in GAN-DDQN for network slicing spectrum allocation.

### Metaheuristic Optimization of ML Classifiers (Bio-Inspired Algorithms)
<!-- sources: elevating_optical_networks_machine_learning_approach_for_optimal_resource_scheduling_and_performance_boost | gen: 9f12b1f08b8b -->
Metaheuristic, bio-inspired optimization algorithms are used to tune the hyperparameters of machine-learning models when exhaustive search is impractical. Dynamic Black Widow Optimization (DBWO) is an evolutionary algorithm modeled on the hunting and mating behavior of black widow spiders; applied to a Random Forest classifier, it searches for parameter settings that maintain high classification accuracy while preventing overfitting in dynamic environments, yielding the hybrid DBWO-RF model (95.3% accuracy on optical channel quality classification). Related bio-inspired heuristics appear throughout the networking literature, including the African Vulture Optimization Algorithm (AVOA) and the Aquila Optimizer combined with the Whale Optimization Algorithm (AWOA), alongside other optimization approaches such as game models for LTE-LAA/WiFi coexistence, binary computation offloading in H-MEC networks, reinforcement learning for 5G RAN slicing and deep Q-learning for Space-Time Networks.
