# Key Concepts

Reference notes synthesizing the core concepts needed to read this collection of papers on coherent, flexible, and rate-adaptive passive optical networks (PON), their integration with multi-access edge computing (MEC), and their role in 5G/6G x-haul transport.

## PON Fundamentals and Evolution

### Passive Optical Network (PON) Evolution
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios, advanced_technologies_for_next-generation_passive_optical_networks, flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: 76c7675c7abc -->
PON standardization has progressed from ATM-PON (622 Mb/s) and GPON (ITU-T G.984) and EPON, through 10G systems (10GE-PON, XG-PON/G.987) to NG-PON2 (G.989), whose TWDM-PON design adds wavelength stacking to reach tens to hundreds of Gb/s aggregate. Current ITU-T work is moving toward 50 Gb/s per wavelength (50G-PON), which introduces digital signal processing (DSP) into the transceivers while retaining intensity modulation/direct detection (IM/DD). Beyond this, the Very High Speed PON (VHSP) project, referred to as Beyond-50G-PON, targets 100 Gb/s and potentially 200 Gb/s per wavelength, with expected requirements of 100G to 200G per wavelength by 2030, driven by 8K/16K video, VR/AR, edge computing and 5G/6G mobile X-haul and by the F6G goal of a tenfold improvement in capacity, connectivity and latency. This growth in capacity also underpins the use of PON as a cost-effective x-haul for 5G-and-beyond networks, including integration with multi-access edge computing for latency-sensitive services. In this regime IM/DD systems struggle to maintain a viable loss budget, since O-band fiber dispersion and receiver sensitivity limits make a high budget such as PR-30 hard to sustain at 50G and above, even with PAM-4, nonlinear equalization, booster amplifiers and SOA+PIN or APD receivers, and TDMA adds latency. Coherent transceivers are therefore proposed as the path forward, owing to their superior sensitivity and DSP-based compensation of fiber impairments, and because they also enable supplementary functions such as optical sensing and enhanced security. Adoption is envisaged as scenario-driven: starting with high-bandwidth users such as business campuses and mobile X-haul, and extending to broader residential deployment as the technology matures and integration lowers cost. Each generational step responds to rising demand for capacity, connectivity and low latency from FTTH/FTTB services and 5G mobile fronthaul/backhaul. As networks become larger and more complex, with higher split ratios, longer reach, and multiple wavelengths and impairments interacting, physics-based models struggle to capture nonlinear and stochastic behavior, and repeated numerical simulation of design variants becomes costly. This pressure motivates data-driven performance assessment of access networks, including machine-learning approaches already applied to PON fault management and quality-of-transmission estimation, although many such models generalize poorly when network configurations change.

### TWDM-PON (NG-PON2)
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios | gen: 1c153726aed3 -->
TWDM-PON, the architecture specified for NG-PON2 in ITU-T G.989, stacks multiple TDM-PON systems on different wavelengths over a shared passive ODN, combining time and wavelength division multiplexing so that capacity scales by adding wavelength channels rather than by raising a single line rate. One studied configuration follows ITU-T G.989.1 and is asymmetric, with 160 Gbps downstream and 80 Gbps upstream, serving 512 users (splitting ratio 512) over a 65 km reach, with evaluation distances ranging from back-to-back (0 km) up to about 70-75 km. Physics-based simulation of such a system typically uses components such as erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFA) and fiber Bragg gratings (FBG), and signal quality degrades with distance through attenuation, noise accumulation and chromatic dispersion, so Q-factor falls and BER rises as reach increases. The high split ratio and aggregate rate make this a demanding scenario for performance assessment, and it serves as the target system for data-driven prediction of upstream and downstream link quality.

### PON Power Budget, Split Ratio and Reach Trade-off
<!-- sources: flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: 672814b59e19 -->
The power budget of a PON is the sum of the transmission loss and the splitting loss, Power Budget = Loss_Transmission + Loss_Split, so for a given budget a larger split ratio shortens the attainable reach and vice versa. The sensitivity gain of coherent detection, more than 10 dB over IM/DD, lets operators redefine this trade-off. With a 10 dB increase in power budget, a coherent PON can support high-density scenarios of up to 256 ONUs at 20 km, long-reach scenarios of up to 70 km for small groups such as 8 ONUs, or hybrid scenarios that serve users at different distances at once, for example 32 ONUs at 30 km together with 16 ONUs at 50 km. These options map onto geographic areas: urban (short reach, high density), suburban (mixed) and rural (long reach, low density). The differing OPL of each node can be accommodated by adapting modulation formats or coding rates per ONU, linking this trade-off to rate-adaptive operation. Maintaining a high budget such as PR-30 is difficult for IM/DD at 50G because of receiver sensitivity limits and O-band fiber dispersion, which is one motivation for the move to coherent technology. IMDD can nevertheless reach this budget at 100 Gb/s/λ by other means: a 33 GBd PAM8 system with 20G-class components achieved a 30 dB loss budget meeting the IEEE 802.3av PR30 requirement, by combining a high launch power of 18 dBm (and a receiver sensitivity of -12 dBm at the 7% FEC limit) with neural-network equalization of the resulting nonlinear distortion.

### Multiple-Access Schemes Beyond TDM for PON
<!-- sources: flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: daca1f3bb20b -->
TDM/TDMA is efficient for bandwidth sharing in PON through statistical multiplexing: downstream traffic is broadcast by TDM and upstream traffic is arbitrated by TDMA in the point-to-multipoint topology. However, it suffers from scheduling latency, including waiting of about 1.5 polling cycles under conservative REPORT-GRANT allocation and delays from discovery windows used to register new ONUs, and it requires expensive burst-mode devices. Latency-oriented optimizations such as bonus-bandwidth grants and learning-based allocation work within TDMA, whereas other approaches change the multiple-access scheme itself. FDMA and CDMA are common in wireless networks but were avoided in direct-detection PONs because unstable laser wavelengths produced beat signals that degraded performance; coherent detection removes this beat-signal problem and enables alternative or hybrid multiple-access schemes that balance connectivity and latency. Wavelength-division multiple access (WDMA), including ultra-dense WDM (UDWDM), assigns dedicated wavelengths to high-end users for low-latency, secure point-to-point services. Frequency-division multiple access (FDMA) uses digital subcarrier multiplexing to allocate sub-bands of a single wavelength, and has the added benefit that each ONU can use lower-bandwidth devices, reducing cost. Non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) works in the power domain through constellation multiplexing to raise network capacity. Code-division multiple access (CDMA) multiplexes users with unique spreading sequences so that they can transmit simultaneously in the same time and frequency slot. Combinations such as WDM-FDM or time-and-frequency-division multiplexing (TFDM) create a multidimensional access system with high flexibility. A joint FDMA/TDMA architecture based on entropy-loading digital subcarrier multiplexing assigns ONUs specific subcarriers and time slots, with flexible-rate technology setting each one's spectral efficiency from its link loss to maximize total capacity. A demonstrated 100G single-wavelength TFDM coherent PON uses four 25 Gb/s PDM-QPSK subcarriers: the OLT sends the subcarriers downstream with an I/Q modulator, each ONU detects them separately, and upstream a single broadband coherent receiver at the OLT aggregates the subcarriers from multiple ONUs. Bandwidth is allocated in two dimensions, so low-latency services can receive dedicated point-to-point frequency channels while other users share bandwidth by TDM. Because laser wavelength drift can cause subcarriers to overlap, guard bands are required, which slightly reduces total spectral efficiency; in aggregation scenarios with many low-bandwidth subcarriers, laser phase noise further limits capacity.

## Transmission Quality Metrics

### Quality of Transmission (QoT) Estimation
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios | gen: 4ae03f2f79a6 -->
QoT estimation predicts whether an optical connection will meet performance requirements, using metrics such as Q-factor, BER and receiver sensitivity. Conventional estimation relies on analytical or physics-based simulation of attenuation, noise and dispersion, which is accurate but computationally expensive during parameter sweeps. Machine-learning approaches instead learn the mapping from link parameters (transmission distance, launch power level, noise factor) to KPIs. In a PON setting, regressors have been trained to predict Q-factor, BER and receiver sensitivity separately for upstream and downstream channels, giving six targets in total. Upstream channels showed slightly higher prediction error than downstream ones, attributed to their greater vulnerability to noise and system fluctuations. Compared with physics-based results, the predictions reproduce the same trends (quality drops from back-to-back to about 70 km) and, at long distances, tend toward more conservative receiver sensitivity estimates, which provides a design safety margin.

### Q-factor and Bit Error Rate (BER)
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios | gen: 6f2c8f7dae7e -->
The Q-factor summarizes signal quality at the receiver decision point, and under Gaussian noise statistics it maps to the bit error rate through BER = 1/2 erfc(Q/√2), where erfc is the complementary error function. Higher Q therefore means exponentially lower BER, and both degrade with fiber distance as attenuation, noise buildup and dispersion accumulate. Because BER spans many orders of magnitude, regression models are better conditioned when it is converted to a base-10 logarithmic scale before training; in one pipeline, log10(BER) targets were additionally scaled with a MinMaxScaler, whereas linear metrics such as Q-factor and sensitivity used a StandardScaler. Q-factor and BER are standard KPIs for judging whether a PON link meets its error-rate requirement.

### Receiver Sensitivity
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios, machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: 26749796103a -->
Receiver sensitivity is the minimum received optical power at which a receiver achieves a target BER, and it is a key KPI in PON power-budget design. In data-driven studies it has been modeled as degrading roughly linearly with transmission distance, with added stochastic perturbation to mimic real variability. Among the predicted KPIs, receiver sensitivity proved the most predictable for machine-learning regressors, reaching R² above 0.99, compared with R² above 0.94 for all targets overall. At longer distances, predicted sensitivity values were more negative, i.e. more conservative, than physics-based simulation results, which the authors of that work argue gives system designers a safety margin. In experimental IMDD PON work, sensitivity is quoted at a FEC threshold: a 33 GBd PAM8 100 Gb/s/λ system with NN equalization reached -12 dBm at the 7% FEC limit (BER of 3.8×10^-3). Because PAM-8 has small symbol spacing, thermal and shot noise strongly affect sensitivity, so to separate the equalizer's contribution from these additive noises the power injected into the photodetector was fixed at 3 dBm.

## Coherent PON Technologies

### Coherent PON Transceivers
<!-- sources: advanced_technologies_for_next-generation_passive_optical_networks, flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects, machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: ea41733e6e90 -->
Coherent detection is presented as the rational path for 100G-200G+ PON because of its superior receiver sensitivity and spectral efficiency, and its ability to compensate fiber impairments in DSP. Mixing the received signal with a local oscillator (LO) provides beating gain that yields more than 10 dB of sensitivity improvement over IM/DD, which allows higher power budgets (direct-detection systems already struggle with budgets of 32 dB or more, and with power budgets at 100G and beyond), long reach and high split ratios, and the use of C-band wavelengths avoids O-band congestion and eases coexistence with legacy systems. Spectral efficiency is raised through polarization multiplexing and I/Q modulation. Coherent detection also converts the optical field linearly to the digital domain, enabling advanced modulation formats, digital dispersion compensation and inherent frequency selectivity, so frequency-division multiplexing can be performed without optical filters. The alternative, IM/DD, has been demonstrated at 100 Gb/s using PAM-4, nonlinear equalizers to overcome bandwidth limits, booster amplifiers and SOA+PIN or APD receivers; 200 Gb/s IM/DD demonstrations exist in research but depend on expensive components and computationally heavy DSP, making them impractical for commercial PON. Low-cost IM/DD remains competitive at 100 Gb/s/λ: a 33 GBd PAM8 link built from commercial 20G-class components and a neural-network equalizer reached a 30 dB loss budget (PR30) with 18 dBm launch power, whereas coherent detection, despite better sensitivity, is costly and power-intensive. Although 800 Gb/s and 1.6 Tb/s coherent technologies exist for point-to-point networks, their use in PON is hindered by cost and power consumption at the ONU, so commercial viability depends on low-cost transceivers and DSP architectures with fast convergence and low complexity. The cost of components and DSP at the ONU is the primary barrier to commercialization, and reductions in modulation dimensions or receiver sensitivity are regarded as acceptable trade-offs for significantly lower cost. Because standard dual-polarization intradyne receivers are too costly for access, intermediate designs trade performance against cost. Asymmetric intensity-modulation/coherent-detection schemes either use intensity modulation at the OLT to simplify the coherent receiver at the ONU, or, more cost-effectively, place a simple intensity modulator at the budget-constrained ONU and a shared coherent receiver at the OLT. In the upstream, Mach-Zehnder modulators (MZMs) replace expensive IQ modulators in simplified transmitters: intensity modulation uses coherent detection of an intensity signal that is squared at the receiver, giving the simplest DSP and high frequency-offset tolerance but precluding complex-domain processing, while amplitude modulation either ignores phase information or uses only 0° and 180° phases (PAM-4 ASK), which permits complex-domain DSP but requires carrier recovery. Simplified coherent receivers reduce optoelectronic component counts in several ways: heterodyne detection replaces the 90° optical hybrids with simpler 3-dB couplers at the price of higher receiver bandwidth (e.g., dual-polarization heterodyne receivers built from 2x2 couplers and balanced photodiodes), and single-polarization reception removes polarization diversity to halve the components at the price of spectral efficiency. A minimal coherent receiver further replaces the balanced photodiode with a single-ended photodiode (SPD), which incurs a 3 dB power loss and degrades sensitivity but lowers hardware cost, and a 3x3 coupler design allows polarization-independent intensity detection with only three SPDs. To keep these simplified receivers insensitive to the state of polarization (SOP), the OLT applies transmitter-side diversity, with Alamouti coding identified as the most effective method for minimizing performance variation with SOP; it lets the ONU use a 2x2 coupler and a single balanced photodiode. The main barriers to adoption are cost and complexity of full-field coherent transceivers; proposed mitigations include low-cost DFB lasers in place of external-cavity lasers (ECLs) and remote laser sources, while photonic integrated circuit (PIC) technology is regarded as essential for reducing size, power consumption and cost.

### Coherent P2MP Optical Aggregation
<!-- sources: flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions | gen: e71d1e1d8f1e -->
Coherent point-to-multipoint (P2MP) optical aggregation is proposed as an alternative to point-to-point (P2P) electrical aggregation in fixed networks. In electrical aggregation, routers converge multiple low-speed flows into one high-speed flow; in the coherent P2MP approach, a passive optical splitter converges these flows optically, so a single high-speed coherent transceiver at the aggregation node can serve several lower-rate transceivers using digital subcarriers. The main advantages are cost and complexity, performance and scalability: the total number of transceivers is reduced by about 50%, lowering CAPEX and OPEX; eliminating router processing removes its latency contribution; and capacity can be upgraded flexibly by adding digital subcarriers rather than replacing all hardware at once. The approach also faces challenges. Passive splitters lack the programmability, authentication features and reliability monitoring that routers provide. Direct optical transmission suffers higher link loss than regenerated electrical signals, which may call for optical amplifiers. Finally, current FDMA-based aggregation is limited in capacity by laser phase noise when many low-bandwidth subcarriers are used. The scheme builds on the same coherent multiple-access and flexible-rate techniques developed for PON, with subcarriers assigned per tributary and rates matched to link quality.

### Digital Subcarrier Multiplexing (DSCM) for PON
<!-- sources: flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: 69d3eb391098 -->
Digital subcarrier multiplexing (DSCM) divides a single optical carrier into several digitally generated subcarriers, and in coherent PON it provides the frequency-domain dimension of a joint FDMA/TDMA architecture, typically in an entropy-loading form in which each subcarrier is assigned its own spectral efficiency. Downstream, the central office (CO) broadcasts the DSCM signal and each ONU shifts its local oscillator to its assigned subcarrier frequency, so it can ignore the other traffic and use lower-bandwidth, lower-cost transceivers. Upstream, ONUs are allocated specific subcarriers and time slots; each generates an optical subcarrier matching its subcarrier width, and the signals are aggregated at the CO through a passive splitter and received by a coherent receiver. Flexible-rate technology then assigns spectral efficiencies to subcarriers according to each ONU's link loss, maximizing total network capacity instead of dimensioning for the worst-case ONU. In the time-frequency division multiplexing (TFDM) architecture, multiple digital subcarriers are used (e.g., five, of which one is left blank to support heterodyne detection), and rates are adjusted by varying the QAM order per subcarrier in the frequency dimension, or by using PS and different QAM orders in different time slots in the time dimension. This is often paired with Alamouti-coded receivers to maintain low ONU cost. Combining frequency and time allocation balances connectivity and latency: dedicated subcarriers can serve latency-sensitive users while others share by TDMA. Practical limits include guard bands needed to absorb laser wavelength drift and laser phase noise, which constrains capacity when many narrow subcarriers are used.

### Burst-Mode DSP for Coherent TDMA PON
<!-- sources: flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: f7b7fba807cf -->
In coherent TDMA PON, upstream bursts from different ONUs are short and arrive with different powers, clocks, frequency offsets and polarization states, so the OLT must handle rapid transitions between users. Standard continuous-mode DSP relies on blind algorithms with feedback loops whose convergence is too slow for such bursts, reducing payload efficiency. Burst-mode DSP replaces them with feed-forward estimation driven by frame structures containing synchronization patterns (SPs) for fast convergence and pilot structures for rapid channel estimation, here realized as a specialized two-part preamble. Preamble A uses frequency tones, at half and quarter of the baud rate, on the X and Y polarizations, supporting frame detection, frequency offset estimation (FOE), state-of-polarization (SOP) estimation and initialization of the sampling phase offset (SPO). Preamble B employs constant-amplitude zero-autocorrelation (CAZAC) sequences with specific multiplication coefficients to enable frame synchronization and estimation of the equalizer tap coefficients. Timing recovery uses feed-forward SPO estimation from Preamble A, which removes the long convergence time of conventional feedback timing loops. The MIMO equalizer taps are estimated from Preamble B with either the minimum mean square error (MMSE) or the zero-forcing (ZF) algorithm: MMSE gives better performance, while ZF has lower computational complexity. Because the preamble provides a proper initialization, the subsequent feedback loop that tracks the signal has effectively no convergence time, so the payload can begin immediately after the preamble. Neural networks have also been noted as a means of enhancing receiver sensitivity in burst-mode operation.

### Local Oscillator Power Adjustment for Coherent PON Dynamic Range
<!-- sources: low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: 7203128f9350 -->
In coherent PON, a wide span of received optical powers must be handled: high-power signals risk saturating the analog-to-digital converter (ADC), while low-power signals need the LO beating gain to maintain sensitivity. Dynamically adjusting the LO power addresses both. It is implemented with a variable optical attenuator (VOA) on the LO path, and the adjustment can be driven either by monitoring the electrical signal at the ADC input or by referencing the power of the optical input signal. The LO power is decreased as the received optical power increases, which keeps the signal within the ADC range and the bit error rate (BER) below the FEC threshold. The effect is largest for high-order modulation: experimental data show that without adjustment the dynamic range for formats such as 64QAM is very narrow, around 5 dB, and LO adjustment significantly expands it. Coverage thus extends across both high and low optical path loss (OPL) scenarios where IM/DD is limited. In the downstream, which operates in continuous mode, DSP compensates chromatic dispersion, clock synchronization and the state of polarization, and dynamic ranges as high as 39 dB at 200G have been reported. In the upstream burst mode, the OLT must additionally cope with consecutive bursts of different power, clock, SOP and frequency offset, handled by synchronization patterns and pilot structures. A demonstrated 300G peak-rate TDM coherent PON based on PS-QAM likewise reaches a dynamic range above 35 dB by adjusting the LO power.

### Alamouti Coding for Simplified Coherent ONU Receivers
<!-- sources: low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: 4c7b9d7c751d -->
Alamouti coding is a space-time block code that allows an ONU to avoid a dual-polarization coherent receiver. At the OLT, a single-polarization signal is transformed into a space-time block code spread across the two polarizations of the fiber, so that the data are carried redundantly across two symbol periods and two polarization tributaries. Because the code is orthogonal, the ONU can recover the data with a simplified receiver consisting of a 2x2 coupler and one balanced photodiode (BPD), without polarization diversity and without needing to track the state of polarization (SOP) with a full dual-polarization front end. This reduces the optoelectronic component count at the cost-sensitive ONU and makes performance largely insensitive to SOP, which is why Alamouti coding is identified as the most effective transmitter-side diversity method for minimizing performance variation with SOP in simplified receivers. It is compatible with heterodyne detection and with the other receiver simplifications (e.g., single-ended photodiodes), and it is commonly paired with TFDM-based flexible-rate coherent PON, where multiple digital subcarriers are served by low-cost ONU receivers. The trade-off is that the redundancy inherent in the code halves the spectral efficiency relative to a full dual-polarization transmission, a price accepted to lower ONU cost.

## Flexible and Rate-Adaptive PON

### Flexible PON
<!-- sources: advanced_technologies_for_next-generation_passive_optical_networks, flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions | gen: cd6a1adc85b2 -->
Traditional PONs are dimensioned for the worst case, typically the furthest and most lossy ONU, and therefore offer little flexibility; a fixed rate set by the worst-performing ONU limits overall throughput and wastes capacity available on better links, since link losses differ between ONUs because of fiber length and splitter counts. The 50G PON standard introduces some adaptability through transmitter and dispersion eye closure (TDEC) measurements, which allow a trade-off between launch power and transmission quality, and through flexible forward error correction (FEC). Flexible coherent PON is framed as an enabler of sixth-generation fixed (F6G) networks, which aim at roughly tenfold improvements in capacity, connectivity and latency over F5G, a target that direct-detection PONs struggle to reach because of limits in receiver sensitivity and spectral efficiency. It combines three ingredients: coherent optics, flexible multiple access and flexible-rate technology. Flexibility is extended along three axes: access data rate, multiple-access architecture and deployment scenario, which together are argued to be critical for serving use cases ranging from residential FTTH to mobile cell sites. At the layer level, modulation formats can be varied at the physical media dependent (PMD) layer and FEC code rates at the transmission convergence (TC) layer, and rate-adaptive schemes assign each ONU a rate matched to its optical path loss; to limit complexity, ONUs can be grouped by similar link loss so that rates are adjusted discretely rather than continuously. At the architecture level, time-and-frequency-division multiplexing (TFDM) based on digital subcarrier multiplexing lets resources be allocated in both domains, and other coherent-enabled access schemes can be combined with TDM. At the optical distribution network (ODN) level, adjustable variable splitters (AVSs) can dynamically redistribute optical power among ONUs, and the extra power budget of coherent detection can be traded between split ratio and reach to cover urban, suburban and rural areas. Practical realization requires balancing network metrics and developing burst-mode DSP for multiple-access scenarios, and the main constraint on these features is the cost-to-benefit ratio: the added complexity must be offset by operator revenue recovery or low implementation cost.

### Rate-Adaptive Coherent PON
<!-- sources: flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: 5e4c52a510d2 -->
Rate-adaptive coherent PON, also termed flexible CPON (FLCS-PON), assigns each ONU a data rate matched to its channel condition, mainly its optical path loss (OPL), instead of providing a uniform rate to all users according to the worst-performing ONU. To keep complexity manageable, ONUs can be grouped by similar link loss, so that rates are switched discretely between groups rather than tuned continuously per ONU. Coherent transmission offers more degrees of freedom than IM/DD, which is limited largely to multi-rate NRZ or adaptive coding. The schemes surveyed differ in granularity: adaptive baud rate, which adjusts clock and sampling rates, and adaptive modulation order, which switches between formats from BPSK to 16QAM according to the link budget, both give large steps; multidimensional modulation exploits I/Q-imbalanced modulation; adaptive modulation and coding (MCS) combines FEC code rates with modulation formats to narrow the gap between discrete modulation levels (medium granularity); and adaptive probabilistic shaping (PS) adjusts source entropy to approach channel capacity, giving fine-grained rate adjustment. Two main architectures realize flexibility in coherent PON. PS-based FLCS-CPON tunes the constellation entropy of each user through the shaping factor ν, using a hybrid of classical and reversed PS to maximize net data rate under peak-power constraints. TFDM-based FLCS-CPON allocates resources in both time and frequency, using multiple digital subcarriers and varying the QAM order per subcarrier or the PS and QAM order across time slots, typically paired with Alamouti-coded receivers to keep ONU cost low. Flexible FEC complements these: applying shortening and puncturing to a single code word continuously adjusts the payload and overhead of the code, trading optical power budget for net data rate without changing the code itself. A demonstrated 300G peak-rate TDM coherent PON based on PS-QAM achieves a dynamic range above 35 dB by adjusting the local-oscillator power. At the transmitter, raw data is split, one branch undergoes distribution matching for amplitude shaping, and FEC encoding and QAM mapping follow; channel estimation sets the source entropy, and look-up-table (LUT) pre-equalization compensates distortion. At the receiver, burst-mode DSP synchronizes and demodulates the bursts arriving from different ONUs. Over a 20 km fiber link, net rates fell from 255 Gb/s to 85 Gb/s as the OPL increased from 3 dB to 38 dB.

### Probabilistic Constellation Shaping (PS-QAM)
<!-- sources: flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: 64d83c833c84 -->
Probabilistic constellation shaping (PS) changes the occurrence probabilities of QAM constellation points rather than their positions, so that low-energy points are used more often than high-energy ones, approximating a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. In the PON implementation, the transmitter splits the raw data stream and passes one branch through a distribution matcher that generates the Maxwell-Boltzmann amplitude distribution; the shaped amplitude bits are combined with the other branch, FEC-encoded and mapped to QAM symbols. The source entropy of the PS-QAM signal sets the net bit rate and can be tuned continuously, with the value chosen from channel estimation of each ONU's link, which gives fine-grained rate adjustment that approaches the optimal channel capacity at a given signal-to-noise ratio. This contrasts with adaptive baud rate or modulation order, which change rate in large steps, and with MCS, which has medium granularity. The choice of shaping format depends on the system: in peak-power-constrained (PPC) PONs without optical amplifiers, inverse PCS-PAM is preferred for single-carrier signals, whereas conventional PCS-QAM is used for multi-carrier entropy-loading systems. Hybrid PS (HPS) combines Classical PS (CPS), which copes with clipping nonlinearity in burst mode, and Reversed PS (RPS), which is more efficient under peak-power constraints. By adjusting the shaping factor ν, the constellation entropy H is finely tuned to each user's channel conditions, maximizing the net data rate (NDR) and overall throughput. PS-QAM underpins a demonstrated 300G peak-rate coherent TDM PON in which net rates ranged from 255 Gb/s to 85 Gb/s as the optical path loss increased from 3 dB to 38 dB over 20 km of fiber, with LUT-based pre-equalization of distortion and burst-mode DSP at the receiver.

## IMDD PON and Equalization

### IMDD 100G/λ PON with Low-Cost 20G-Class Components
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: c77fb8dde350 -->
Coherent technology offers superior sensitivity for 100 Gb/s/λ PON but is costly and power-intensive, so IMDD remains attractive for cost-sensitive access, driven by demand from 5G and IoT. Its challenges are a demanding loss budget and inter-symbol interference caused by limited device bandwidth. A demonstration of 100 Gb/s/λ IMDD PON uses commercially available 20G-class components (a 20 GHz Mach-Zehnder modulator, with an EDFA to set launch power) to carry a 33 GBd PAM8 signal, with an NN-based equalizer compensating the bandwidth-induced linear distortion and the nonlinear distortion. By raising the launch power to 18 dBm and relying on the NN to handle the resulting nonlinearity, the system reaches a receiver sensitivity of -12 dBm at the 7% FEC limit (BER of 3.8×10^-3). This gives a total loss budget of 30 dB over 20 km of SSMF, meeting the IEEE 802.3av PR30 requirement without coherent detection.

### Digital Signal Processing in Optical Access Transceivers
<!-- sources: advanced_technologies_for_next-generation_passive_optical_networks, flexible_and_adaptive_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_optical_access_network, flexible_coherent_pon_for_6g_fixed_networks_technologies_and_solutions, low-cost_large-coverage_and_high-flexibility_coherent_pon_for_next-generation_access_networks_advances_challenges_and_prospects | gen: 1aa1e340ba8c -->
DSP is regarded as the fundamental enabler of next-generation PONs. Already at 50 Gb/s per wavelength, standards introduce DSP (equalization and FEC) into IM/DD transceivers, and at 100-200 Gb/s coherent transceivers rely on DSP to compensate fiber impairments such as dispersion and to recover the signal with high sensitivity. In coherent PON the linear conversion of the optical field to the digital domain allows digital dispersion compensation. In the downstream, continuous-mode DSP compensates chromatic dispersion, clock offsets and the state of polarization, supporting dynamic ranges as high as 39 dB at 200G. Convergence speed and complexity are central design criteria: continuous-mode DSP based on blind algorithms with feedback loops converges too slowly for short upstream bursts and reduces payload efficiency, so the upstream receiver instead uses burst-mode DSP, with feed-forward estimation and a dedicated preamble, to synchronize and demodulate bursts arriving from different ONUs with different power levels and frequency offsets. ONU-side DSP power consumption is reduced by optimizing adaptive equalization (AEQ): splitting the conventional AEQ into butterfly and non-butterfly FIR filters can halve the number of taps and cut multiplications by about 41%, and frequency-domain (FD) training-aided equalization provides robustness against optical propagation effects independently of filter length. Look-up-table (LUT) pre-equalization at the transmitter compensates distortion, and channel estimation informs adaptive functions such as the source entropy in probabilistic shaping. The same DSP foundation supports flexibility (adaptive modulation formats, variable FEC code rates, digital subcarrier multiplexing), integrated sensing (digital longitudinal monitoring and polarization tracking) and physical layer security (e.g., OFDM-based chaotic filters), as well as polarization-insensitive operation of simplified receivers through Alamouti coding. The drawback is complexity: nonlinear equalizers and heavy DSP raise cost and power, and nonlinear equalization in particular is currently deemed impractical for PON given its cost-benefit ratio, so in access networks each function must be justified against its cost-to-benefit ratio, and ONU-side DSP in particular must be low-complexity. Coherent DSP is nonetheless argued to become the more attractive option at about 100G and beyond, since the electronic dispersion compensation required by IM/DD at those rates eventually exceeds the complexity of coherent DSP.

### Linear and Volterra Nonlinear Equalizers (FFE and VNE)
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: 574978f72ec4 -->
The feedforward equalizer (FFE) is a linear equalizer that computes each symbol as a linear combination of its neighborhood of sampled symbols. Being a linear mapping, it can compensate bandwidth-limited inter-symbol interference but cannot mitigate nonlinearities arising from modulation or fiber transmission. The Volterra nonlinear equalizer (VNE) approximates nonlinear systems by adding higher-order features of the sampled signal, so it handles nonlinearity better than FFE, but it remains essentially a linear combination of pre-processed higher-order features. Both have lower computational cost than a neural network in multiplications per symbol. In a 33 GBaud PAM-8 IMDD PON, FFE, VNE and a neural-network equalizer perform similarly at low launch power and in optical back-to-back tests, where linear distortion dominates. As launch power increases and nonlinearity becomes severe, both FFE and VNE are significantly outperformed by the NN, in simulation over 25 km and experiment over 20 km of SSMF, which makes them useful baselines but limits their use in high-launch-power operation.

### Neural Network Equalization for IMDD PON
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: b3d6f2cd58a7 -->
Neural network (NN) equalizers address the linear and nonlinear distortions that limit 100 Gb/s/λ intensity-modulation direct-detection (IMDD) PON. One demonstrated design is a symbol-spaced, fully connected network with an input layer of 51 nodes (a window of neighboring received samples), two hidden layers of 128 nodes each and an output layer of 8 nodes, so that equalization is cast as classification of PAM-8 symbols. Hidden layers use ReLU activations and the output uses Softmax, with a cross-entropy loss optimized by back-propagation with the Adam optimizer. A dropout rate of 0.2 and batch normalization are applied to limit overfitting and improve convergence. In simulation of a 33 GBaud PAM-8 system with a 16.2 GHz bandwidth limit over 25 km of standard single-mode fiber (SSMF), and in experiments over 20 km, the NN performs similarly to feedforward (FFE) and Volterra nonlinear (VNE) equalizers at low launch power or in optical back-to-back operation, where linear distortion dominates. As launch power rises and nonlinearity becomes severe, it significantly outperforms both. This ability to compensate nonlinear distortion is what allows operation at high launch power and hence a larger loss budget. The cost is complexity: the NN needs more multiplications per symbol than FFE or VNE, which can be mitigated by parallel processing or by reducing the hidden-layer size.

### Training Data Protocol for ML Equalizers (PRBS vs Random Data)
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: f6e3df46ba4d -->
Many machine-learning equalization studies train and test on pseudo-random bit sequences (PRBS), which can lead to misleading results. Because a PRBS is generated by a deterministic rule, a neural network trained on it may learn that rule instead of the channel characteristics. When training and test data follow the same rule, this yields an extremely low and overestimated BER, and when the test data follows a different PRBS rule the BER is underestimated in performance terms, i.e. the result is also unrepresentative. Simulations of a 33 GBaud PAM-8 system with various training/test data combinations established the corrective rule: the NN must be trained on random data so that it acts as a true equalizer. Once trained on random data, it can be tested with either random data or PRBS without bias. This protocol is needed for fair comparison against FFE and Volterra equalizers, which, being low-capacity linear or polynomial mappings, are not prone to memorizing the sequence.

### Launch Power, Fiber Nonlinearity and SBS in High-Power IMDD PON
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: 0203ea8ca42d -->
Increasing the launch power is a direct way to extend the loss budget of an IMDD PON, but it brings strong nonlinear distortion that limits BER when only linear or Volterra equalization is used. A neural-network equalizer can compensate this nonlinearity, so a launch power of 18 dBm, set with an EDFA, becomes usable and yields a 30 dB loss budget for 33 GBd PAM8 over 20 km of SSMF. At such power levels stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) also becomes a concern. It is suppressed by broadening the optical spectrum, by modulating the laser with a low-voltage 100 Mb/s PRBS signal. Because PAM-8 has small symbol spacing, thermal and shot noise at the receiver also cause significant errors, so experiments fix the power injected into the photodetector at 3 dBm to isolate the equalizer's effect from additive noise.

## Machine Learning for Optical Performance Prediction

### Machine Learning for Optical Network Performance Prediction
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios, machine_learning_for_100_gb-s-italic-italic_passive_optical_network | gen: 8b4af030f275 -->
Machine learning can act as a surrogate for iterative optical simulation: a regressor is trained on input-output data from a system and then predicts KPIs directly from configuration parameters. The computational cost shifts to the training phase, and once trained, inference is near real-time, which enables rapid what-if analysis and parameter sweeps that would be slow with numerical simulators. Applied to TWDM-PON, such models learn the effects of attenuation, noise and chromatic dispersion implicitly, without explicit physical equations at prediction time, and have reached R² above 0.94 for Q-factor, BER and receiver sensitivity. Reported benefits include reduced reliance on expensive hardware setups and shorter design cycles, with relevance to FTTH/FTTB and 5G fronthaul/backhaul networks and to intelligent, real-time network optimization. Overfitting is mitigated through train/test splitting (80/20), k-fold cross-validation, L2 regularization for neural networks and depth constraints for tree ensembles, though generalization to configurations outside the training distribution remains a known concern. Machine learning is also applied at the physical-layer signal-processing level, beyond KPI prediction: a neural network trained on received samples acts as a nonlinear equalizer for 100 Gb/s/λ IMDD PON, classifying PAM-8 symbols and outperforming FFE and Volterra equalizers under strong nonlinearity. In this role, the trade-off is computational complexity, since the NN requires more multiplications per symbol than FFE or VNE, and the validity of results depends on training with random data rather than PRBS.

### Ensemble Learning (Gradient Boosting and MLP Hybrid)
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios | gen: 8d9e222c803c -->
Ensemble learning combines multiple regressors to exploit complementary strengths. In a hybrid framework for PON performance prediction, a Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR), valued for stability, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), valued for flexible nonlinear modeling, are trained for each of six targets (Q-factor, BER and receiver sensitivity, upstream and downstream). Hyperparameters such as MLP hidden-layer sizes and GBR learning rate and depth are tuned by GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation, and final predictions use a weighted average y = 0.6·ŷ_GBR + 0.4·ŷ_MLP, a ratio selected by sensitivity analysis as the best balance between stability and nonlinear learning. Evaluated with MAE, RMSE and R², the ensemble outperformed Linear Regression, Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Random Forest baselines, reaching an RMSE of 0.76 and MAE of 0.58, with all targets above R² of 0.94.

### Synthetic Dataset Generation for Optical Systems
<!-- sources: a_hybrid_deep_learning_approach_for_performance_prediction_in_optical_communication_systems_based_on_pon_scenarios | gen: a1e009c0e770 -->
When measured data are scarce, machine-learning models for optical systems can be trained on synthetic datasets. One example generated 1000 samples in Python, each with inputs of transmission distance (discrete values such as 0, 10, 20, 40, 45, 70 and 75 km), power level (-5 to 5 dBm) and noise factor (uniform in [0.1, 2.0]), and targets of Q-factor, BER (derived via the Gaussian relation) and receiver sensitivity for upstream and downstream. Preprocessing included log10 transformation of BER, second-order feature engineering (squared power level and noise factor), and an 80/20 train/test split. Because the data are synthetic, the model inherits their limits: it slightly overestimated Q-factor in back-to-back scenarios, attributed to insufficient noise representation in the training data for ideal conditions. Dataset coverage of distances, powers and noise levels therefore determines how well predictions reflect physics-based results.

## Edge Computing and Optical X-haul

### Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC)
<!-- sources: from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: a87dc5a8509e -->
MEC evolved from Mobile Cloud Computing (MCC) to remove the need for user data to traverse the core network, which causes congestion and propagation latency when computation resides in distant centralized data centers. Standardized by ETSI, MEC places servers at the radio access network (RAN) edge, for example at base stations or cell aggregation points, and provides local computation, storage and caching close to users. Unlike MCC, MEC is designed to serve both wired and wireless networks, so the same edge resources can support mobile users and fixed customers. This proximity is what allows millisecond-range response for latency-sensitive applications such as telesurgery, industrial automation and AR/VR, which centralized cloud computing cannot meet because of the distance between users and data centers. MEC servers also host machine-learning and deep-learning workloads, such as local analytics and traffic prediction, that would otherwise add delay if sent to a remote cloud.

### PON-MEC Integration Architecture
<!-- sources: from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: 6571206da346 -->
An integrated PON-MEC network connects the optical line terminal (OLT) to a centralized cloud while also linking optical network units (ONUs), which co-exist with mobile base stations, to MEC servers at the RAN edge. In this configuration the MEC servers provide low-latency processing for both wireless mobile users and wired customers, and the high-capacity, low-delay PON serves as the transport between them, enabling resource coordination across heterogeneous network segments. The architecture supports several latency-sensitive use cases. In e-health, wearable IoT surveillance and remote surgery require about 99.999% reliability and latency below 1 ms, so PON links carry the data while MEC servers run heavy ML/DL analysis locally. In Industry 4.0, applications such as Human-Agent-Robot Teamwork (HART) and predictive maintenance need latencies of roughly 10-25 ms and packet loss rates of 10^-9 or lower, with MEC computation supporting task selection between resource-constrained robots and resource-rich cloud agents. For AR/VR/XR in medical training or robotic surgery, the architecture provides the bandwidth and edge processing needed for large volumes of video and audio data with minimal delay.

### Optical X-haul and Functional Splits for 5G/6G
<!-- sources: from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: 078552f787ba -->
6G targets peak data rates of 100 Gb/s and latency below 1 ms, which makes the massive fronthaul traffic of traditional CPRI impractical. To manage it, baseband unit (BBU) functions are divided through functional splits into a Centralized Unit (CU), a Distributed Unit (DU) and a Remote Unit (RU), reducing the transport bandwidth between the units compared with raw CPRI sampling. PON is identified as a cost-effective x-haul (fronthaul, midhaul and backhaul) solution for split options above 7.2, such as those used in O-RAN, since it shares fiber and transceivers among many cells. Open challenges include scalability as the number of cells and ONUs grows, and the impact of PON discovery windows, during which upstream transmission is paused to register new ONUs, on network latency.

### Tactile Internet and Human-to-Machine/Robot Communications

The emergence of 6G, the Tactile Internet and Human-to-Machine/Robot (H2M/R) communications creates demand for millisecond-range latency and very high reliability (on the order of 99.999%) to support applications such as telesurgery, Human-Agent-Robot Teamwork (HART) in smart factories, and AR/VR. ## Resource Allocation and Control

### Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation in PON
<!-- sources: from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: 869d6b2c3368 -->
Upstream bandwidth in a PON is shared through a REPORT-GRANT process: each ONU reports its queue occupancy to the OLT (or a central office, CO), which returns a GRANT specifying the transmission window. In conservative schemes the CO grants only what was requested, so packets that arrive after the report must wait for the next cycle, giving an average packet waiting time of about 1.5 polling cycles. To reduce this, a 'bonus' bandwidth is added to the request, so that BW_GRANT = min{BW_REQ + BW_BONUS, BW_MAX}, with BW_MAX capping the grant. The goal is to choose BW_bonus so that waiting time falls to about 0.5 polling cycles without over-granting, which would waste bandwidth and reduce efficiency. The best bonus depends on network features such as the number of ONUs, maximum link distance, data rate, traffic load and packet arrival pattern; for example, more ONUs raise final latency through greater competition for bandwidth, and longer links require a larger bonus to offset the increased round-trip time.

### Federated Reinforcement Learning for Network Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: from_5g_to_beyond_passive_optical_network_and_multi-access_edge_computing_integration_for_latency-sensitive_applications | gen: b741f2bafd63 -->
Federated reinforcement learning (FedRL) combines reinforcement learning for decision optimization with federated learning (FL) for sharing experience among multiple PON-MEC systems, and has been applied to choosing the bonus bandwidth in PON uplink grants. In each local PON-MEC, the CO runs Q-learning with reward defined as negative latency, updates a local value function Q_net using the Bellman optimality equation, and applies a greedy policy to pick the bonus from a discretized set of values. To avoid the time cost of RL exploration, which produces sub-optimal decisions, multiple PON-MECs share their Q-values through a federated function Q_fed that local COs consult to converge faster; the process is asynchronous and computationally light (O(1) value updates). FL effectiveness depends on environment homogeneity, so only PON-MECs with similar features (number of ONUs, maximum link distance, data rate, traffic load and packet arrival pattern) should form a federation, to avoid performance degradation. In simulations with tactile-haptic traffic (Generalized Pareto distribution) and UDP traffic, FedRL reduced latency by 40% relative to baseline schemes for tactile traffic, and a federation of N=10 PON-MECs reached optimal latency with 75-90% time savings compared with purely local RL. Remaining work includes refining the ML techniques and defining criteria for selecting which PON-MECs join a federation.

## Sensing and Security in PON

### Distributed Fiber Optic Sensing in PON
<!-- sources: advanced_technologies_for_next-generation_passive_optical_networks | gen: 162ebccd5288 -->
Distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) enables monitoring of civil infrastructure and of the networks themselves, but it is difficult in point-to-multipoint PONs: backscattered signals are attenuated by the passive splitters, and results become ambiguous because reflections from multiple drop fibers overlap. Experimental remedies are generally invasive. On the ONU side they add reflective semiconductor optical amplifiers (RSOA) or Faraday rotator mirrors (FRM); on the ODN side they replace standard single-mode fiber with enhanced scatter fiber (ESF). The transition to coherent PONs is expected to allow more efficient, lower-cost DSP-based sensing, since the coherent receiver already captures amplitude and phase and polarization information, supporting digital longitudinal monitoring along the fiber and polarization state tracking without such hardware modifications.

### Physical Layer Security in PON
<!-- sources: advanced_technologies_for_next-generation_passive_optical_networks | gen: d978f91681d4 -->
Downstream PON signals are broadcast to all ONUs, making them inherently exposed to eavesdropping. Higher-layer encryption exists, but physical layer security (PLS) is preferred because it avoids added latency and transmission overhead. Existing PLS approaches have drawbacks: quantum key distribution (QKD) offers unconditional security but is limited by data rate, requires expensive hardware and favors point-to-point links, while chaos communications mask data with chaotic carriers but share similar cost and device requirements. An alternative uses chaotic digital filters in which noiselike orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) signals serve as private keys. This approach provides security-by-design, being integrated at the transceiver design stage; openness-by-design, easing interoperability across vendors; and dynamic security, allowing protection to be enabled or disabled for specific traffic flows without disrupting the rest of the network.
