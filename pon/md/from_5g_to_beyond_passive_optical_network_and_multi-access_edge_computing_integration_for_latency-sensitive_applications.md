# A B S T R A C T

A recent paradigm shift in support of 5G-and-beyond (5GB), Human-to-Machine/Robot (H2M/R), and the Tactile Internet has resulted in a surge of latency-sensitive applications being delivered across communication networks. These applications in conjunction with exponential growth in connected devices, have pushed for architectural and capacity changes in both wired and wireless networks. Passive optical networks (PONs) have evolved in terms of their capacity and function over the years and are now supporting both fixed as well as x-haul solutions to deliver cost-efficient, low-latency and high-bandwidth applications to end-users. In combination with multi-access edge computing (MEC), high computational tasks of applications can be offloaded to servers much closer to end-users further reducing the network latency. To this end, this paper presents a comprehensive review of the capabilities of integrated PON-MECs in delivering emerging latencysensitive applications. Specific use case scenarios benefiting from integrated PON-MECs are also detailed. Shared learning through a federated PON-MEC framework to expedite resource allocation decisions is discussed and finally, the time-savings arising from implementing the proposed framework is quantified, showing further improvements in network latency performance.

# **1. Introduction**

With the proliferation of 5G networks, Internet-of-Things (IoT) [[1](#page-9-0)], and Machine-to-Machine communication (M2M) [\[2\]](#page-9-1), attention is now focused on next-generation network paradigms including 6G networks and the Tactile Internet [[3](#page-9-2)[,4\]](#page-9-3). Wired and wireless communication networks will continue to rapidly evolve both in terms of their architectures and capabilities to support the surge in latency-sensitive human-to-machine/robot (H2M/R) applications such as augmented reality/virtual reality (AR/VR) [[5](#page-9-4)], industry automation [[6\]](#page-9-5), telesurgery [[7](#page-9-6)], and intelligent transport systems (ITS) [[8](#page-9-7)]. The expedited growth in the number of mobile devices, projected to reach 29.3B in 2023 and with each envisioned to generate large amounts of data, has also warranted new network architectures, hardware, and software solutions [\[5,](#page-9-4)[9](#page-9-8),[10\]](#page-9-9).

Today, the seamless delivery of latency-sensitive and reliable services in 5G networks is realized by enabling technologies such as multi-access edge computing (MEC) [\[9,](#page-9-8)[11](#page-9-10)] and fog computing [\[12](#page-9-11)]. Prior to MEC, cloud services such as computing, storage, and caching were performed at centralized data centers, often at a long distance away from end-users [[13\]](#page-9-12). As a result, user data experienced high propagation delays that prevented the underlying networks from meeting stringent end-to-end latency requirements of applications. To overcome this problem, MEC enabled cloud computing and IT services to be brought closer to the end-users through the placement of MEC servers at the access network edge [\[9,](#page-9-8)[14](#page-9-13)[,15](#page-9-14)]. Rather than having the data traverse through the mobile backhaul or core networks, computing, storage, and caching are now rendered at the access network edge [[16](#page-9-15)].

Recent studies on MEC and 5G have highlighted the benefits of using optical fiber networks to support the increasing number of connected devices and their quality-of-service (QoS)-aware applications. Optical fiber benefits from high capacity and low propagation delay. Taking advantage of optical fiber that is already deployed in a majority of urban areas close to residential and industrial premises, the cost-efficient and high-capacity passive optical network (PON) has become a natural choice for fronthauling and backhauling of 5G and 5GB networks [[17](#page-9-16)]. The PON has evolved over the years in terms of its architecture, hardware, capacity, and underlying protocols and algorithms [[18](#page-9-17)]. These developments in conjunction with optical fiber deployments that

# **2. Background: Passive optical networks and multi access edge computing**

<span id="page-1-0"></span>In this section, we provide an overview of major PON and MEC architectures and their inception to recent deployments, which serves as the foundation to discuss the main concepts covered in the rest of the paper.

# *2.1. PON overview*

Over the last decade, we have witnessed the unrelentless penetration of optical fiber deeper and deeper into residential and business premises. According to [[19\]](#page-9-18), 40 percent of the world's population will have fixed Fiber-To-The-x (FTTx) connection by 2026. The models of FTTx, namely FTTH (home), FTTC (curb) and FTTB (building), installed either through a physical point-to-multipoint (P2MP) or point-to-point (P2P) topology offer direct fiber connection to or close to the endusers. The global FTTx market is still growing with a steep trajectory, predicted to reach 22.4B by 2026 [[20\]](#page-9-19).

Most FTTx networks are based on the PON technology due to its cost-effectiveness and low energy consumption per bit. The PON has a P2MP topology with an unpowered optical distribution network (ODN). Specifically, the ODN comprises a feeder fiber that connects the optical line terminal (OLT) at the central office (CO) to a passive splitter and a number of distribution fibers that connects the passive splitter to optical network units (ONUs) at end-user locations. For *N* subscribers, a PON requires only the installation of a single transceiver at CO and *N* transceivers at the ONUs, giving rise to *N*+1 transceivers overall. The shared feeder fiber saves CO space and allows for easy termination. This physical topology also allows its downstream and upstream bandwidth to be naturally shared through time division multiplexing (TDM) and time division multiple access (TDMA), respectively. # *2.2. MEC overview*

The concept of MEC evolved from mobile cloud computing (MCC) where mobile devices accessed remote centralized data centers through the mobile network to offload their computation and data processing [[29\]](#page-9-30). The downside of MCC is the user data having to traverse through the core and access networks to reach centralized cloud resources, thereby experiencing significant propagation delay and congesting the backhaul and core networks [[30\]](#page-9-31). As the number of mobile devices and the QoS requirements of their applications continued to grow, in order to enjoy the benefits of MCC while also meeting latency and other QoS requirements, cloud and IT services had to be brought closer to the end-users. For this purpose, Mobile Edge Computing which is an initiative of the ETSI Industry Specification Group (ISG), was identified as a favorable solution to integrate edge computation into mobile networks [[9](#page-9-8)[,31](#page-9-32),[32\]](#page-9-33).

[Fig.](#page-1-1) [1](#page-1-1) illustrates the placement of mobile edge computing servers at a close proximity to end-users in a radio access network (RAN). Specifically, the servers are placed at multiple locations including the LTE macro base station, radio network controller (RNC) or the multiradio access technology (RAT) cell aggregation point [\[31](#page-9-32)]. The servers function to either process requests from end users in its vicinity or forward requests onto the centralized data centers for further processing. Realizing the possibility of serving both mobile and wired networks through edge computing in order to deliver latency-sensitive and computation-heavy applications, ETSI further redefined Mobile Edge Computing as Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) in which the new MEC architecture is defined as one that serves both wired and wireless networks at the edge [\[35](#page-9-34)[,36](#page-9-35)], as shown in [Fig.](#page-1-1) [1.](#page-1-1)

## **3. PON-MEC integration**

### <span id="page-2-0"></span>*3.1. Network architecture*

It has emerged in recent literature the integration of both PON and MEC as a favorable solution in provisioning broadband access to meet QoS requirements. In [[13\]](#page-9-12), the authors investigate how to optimally plan a WDM-PON-based MEC-enabled fiber wireless access network (MFWAN) for latency-sensitive applications. The proposed work presents a mathematical model to minimize the end-to-end latency using constraints such as maximal transmission distance, maximal PON power budget, and bandwidth requests. In [\[37](#page-9-36)], the authors investigate different optical fronthaul architectures to support ultralow latency and reliability required in 5G mobile fronthaul. Taking Tactile Internet as a use case, the authors of [[3\]](#page-9-2) present a decentralized cooperative dynamic bandwidth allocation algorithms for end-to-end resource coordination in a MEC-enabled fiber-wireless (FiWi) access networks using an EPON. Additionally, the authors of [[38\]](#page-9-37) study the collaborative computation offloading in fiber-wireless networks, specifically considering the resource allocation between a mobile device and the MEC server, and also between the mobile device and the centralized server. The authors of [[33\]](#page-9-20) also propose a dynamic resource allocation algorithm considering both centralized cloud and MEC enabled fiber-wireless networks.

The architecture of an integrated PON-MEC network is schematically shown in [Fig.](#page-1-1) [1](#page-1-1), whereby the OLT of the PON interconnects a centralized cloud (via the core network and the Internet) with number of ONUs (via a passive splitter). The ONUs co-exist with mobile base stations (BSs) and are connected to MEC servers placed at the edge of the RAN. Under such an integrated PON-MEC network, the MEC servers support not just the mobile users but also wired customers through the ONUs. It is also important to note that not all mobile services require mobility and therefore would benefit from the fixed optical network resources connected to the RAN.

### *3.2. Use cases*

As discussed in the previous section, both PON and MEC have inherent characteristics to support QoS-aware emerging use cases. In this section, insights into how the integration of high-capacity PONs with MEC capability can address QoS requirements in some use case scenarios are highlighted.

# *3.2.1. X-hauling 5GB wireless networks*

Driven by the increasing number of mobile devices and the use of smart mobile applications, mobile technology is continuously and rapidly advancing. The rapid deployment of 5G wireless technology has reshaped network capabilities in an unimaginable way. To address the ongoing issue of providing mobile connectivity to an ever growing number of connected devices and with increasingly stringent QoS requirements, both academia and industry have recently begun to research the sixth generation (6G) mobile technology. According to [\[39](#page-9-38)], 6G mobile technology is envisioned to provide a peak data rate of 100 Gb/s, a user-experienced data rate of 1 Gb/s and an ultra-low latency of less than 1 ms over ubiquitous 3D coverage areas.

These high capacity future 5G/6G wireless networks must be supported by complementary high-capacity and secured transport networks for backhauling/fronthauling (x-hauling) the wireless cells to transport all wireless data to the metro or core networks [[40\]](#page-9-39). In particular, emerging 6G wireless networks is envisioned to consist of ultra-small wireless cells to achieve the required capacity and coverage requirements. As higher cell densities can lead to costlier x-haul transport connectivity [[41\]](#page-10-0), such cell densification calls for cost-efficient x-haul solutions in order to meet global business goals.

A traditional transport network typically consists of direct P2P fiber/wireless links between cell sites and the network core, which is commonly referred to as backhaul. However, to cope with the increasing demands in a cost and energy-efficient manner, a new paradigm of centralized processing of mobile signals was first introduced with 4G technology [[43\]](#page-10-1). Cloud/centralized radio access network (C-RAN) is one of the main architectures currently deployed as centralized processing allows for advance wireless cooperative communication functionalities. In C-RAN, all functions are centralized in a baseband processing unit (BBU) which is placed at a central location. Radio frequency (RF) functionalities are performed at the remote radio head (RRH), located at the antenna cell site. In this architecture, RF signals are digitized and sent over fiber to the centralized BBU using Common Public Radio Interface (CPRI).

In a native C-RAN, the bandwidth required for this digitized signal scales with the physical parameters of antenna, e.g., antenna port count and wireless bandwidth in use. However, with the high number of antenna ports in use in 5G and beyond networks, the fronthaul between the BBU and RRH consequently demands exceedingly high-capacity links. As an example, using CPRI to support a fronthaul with 200 MHz of bandwidth, 64 QAM, 8X12 MIMO, and 96 antenna ports, will necessitate a 896 Gb/s link [[44\]](#page-10-2). The demand for such high-capacity links makes C-RAN an impractical architecture.

A solution to this problem is to move some functionalities back to the cell site. There are ten different standardized ways, termed functional split options, in which functions can be split between the BBU and RRH [\[45](#page-10-3)]. In the latest 5G New Radio (NR) transport architecture, BBU functions are split into three entities. The centralized unit (CU), distributed unit (DU), and remote unit (RU). This new design in 5G facilitates RAN virtualization with flexible assignment of computing resources. The functional split options and their required x-haul data rates are illustrated in [Fig.](#page-3-0) [2](#page-3-0).

In comparison to other competing access technologies, PON can play a critical role in realizing high-capacity yet low-cost x-haul links [[46\]](#page-10-4). To date, different PON architectures have been investigated for this purpose [\[46](#page-10-4)[–48](#page-10-5)]. As an example, ITU-T G.Sup66 investigated traditional and emerging PON technologies to support different functional split options, while considering different bandwidth, latency, and other x-haul and 5G parameter constraints [\[48](#page-10-5)]. It is also worth mentioning that while the PON is unable to support low layer functional split options such as Option 8 and 7.1 due to its capacity limitation, it is still a suitable candidate to support functional split options above 7.2 (refer to [Fig.](#page-3-0) [2](#page-3-0)). Specifically, PON can easily support 5G transport with the 7.2 or higher layer functional split options, and this specific use of PON is considered in the latest RAN architectures such as Open Intelligent RAN (O-RAN) [[49\]](#page-10-6). With the principles of intelligence and openness in mind, the O-RAN architecture is standardized for building a virtualized RAN on open hardware with the help of artificial intelligence assisted radio control.

There are however existing challenges that requires addressing if considering PON-based x-haul solutions. Depending on the actual functional split option used, a key issue concerns the realization a scalable and cost-effective deployments. Several studies have investigated approaches to plan and dimension PON-based x-haul cost-effectively [[42](#page-10-7)[,50](#page-10-8)[,51](#page-10-9)]. For example in [[42\]](#page-10-7), the authors propose a generalized integer linear programming based optimization framework that can be used to jointly plan both PON-based x-haul and 5G wireless networks to provide the cost-optimal network design in any given deployment scenario and irrespective of the RAN architecture used. # *3.2.2. e-Health*

Healthcare has evolved significantly over the years. Services that previously required in-person interaction with healthcare professionals are now being delivered remotely. For example, intelligent health surveillance systems have been proposed using wearable IoT sensors, which collect data pertaining to user behaviors. This sensor data is then processed and analyzed using ML and deep learning (DL) techniques to identify user patterns and anomalies. Such applications however, are latency sensitive and require significant processing resources which an end user may not be equipped with [\[60](#page-10-12)]. As such, MEC has been proposed for such applications to offload their computation and processing tasks to an edge server placed close to the end-user. Another emerging e-health application gaining interest is remote surgery, where a skilled surgeon performs a surgery on a patient who is remotely located. The surgeon controls a surgical robot placed at the patient side who mirrors the actions taken by the surgeon. Due to the life-critical nature of this application, the end-to-end latency is expected to be less than 1 ms with a reliability of 99.999% [[36\]](#page-9-35). Applications of this nature will benefit from PON-MEC integration as the PON will provide high bandwidth and reliability while the MEC ensures faster processing of data using ML and DL at the network edge.

[Fig.](#page-4-1) [3](#page-4-1) depicts integrated PON-MECs supporting different use case scenarios. More specifically, MEC servers are placed at the edge of the network and are connected to the ONUs placed at the base stations. For health surveillance applications, data generated by the user wearable sensors will be analyzed in these MEC servers in order to identify user patterns and to send warnings or notifications to carers or emergency services in case of an emergency. The location and contextawareness together with high-capacity optical fiber connections will ensure latency-sensitive service delivery in this scenario. In applications such as remote surgery, the MEC-PON integration itself may not be able to satisfy stringent latency requirements. Additional techniques, including ML to predict the resource requirement of the end-users, are also necessary [[61\]](#page-10-13). Both MEC and centralized servers can be used for this purpose, depending on their computation capabilities and trained data stored at the MEC for reduced latency.

### *3.2.3. Industry 4.0*

Industry 4.0 involves improving the operation of traditional factories such that they are able to run more efficiently and adapt quickly to changing requirements of the production lines [\[62](#page-10-14)]. A shift in this direction is made possible by novel IoT technologies, data analysis techniques, and network improvements such as edge computing. As discussed in [\[63](#page-10-15)], a large amount of data is being generated at factory settings. These data can be analyzed and be converted to intelligence which can be used to optimize different aspects of the manufacturing process [\[64](#page-10-16)]. The human–agent–robot teamwork (HART) is another promising concept that can be used in factory floors to provide either location-dependent or location-independent but computation heavy services [[58\]](#page-10-17). The process can be optimized to select either a resource-limited robot for tasks that requires moving from one place to the other or a resource-rich cloud agent for tasks that requires heavy computation.

The factory automation services of this nature deals with controlling the equipment in real-time, which would need latency in the range of 25 to 10 ms depending on the application and significant data processing. Further, due to the critical nature of these applications, a reliability with packet loss rate no less than 10−<sup>9</sup> is expected [[65\]](#page-10-18). As such, a network architecture that combines the low-latency computation of MEC and reliability and high bandwidth of PON is preferred. The

# *3.2.4. Augmented Reality/Virtual Reality*

Augmented Reality (AR), Virtual Reality (VR) and Extended Reality (XR) have become popular technologies in recent years to enhance the digital experience of users through providing a digital component to the real world, real life experience in the digital world, and a combination of both. AR-based remote monitoring is proposed to perform daily routines carried out by the medical professional using robots and is expected to have comparatively high precision than other conventional remote monitoring systems. AR-based robotic surgery is also proposed as means of getting more precision while performing invasive remote surgery, either when the surgeons are not available at the patient's medical facility or to reduce risk of exposure. # **4. Federated PON-MECs for H2M/R communications**

# <span id="page-4-0"></span>*4.1. Motivation*

# *4.2. Bandwidth allocation in PON-MECs*

As H2M/R communications are highly interactive, the PON needs to support frequent data exchange amongst the MEC servers/ONU as shown by PON-MEC 2 of [Fig.](#page-5-0) [4](#page-5-0). As uplink bandwidth is shared, the allocation of uplink bandwidth to each MEC server/ONU to communicate with the CO, is particularly important to maximize bandwidth efficiency and latency performance. In that regard, a REPORT-GRANT process is adopted in classic dynamic bandwidth allocation algorithms to allocate bandwidth to ONUs [[75](#page-10-29)]. Firstly, ONUs request for bandwidth based on their uplink buffer occupancy, in REPORT messages to the CO in a round robin fashion. Upon receiving a REPORT message, the CO then allocates bandwidth to the ONU through the GRANT messages. The time interval between an ONU's consecutive uplink transmissions is known as a polling cycle, which in general is the scale of milliseconds [\[76](#page-10-30)]. The dominant latency is attributed to the waiting time a packet incurs while waiting to be transmitted upstream from the ONU. If an ONU is allocated insufficient , its packets will spend an increased waiting time in the buffer, thus degrading the overall latency performance of the network.

In the baseline scheme, the CO grants equivalent to that requested by an ONU but only up to a maximum, i.e. = min{, }. This scheme is considered a conservative scheme as any new packet arrivals will need to be queued and buffered until they are reported. On average, a packet will incur a latency equivalent to an approximate 1.5 polling-cycle time before being transmitted. To reduce this latency, a common solution is to predict the surplus bandwidth corresponding to new packet arrivals and allocate this bandwidth, , along with [[77\]](#page-10-31). Hence, with bandwidth prediction, the CO grants bandwidth as follows:

$$BW_{GRANT} = \min\{BW_{REQ} + BW_{BONUS}, BW_{MAX}\}. \tag{1}$$

As can be gathered, is a key decision parameter in which under-granting can lead to increased waiting time while over-granting can lead to bandwidth inefficiency. Ideally, will reduce the waiting time of a newly arrived packet to 0.5 polling-cycle time. That is, newly arrived packets can be transmitted immediately without having to wait for the next polling cycle. To determine , conventional dynamic bandwidth allocation algorithms exploit statistical prediction, i.e. using statistical features of the data received and transmitted in previous polling cycles. Different statistical prediction methods suit different data traffic profiles. For example, Bayesian estimation [[78\]](#page-10-32) and linear regression [[79\]](#page-10-33) are used to predict traffic with favorable time-average performance whereas moving average [\[80](#page-10-34)] is more suitable to predicting bursty traffic.

# *4.3. Reinforcement learning-based bandwidth allocation in federated PON-MECs*

In recent years, supervised ML techniques have been investigated to improve the prediction accuracy of [\[81](#page-10-35)[–84](#page-10-36)] though the focus has not always been to meet stringent latency constraints. Specifically to support H2M communications over PON-MECs of [Fig.](#page-5-0) [4](#page-5-0), we proposed a federated reinforcement learning (FedRL) framework that leverages: (a) reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize together; with (b) federated learning (FL) between multiple PON-MECs to minimize the time cost in executing sub-optimal decisions [[85\]](#page-10-37).

RL is renown for optimizing decisions via exploration–exploitation and allowing the decisions to be self-adaptive to the environment [[86](#page-10-38)]. In RL, an agent learns the best decision through strategic exploration of various decisions and receiving the rewards. As a trade-off, an optimal decision comes at the expense of time cost in executing sub-optimal decisions. In the short term, the decision exploration may negatively affect network performance, e.g. network latency.

<span id="page-5-1"></span>To overcome the limitations of RL, we leverage FL to enable experience sharing between multiple PON-MECs that form a federation. By definition, FL is to enable distributed devices to collaboratively learn a shared ML model [[87\]](#page-10-39). It is expected that all PON-MECs in a federation have similar states, e.g., with similar architecture and traffic environment, and thus sharing of decision experience between the PON-MECs will accelerate individual decision making, eventually benefiting all PON-MECs in the federation.

In the subsections below, we first introduce local RL in individual PON-MECs, and then FL to expedite local learning in the proposed FedRL framework. Finally, we introduce the key network features to be considered in selecting federated PON-MECs for FL.

### *4.3.3. Key features for federated PON-MEC*

In FedRL, the heuristic sharing in ([4\)](#page-6-2) is efficient when the federated PON-MECs are homogeneous. ### **5. Summary**

<span id="page-8-0"></span>The surge in latency-sensitive and computation-intensive applications due to the proliferation of 5GB, Tactile Internet and H2M/R communication in conjunction with the exponential growth in connected devices, have pushed for architectural and capacity changes in both wired and wireless networks. To this end, we have discussed the evolution of high-capacity PONs and MEC, and as importantly how their inherent characteristics help in supporting latency-sensitive applications. Example use case scenarios that can benefit from integrated PON-MECs have also been detailed. As the integration of these two architectures does not guarantee that latency requirements will be met, end-to-end resource allocation across these heterogeneous networks has to be carefully managed through dynamic resource allocation algorithms. Specifically to support latency-sensitive H2M/R communications over PON-MECs, we proposed a framework based on federated reinforcement learning that harnesses reinforcement learning to optimize bandwidth allocation decision together with shared learning between multiple PON-MECs to maximize time-savings in reaching such decisions, further improving latency performance. Results show that the proposed framework achieves significant time-savings as compared to the baseline and linear credit schemes. With the investigation of the framework for PON-MEC resource allocation showing overwhelming benefit in supporting latency-sensitive applications, our future research focus will be on the exploration of suitable machine learning techniques to accelerate reinforcement learning decisions within each PON-MEC and the identification of which specific PON-MECs to form a federation.
