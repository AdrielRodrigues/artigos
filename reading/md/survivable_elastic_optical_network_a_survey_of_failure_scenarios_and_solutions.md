### A B S T R A C T

Survivable Elastic Optical Networks (SEON) are at the forefront of high-speed, flexible, and bandwidthefficient optical communication. Modern flex-grid Elastic Optical Networks (EON) allocate variable-width frequency slots to match heterogeneous traffic demands and modulation formats; the survivability mechanisms discussed here operate at the spectrum slot layer and are agnostic to the underlying transponder waveform. Despite these advantages, ensuring survivability against diverse failures, including single-link, multi-link, node and hardware outages, remains a major challenge. This paper presents a comprehensive survey of SEON survivability, with a particular emphasis on classifying solutions according to different failure scenarios. Stateof-the-art strategies such as Dedicated Path Protection, Shared Backup Path Protection, segmented and span restoration, and p-cycle protection are systematically analyzed and compared. Unlike previous surveys, this work integrates a classification framework with performance insights, highlighting how existing mechanisms fare under different types of failures. To bridge existing gaps, we also discuss open research challenges, including centralized control, spectrum management, AI-driven resilience, physical layer impairments and disaster recovery. Furthermore, we outline the potential of a hybrid protection paradigm combining stateaware traffic splitting, dynamic allocation, and vulnerability scoring to improve spectrum utilization and reduce blocking probability. By linking failure scenarios with recovery strategies and future research needs, this survey provides a unique perspective to guide the design of scalable, adaptive and intelligent SEON architectures.

#### <span id="page-1-0"></span>**1. Introduction**

The technology associated with Elastic Optical Networks, particularly in terms of their resilience and adaptability, has undergone significant advancements in recent years. With the rapid evolution of global internet connectivity encompassing 5G and beyond, WiFi, the Internet of Things (IoT), mobile technology [\[1\]](#page-18-6), machine-to-machine (M2M) communication [[2](#page-18-7)], and centralized control paradigms such as Software-Defined Networking (SDN) [[3](#page-18-8)], the global digital landscape is set to transform dramatically. According to Cisco's Annual Internet Report (2018–2023) projected that by 2023 roughly two-thirds of the world's population (about 5.3 billion people) would be online [[4](#page-18-9)]. Recent ITU estimates are consistent with that trajectory: around 5.5 billion people about 68% of the global population were online in 2024, with roughly 2.6 billion still offline [\[5\]](#page-18-10). A comprehensive list of the acronyms and abbreviations used throughout this paper is provided in [Table](#page-2-0) [1](#page-2-0) for ease of reference

Simultaneously, the field of optical networks has experienced rapid development, evolving from TON [[6](#page-18-11)] based on WDM and fixed-grid technology. Given that networking resources are both crucial and finite, inefficient utilization of spectrum resources becomes an even greater concern, particularly as frequency spacing expands. A significant portion of the available spectrum can remain underutilized when channels are assigned fixed bandwidths, creating large unused frequency gaps where no traffic can be transmitted. This inefficiency is inherent in WDM systems due to their coarse granularity, with fixed 50 GHz or 100 GHz channel spacing. As a result, traditional WDM network architectures [\[7\]](#page-18-12) exhibit limited elasticity and flexibility at the optical layer.

The demand for highly dynamic and adaptive traffic management is becoming increasingly critical in today's connectivity-driven world [[8](#page-18-13), [9](#page-18-14)]. To address the rigid constraints of TONs, researchers have proposed spectrum-efficient EON built on Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) [[10–](#page-18-15)[12\]](#page-18-16). Due to their ability to dynamically allocate large optical bandwidths based on heterogeneous traffic demands, flexigrid EON are widely recognized as a promising solution for future ultra-high-speed optical transmission systems [[13\]](#page-18-17).

One of the first EON architectures, SLICE, was introduced in [\[14](#page-18-18)]. Compared to traditional WDM networks, this EON model features finer spectrum granularity, allocating an appropriate number of FSs for each optical channel based on the actual data rate and modulation format. Unlike conventional 50 GHz or 100 GHz fixed-grid frequency allocation, EON align optical channels to a narrower grid frequency, enabling greater flexibility in bandwidth allocation and improving spectrum utilization efficiency.

However, spectrum continuity constraints impose a significant challenge in EON. Each optical channel must utilize the same set of contiguous FSs across all fiber links it traverses, which complicates network management. While a similar limitation, wavelength continuity exists in traditional WDM networks, the spectrum continuity constraint in EON is more complex because it requires ensuring the continuity of multiple FSs across different links.

The EON has drawn a lot of interest from both academia and business since it was initially put forth. Numerous relevant studies have been conducted, including:

• Centralized network control and management such as SDON, SDEON [\[12](#page-18-16),[15–](#page-18-19)[32,32–](#page-19-0)[40\]](#page-19-1).

- Distributed network control and management based on the GM-PLS [[41–](#page-19-2)[48](#page-19-3)].
- Traffic grooming such as bandwidth squeezing [[49,](#page-19-4)[50](#page-19-5)].
- RSA [\[51](#page-19-6)].
- Issues regarding survivability, such as single and multi-link failures on EON [[8](#page-18-13)].

Since all of the previously mentioned studies concerning the EON have been skimmed over by researchers working in fields like GMPLS, RSA and network control and management. Network survivability is one of the issues that has to be further investigated in terms of various unique constraints and specific primary techniques for restoration and protection that aid our researchers in the field of SEON development.

The novelty of this survey lies in its comprehensive treatment of survivability in SEON by unifying various failure scenarios ranging from single link to hardware and node-level disruptions into a single structured survey framework. Unlike existing surveys that focus narrowly on either protection or restoration, our work systematically examines both strategies under the lens of modern SEON requirements. Furthermore, we introduce a hybrid protection perspective that integrates symmetrical traffic splitting, dynamic allocation and a vulnerability scoring. This integrated model not only enhances resource efficiency and blocking probability performance but also provides a fresh direction for intelligent and adaptive SEON design.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

- We present a detailed classification of failure scenarios in SEON, covering single-link, multi-link, node and hardware failures and analyze their implications on spectrum allocation and survivability.
- We survey state-of-the-art survivability mechanisms and benchmark their effectiveness in SEON.
- We propose a hybrid protection model that combines Vulnerability scoring-based path selection with traffic splitting, thereby reducing blocking probability and improving spectrum utilization.
- We identify open research challenges such as centralized control integration, AI-driven resilience and disaster recovery, outlining promising directions for future work.

This paper begins by presenting a comprehensive overview of the existing state of the art in the field of SEON. Subsequently, it delves into a discourse on recent progress and scholarly investigations pertaining to various schemes in Section [2.](#page-1-2) Finally, an examination of diverse failure scenarios is conducted with the objective of ensuring the preservation of survivability in Section [3.](#page-7-4) In light of the literature review and investigations discussed before, we now proceed to examine the future research challenges pertaining to the SEON in Section [4](#page-9-2). The work's conclusion is given in Section [5.](#page-14-2)

The core mechanisms for ensuring survivability are protection and restoration strategies, which are further classified into: Link Protection and Restoration, and Path Protection and Restoration, as depicted in [Fig.](#page-2-1) [1](#page-2-1). These strategies aim to minimize service disruptions and enhance network robustness by preemptively securing backup resources (protection) or dynamically recovering from failures (restoration).

Protection-based techniques in EON are designed to ensure network resilience by preemptively allocating backup resources or paths to mitigate potential failures. These methods are broadly categorized into Ring-Cover, p-Cycle, DPP, and SBPP. The core principle of protectionbased survivability is to establish fail-safe routes in advance, which remain idle until a failure occurs. When a disruption is detected, the affected traffic is swiftly rerouted to a pre-configured backup path, enabling fast recovery with minimal service interruption. This proactive approach significantly enhances network reliability by reducing recovery time compared to reactive methods. Additionally, protectionbased techniques offer efficient resource allocation, as some methods, such as SBPP, allow for resource sharing among multiple connections, optimizing bandwidth utilization. These techniques are highly scalable and adaptable, ensuring robust protection at both the link and path levels. By employing these strategies, EON can effectively handle network failures while maintaining high availability, seamless connectivity, and minimal data loss. The following are the primary traits of protection-based survivability.

- Fast Failover: Because the backup link is already built and available to be used when necessary, protection-based solutions provide fast failover speeds.
- Paths already established: Prior to any failure, backup routes are calculated and set up. This means that even if they are not being used right now, the network must commit resources for protection.
- More Resource Overhead: Compared to restoration-based procedures, protection-based strategies may have more resource overhead since they need additional network resources to support the backup channels.

Unlike protection-based methods, which rely on pre-configured backup paths, restoration-based solutions focus on dynamically identifying alternative routes in real-time when a failure occurs. These techniques leverage the network management system to detect failures and compute new paths as needed, rather than reserving dedicated backup resources in advance. This dynamic approach provides greater resource efficiency since spare capacity is not pre-allocated, making it more adaptable to unpredictable network conditions. However, restorationbased methods typically involve longer recovery times compared to

In SEON, restoration-based solutions focus on recovering disrupted traffic and services following a network failure. These methods swiftly detect alternative channels and reroute affected traffic to ensure minimal service disruption. Unlike protection-based techniques, which rely on pre-configured backup paths, restoration methods dynamically identify and allocate available resources in real time. Common restoration strategies in SEON include local restoration, end-to-end restoration, segment-based restoration, and dynamic restoration, each offering varying levels of recovery speed and resource efficiency.

Network protection and restoration techniques are generally classified into link-based and path-based approaches. In link-based restoration, failures caused by a damaged or failed link are addressed by rerouting traffic to a pre-established backup path (a set of links). This method enables a rapid localized response and minimizes network downtime. On the other hand, path-based restoration focuses on protecting or restoring entire communication paths (lightpaths) rather than individual links. Since a communication path consists of a series of interconnected nodes and links connecting the source and destination, path-based solutions involve the network management system dynamically determining an alternate route to bypass the affected area. This approach ensures that traffic is successfully rerouted to its intended destination, even in the case of major failures. Path-based solutions primarily rely on the two end nodes of the communication path to coordinate the recovery process. While link-based restoration offers faster failure recovery, path-based restoration provides a more comprehensive and flexible fault-tolerant mechanism, making both approaches essential for ensuring robust network survivability in SEON.

### <span id="page-3-0"></span>*2.1. Dedicated path and shared backup path protection scheme*

<span id="page-3-2"></span>Among various network survivability techniques, DPP is highly recommended due to its short recovery time and ability to pre-provision backup network resources, such as lightpaths, for each connection. In EON, network resources refer to the spectrum resources of optical fiber links, and path protection is equivalent to allocating backup lightpaths for functional lightpath connections [\[54](#page-19-9)].

To illustrate, consider a scenario where a virtual link transmits 100 Gbit/s of traffic. One way to protect this traffic against failures is by establishing an additional physical path with the same 100 Gbit/s capacity, but along a separate route, as shown in [Fig.](#page-3-1) [2.](#page-3-1) If a failure occurs on the primary path S-A-B-D, for example, the failure of link A-B—the affected virtual link is restored using the backup path S-C-D. This methodology is referred to as DPP [[55\]](#page-19-10).

DPP can be categorized into three types: 1+1 protection, 1:1 protection, and quasi 1+1 protection. In 1+1 protection, traffic is simultaneously transmitted on both the working and backup paths, ensuring instantaneous recovery in the event of failure. Switching equipment is deployed at the receiver, which continuously monitors both paths and instantly switches to the backup path if a failure occurs. While this method ensures rapid recovery, it requires additional hardware and dedicated spectrum allocation. Conversely, in 1:1 protection, traffic is transmitted only on the working path under normal conditions, and only switched to the backup path upon failure detection. While this approach reduces resource consumption, it requires longer recovery time compared to 1+1 protection. However, in 1:1 protection, the backup path can be temporarily utilized for lower-priority traffic that would otherwise be discarded in case of a failure [\[56](#page-19-11)]. A hybrid approach, quasi 1+1 protection, attempts to balance dedicated and shared protection by grouping multiple critical paths with similar protection requirements. This technique optimizes spectrum allocation and resource utilization, improving network efficiency [[57–](#page-19-12)[60\]](#page-19-13).

A defragmentation-based approach using 1+1 path protection in EON has been explored in [[57–](#page-19-12)[60\]](#page-19-13). The primary objective is to enhance network traffic acceptance rates by optimizing the spectrum allocation for backup paths. This method enables dynamic rerouting and spectrum reallocation, allowing primary and backup paths to exchange roles based on varying network conditions. Another strategy, Partitioned DPP (PDPP), was introduced in [[55,](#page-19-10)[61,](#page-19-14)[62](#page-19-15)], where the total transmission rate is divided into multiple disjoint paths. These paths may carry equal or unequal portions of the traffic, thereby reducing spectral usage and minimizing resource wastage compared to traditional protection mechanisms.

Further improvements in dedicated traffic-aware strategies were proposed in [[63\]](#page-19-16), integrating DPP with traffic forecasting models to reduce bandwidth blocking probability, especially under dynamic traffic demands. Additionally, [[64\]](#page-19-17) investigated the integration of DPP with Content-Oriented Networking (CON) for content delivery in EON, evaluating its impact on capital expenditure (CAPEX), power consumption, and spectrum usage. Numerical analyses based on Cisco traffic patterns and real-world network topologies indicate that DPP protection introduces an additional overhead of 2.1 to 3.5 times in the examined metrics. However, optimizing the content delivery scenario can lead to up to 50% savings across key network performance metrics. Overall, integrating DPP with CON presents a promising approach for efficient optical bandwidth distribution in next-generation networks.

Next is SBPP where, multiple protection paths share common resources, reducing the overall spectrum and cost requirements for survivability. As illustrated in [Fig.](#page-4-1) [3,](#page-4-1) the solid lines represent primary paths, while the dashed lines indicate the shared backup paths. In this example, the primary paths S-A-B-D and S-E-F-D each have separate working paths but share a common backup path, S-C-D. If a failure occurs along the primary path S-A-B-D, the backup path S-C-D is activated. However, SBPP follows a key constraint: only one failure can be handled at a time per shared backup resource. This approach segments the spectrum to reduce fragmentation and increase spectrum allocation success rates. This model enhances survivability by sharing backup resources when spectrum sharing is not possible, utilizing methods like Backup Spectrum Reservation with Multi-Path Protection (BSR-MPP) and SBPP. ### <span id="page-4-0"></span>*2.2. Segmented protection and restoration schemes*

A survivability strategy called Segmented protection and restoration scheme as shown in [Fig.](#page-4-2) [5](#page-4-2) is used in elastic optical networks to increase network resilience and guarantee continuous operation in the event of failures. With this plan, the network is divided into smaller segments, each of which has a subset of nodes and the links that connect them. #### <span id="page-5-0"></span>*2.3. Path and span restoration schemes*

In SEON, PR [\[70\]](#page-19-23) and SR [[87\]](#page-20-10) are two distinct techniques used to recover disrupted traffic and maintain network resilience in the event of failures. PR restores the entire communication path or lightpath affected by a failure, ensuring end-to-end service continuity. Since a communication path consists of a sequence of interconnected nodes and links, PR reconstructs the complete route from source to destination.

In contrast, SR focuses on restoring only the failed segment rather than the entire path. A span consists of a single connection or multiple consecutive links between two adjacent nodes. Instead of rerouting the entire traffic flow, SR repairs only the affected segment, making it a faster and more resource-efficient solution for localized failures. Various studies have explored SR techniques to optimize resource allocation. The authors in [[88\]](#page-20-11) investigate different spectrum conversion capabilities to minimize spare capacity requirements and the maximum number of link frequency slots. Additionally, techniques such as Bandwidth Squeezed Restoration (BSR) [[61,](#page-19-14)[89,](#page-20-12)[90](#page-20-13)] enhance restoration efficiency by adapting to the limited frequency slot capacity on each fiber link. Another study [\[91](#page-20-14)] applies SR to CO-OFDM-based EON, leveraging spectrum conversion to further reduce spare capacity needs and maximize traffic restoration. A novel fiber-optic modulation approach proposed in [\[92](#page-20-15)] achieves high spectral efficiency, flexible bandwidth allocation, and resistance to impairments like chromatic dispersion, aligning with the findings in [[91\]](#page-20-14). Moreover, spectrum conversion is highlighted as a key factor in reducing spare capacity redundancy and enhancing spectrum efficiency in flexi-grid EON.

The choice between PR and SR depends on failure type, network topology, traffic patterns, and required restoration speed. PR is more suitable for large-scale failures that impact the entire communication path, ensuring complete service recovery. Conversely, SR is faster and more effective for localized failures, as it repairs only the damaged span without affecting the entire path. In practice, many networks employ a combination of both techniques to provide comprehensive protection and restoration coverage, balancing speed, efficiency, and resource utilization.

## <span id="page-5-1"></span>*2.4. Ring cover and p-Cycle protection schemes*

To enhance network survivability and ensure efficient failure recovery, SEON employ two advanced protection techniques: Ring Cover Protection and p-Cycle Protection [[93\]](#page-20-16). These methods are designed to mitigate failure impacts while maintaining uninterrupted service delivery. Ring Cover Protection involves deploying specialized backup rings alongside primary communication paths, forming circular protection routes that instantly take over in case of failure. In contrast, p-Cycle Protection is a more flexible and resource-efficient approach that precomputes backup paths (p-cycles), allowing for rapid adaptability to different failure scenarios.

Ring Cover Protection can be further classified based on network topology into UPSR and BLSR, both of which optimize spare capacity sharing [\[8\]](#page-18-13). The concept was first introduced in EON in [[95\]](#page-20-17) to reduce overall spectral usage and minimize required protection capacity. A case study in [\[95](#page-20-17)] demonstrates the implementation of Ring Cover Protection in an EON topology, where two pre-deployed rings, (B-F-E-C-B) and (B-A-F-E-C-B), serve as backup paths in case of failures. If a failure occurs on the B-C link, it disrupts operational connections between node pairs B-C and B-D. To restore these flows, the ring (B-F-E-C-B) allocates spectrum slots 3–4 for the B-C flow, while the ring (B-A-F-E-C-B) assigns spectrum slots 5–7 for the B-D flow. Ensuring spectrum continuity is crucial for effective ring based recovery, requiring two pairs of opposite fibers on each link. As illustrated in [Fig.](#page-6-0) [8](#page-6-0), dual opposite fiber pairs (e.g., link D-E) enable the reuse of the same spectral resources on different fibers, allowing both working flows and

On the other hand, p-Cycle Protection technique has been widely studied as a robust mechanism for enhancing network survivability in SEON. The pioneering work in [[87\]](#page-20-10) introduced p-cycles as a means to improve restorable network design and resource utilization, significantly advancing the understanding of pre-configured protection structures.

To illustrate, consider the example shown in [Fig.](#page-6-1) [7](#page-6-1), where multiple p-cycles of varying lengths (from one to five links) can be identified, including M-Q-N-M, M-Q-N-O-P-M, M-N-O-P-M, M-N-O-R-P-M, and P-O-R-P. However, the M-Q-N-O-R-P-M cycle, despite covering all nodes and links, is unsuitable due to the imposed maximum cycle length constraint of five links [\[94](#page-20-18)]. The application of p-cycle protection has been further expanded in [[96\]](#page-20-19), where researchers extended the technique to protect transparent optical express flows in regional networks. This study also compared p-cycle protection with SBPP in terms of capacity efficiency. Other studies [\[97](#page-20-20)[–99](#page-20-21)] explored the Failure Independent Path-Protecting (FIPP) p-cycle algorithm, leveraging heuristic-based techniques to enhance path protection in elastic optical networks. Further improvements were made in [\[86](#page-20-9)], where a multiplexing algorithm was used to optimize backup resource utilization in FIPP p-cycle protection.

Under cloud-ready SEON with SDN control and anycast relocation, stream-based, linear pre-configured protection has been proposed as an alternative to cyclic schemes. The approach in [[100](#page-20-22)] enables relocationaware protection by first transforming the physical graph with a virtual hub connected to all data centers; two link-disjoint paths from the source to the hub are computed and then mapped back to one or two data centers, allowing relocation to a nearer center while keeping BV-OXCs pre-configured along a non-divergent linear overlap of backup routes for rapid switchover. The same work categorizes FIPP p-cycle operation and explains how a stream structure avoids branchselection ambiguities when the primary is on or partly on the cycle, while remaining distinct from PXT trails—which can be infeasible in cloud-ready SEON if a trail traverses multiple data centers.

Complementing this, [\[101\]](#page-20-23) develops fragmentation-aware resourceefficient protection with fast recovery (FRPFR), which merges backup paths into pre-configured streams and selects spectrum gaps using a fragmentation-minimizing contiguous-gap rule under contiguity/ continuity constraints; it also applies a relocation-enabling topology transform and provides polynomial-time bounds for controller deployment. Taken together, these stream-based designs preserve immediate failover while aligning with anycast relocation and fragmentation control, offering a flexible alternative to p-cycle families in cloud-ready SEON settings [\[100](#page-20-22)[,101\]](#page-20-23).

Comparative studies between Ring Cover Protection and p-Cycle Protection have also been conducted [[102](#page-20-24),[103](#page-20-25)], evaluating their effectiveness in EON survivability. To further reduce spectrum utilization and spectrum fragmentation ratio (SFR), researchers in [[104](#page-20-26)] introduced Spectrum Shared pre-configured cycles (SS-p-cycles), allowing multiple p-cycles to share spectral resources along common links. Additionally, [[105](#page-20-27)] proposed a p-cycle-based protection strategy for multicast traffic demands, further broadening its applicability. Recent advancements in genetic algorithms have also influenced EON survivability techniques. In [[106](#page-20-28)], researchers introduced the GPCPS, employing an EGA to address issues such as spectrum fragmentation and redundancy in p-cycle protection systems. In [\[107\]](#page-20-29), three distinct directed p-cycle designs were proposed to minimize power consumption and spectrum utilization in EON. Additionally, [[108](#page-20-30)] introduced two novel link-based p-cycle evaluation methods: individual p-cycle selection and p-cycle set selection, to identify optimal p-cycle configurations for failure-dependent link protection. Other studies have explored energy efficiency and power optimization in p-cycle protection. In [\[109,](#page-20-31)[110](#page-20-32)], the authors established p-cycles as a technique to reduce energy consumption in EON. Meanwhile, in [[111](#page-20-33)], a p-cycle reconfiguration strategy was proposed to dynamically optimize protection structures, improving spectrum efficiency and reducing bandwidth blocking probabilities. Overall, p-cycle protection continues to be a promising failure recovery mechanism in EON, offering highspeed restoration, resource optimization, and adaptability to different network scenarios.

To further assess the effectiveness of Ring Cover Protection and p-Cycle Protection, we conducted a dynamic simulation using the USNET topology, a realistic model for wide-area optical networks. Connection arrivals followed a Poisson process, with exponential holding times spanning 1000 time units. Each connection, requiring 4 contiguous spectrum slots to support 100 Gb/s under BPSK modulation, was assigned a primary path using a shortest-path algorithm, while backup paths were pre-configured through ring cover or p-cycle protection schemes. In our evaluation, the Ring Cover Protection scheme selects the longer segment of the ring, making it less resource-efficient. In contrast, the p-Cycle Protection scheme selects the shorter segment, optimizing backup resource consumption. This technical approach rigorously enforces continuity and contiguity constraints across links, ensuring accurate resource allocation. Our results confirm that p-Cycle Protection is significantly more efficient than Ring Cover Protection, achieving an overall blocking probability reduction of 42% shown in [Fig.](#page-7-5) [9.](#page-7-5) This demonstrates superior network resilience and resource optimization in SEON.

#### <span id="page-7-0"></span>**3. Different failures scenarios**

<span id="page-7-4"></span>Failures in EON significantly threaten service continuity and demand robust survivability mechanisms. These failures manifest in various forms. Link failures disrupt communication routes and require rapid rerouting; node failures cause connectivity loss and potential network congestion; transponder malfunctions degrade signal quality; and wavelength conflicts introduce signal interference that reduces reliability. More severe disruptions such as fiber cuts lead to total transmission loss, while simultaneous failures introduce high complexity, requiring advanced restoration strategies. Additional risks include power outages, optical signal degradation, hardware or software faults, routing errors, and security attacks, all of which can compromise network performance or availability.

To understand the scope and relationship between these failures, [Fig.](#page-8-1) [10](#page-8-1) presents a failure classification tree, categorizing them into single link failures, multi-link failures, and path failures. Single link failures often result from localized issues like hardware faults or isolated node failures. Multi-link failures represent more extensive disruptions, commonly caused by natural disasters or simultaneous equipment failures. Path failures occur when an entire communication path becomes unusable due to cascading link or node disruptions. This hierarchical classification is essential for designing effective and scenario-specific protection and restoration mechanisms within the EON architecture.

Informed by an extensive literature review and domain knowledge, this section provides a detailed analysis of these failures to support the development of resilient mitigation strategies. Key failure scenarios include:

#### <span id="page-7-1"></span>*3.1. Single and dual-link/multi-link failure scenarios*

Link failures in SEON pose significant challenges to network resilience and uninterrupted service delivery. A link failure occurs when a physical fiber link between two nodes becomes non-functional, which can result from fiber cuts, device malfunctions, or signal degradation. Such failures disrupt connectivity, reducing the network's ability to maintain operational efficiency. Several studies [[55,](#page-19-10)[62,](#page-19-15)[65](#page-19-18)[,69](#page-19-22)[,93](#page-20-16), [112](#page-20-34)[–116](#page-20-35)] have explored single-link failures, discussing protection and restoration strategies to enhance EON survivability.

In contrast, multi-link (or dual-link) failures are significantly more complex. These failures involve the simultaneous failure of multiple fiber links, affecting multiple communication pathways across the network. Unlike single-link failures, which impact a specific path and trigger localized rerouting, multi-link failures introduce greater interdependencies among lost links, requiring sophisticated restoration strategies. The key challenge lies in managing the increased complexity, limited available resources, and network-wide disruptions. Researchers [\[117–](#page-20-36) [128](#page-21-0)] have extensively analyzed multi-link failure scenarios, proposing various solutions to mitigate their impact.

To address multi-link failures, advanced protection and resource allocation mechanisms are essential. One approach, introduced in [[126](#page-21-1)], leverages Software-Defined Networking (SDN) to enhance network survivability. The study proposes an Adaptive Survivability (AS) strategy, which optimizes the balance between path protection efficiency and routing costs. Additionally, [\[129](#page-21-2)] introduces a novel Routing, Modulation, and Spectrum Assignment (RMSA) algorithm, optimizing both anycast and unicast traffic flows. The performance of RMSA algorithms and the effectiveness of the AS approach are further evaluated in [\[130\]](#page-21-3), demonstrating their potential in improving SEON resilience under multi-link failure conditions.

### <span id="page-7-2"></span>*3.2. Node failure*

In SEON [\[8\]](#page-18-13), a node failure occurs when a network node–responsible for routing, forwarding, and processing data–malfunctions or becomes non-functional. Such failures disrupt connectivity, impacting all communication paths passing through the affected node. This can lead to service interruptions, degraded network performance, and a poor user experience.

To mitigate the impact of node failures, SEON employ advanced restoration techniques, including traffic rerouting via alternative paths that bypass the failed node. Additionally, backup resources, distributed control mechanisms, and intelligent restoration procedures ensure continuous network operation even in failure scenarios. These mechanisms allow SEON to adapt and recover promptly, maintaining high network availability and reliable service delivery. Unlike single or multi-link failures, node failures are significantly more complex and costly since a single node failure can lead to multiple link or path failures. Due to this complexity, research often focuses more on link failure scenarios, as seen in the previous discussions on single and multi-link failures. However, studies in [\[111,](#page-20-33)[112](#page-20-34)[,128](#page-21-0)[,131,](#page-21-4)[132](#page-21-5)] specifically address node failure scenarios in SEON, analyzing their challenges and proposing solutions to enhance network survivability and resilience.

Beyond link and node failures, another critical failure scenario in SEON is path failure, where an entire communication path becomes non-operational. The next section will explore path failures, their causes, and effective strategies for ensuring survivability in SEON.

#### <span id="page-7-3"></span>*3.3. Path failures*

In SEON [\[8\]](#page-18-13), a path failure occurs when a critical communication route comprising interconnected links and nodes are disrupted due to various causes such as fiber cuts, hardware failures, or unforeseen events. This disruption severs connectivity between the source and destination nodes, affecting services and data flows that rely on the impacted path.

To mitigate path failures, SEON utilize resilient protection and restoration mechanisms, including pre configured backup paths and dynamic rerouting strategies. By promptly detecting failures and adapting to changing network conditions, SEON minimize service disruptions and ensure high network availability. This proactive approach enhances network resilience, reinforcing the system's ability to deliver reliable and continuous connectivity to users. Path failures often arise from link failures [[55](#page-19-10),[62,](#page-19-15)[65,](#page-19-18)[69](#page-19-22)[,93](#page-20-16),[112–](#page-20-34)[116](#page-20-35)], multi-link failures [\[117–](#page-20-36)[128](#page-21-0)], or node failures [\[111,](#page-20-33)[128](#page-21-0),[131](#page-21-4)[,132\]](#page-21-5). Since a path consists of multiple links and nodes, any failure within these components can directly or indirectly impact the entire path. Researchers have also explored additional factors contributing to path failures in studies [[65,](#page-19-18)[70,](#page-19-23)[111](#page-20-33), [127](#page-21-6)[,128,](#page-21-0)[131](#page-21-4)[–139\]](#page-21-7) emphasizing the need for robust path protection and restoration strategies to safeguard network performance.

Beyond path failures, another critical challenge in SEON is hardware failure, which can severely impact network operations. The next section will discuss hardware failures, their implications, and strategies for ensuring network survivability in SEON.

#### <span id="page-9-1"></span>*4.1. Centralized network control and management*

Over the past few decades, Generalized Multi-Protocol Label Switching (GMPLS) [[45,](#page-19-28)[83,](#page-20-6)[84](#page-20-7)[,180,](#page-22-2)[181](#page-22-3)] has become the dominant architectural framework for backbone networks in the telecommunications industry. Its widespread adoption is attributed to flexibility, automatic manageability, fault tolerance, and rapid resiliency, with restoration times of less than 50 ms [\[182\]](#page-22-4). GMPLS has successfully replaced SDH/SONET and ATM, establishing itself as the global standard for Layer 2/3 protocols. Offering long-term compatibility with legacy systems, GMPLS supports label-based resource representation, enabling seamless integration with technologies like Frame Relay and ATM.

However, Software-Defined Networking (SDN) is emerging as a potential successor to GMPLS. While GMPLS has yet to achieve full interoperability between IP networks and optical transport networks (OTN) [[183](#page-22-5)], SDN provides greater flexibility and dynamic network management. With advancements such as Bandwidth-Variable Transponders (BVTs) [[184](#page-22-6)[,185\]](#page-22-7), EON can allocate frequency slots dynamically based on bandwidth demand, overcoming GMPLS's rigid ITU frequency slot allocation [\[146](#page-21-19)].

One of the key challenges in future-proof network architectures is the demand for programmable networks, driven by evolving telecommunications technologies. SDN addresses this need by fully separating the control and data planes, enabling centralized network management that can be physically and logically distributed for unprecedented scalability and flexibility. This has led to the widespread integration of SDN functionalities into modern routers.

The increasing demand for programmable networks in EON has positioned Software-Defined Networking (SDN) as a key enabler of next-generation network architectures. SDN offers centralized control, enhanced flexibility, and dynamic resource management, making it a crucial technology for Software-Defined EON (SD-SEON). To better understand the evolution of SD-SEON, we examine an expanded SDN architecture that integrates control, data, and management planes. The study in [[186](#page-22-8)] proposes a comprehensive SDN-based framework, illustrated in [Fig.](#page-9-4) [12,](#page-9-4) where the Southbound Interface enables communication between the control and data planes, while the Northbound Interface, based on OpenFlow [[15,](#page-18-19)[123,](#page-20-37)[187](#page-22-9)[–189\]](#page-22-10), facilitates interactions between the control and management planes–although standardization remains a challenge. Additionally, the East/Westbound Interface ensures efficient communication between multiple network controllers, enhancing network coordination and scalability [\[190\]](#page-22-11). These architectural advancements underscore SDN's potential to transform SEON, but challenges related to interoperability, security, and resource optimization must be addressed for widespread adoption.

In regard to the SD-SEON, we have identified some difficulties that necessitate resolution :

- The identification, detection, and localization of failures in SD-SEON can be significantly enhanced through SDN's centralized control and management capabilities. Unlike traditional networks, where failure detection is often distributed and reactive, SDN enables real-time monitoring and proactive fault management by maintaining global visibility over all links and nodes. By leveraging this centralized intelligence, SDN can efficiently analyze network status, detect anomalies, and precisely locate failures, allowing for faster recovery and improved network resilience [[191](#page-22-12)].
- For centralized control in SD-SEON, a standardized protocol like OpenFlow is essential for managing and controlling the optical data plane. However, current protocols face limitations in handling the unique requirements of optical networks, such as dynamic spectrum allocation, flexible grid management, and ultralow latency switching. To fully leverage SDN's capabilities in SEON, there is a need for enhanced protocols that support advanced optical functionalities, improved scalability, and seamless interoperability with existing network infrastructures. Future developments should focus on extending OpenFlow or designing new optical control protocols that can efficiently manage elastic spectrum resources, wavelength routing, and fault recovery in SEON.
- SD-EON can rapidly and consistently detect environmental factors that may lead to fiber cuts or physical damage, minimizing data loss and ensuring network resilience. By leveraging centralized monitoring and real-time analytics, SD-EON can identify signal degradations, power fluctuations, or abnormal transmission losses, allowing for immediate failure localization and proactive restoration measures. This capability significantly enhances network survivability, ensuring minimal service disruption in the event of physical infrastructure damage.

### <span id="page-11-0"></span>*4.2. Spectrum management*

<span id="page-11-2"></span>Fragmentation in a SEON refers to the partitioning of the available optical spectrum into discrete and non-contiguous segments. This occurs due to the allocation of wavelength channels with varying sizes to accommodate diverse traffic demands, similar to the fragmentation challenges found in EON. However, in a survivable network, fragmentation introduces additional complexities that affect network resilience, fault recovery, and restoration strategies.

This section provides a detailed overview of fragmentation in SEON, focusing on its causes and implications for network survivability. To ensure efficient protection and restoration mechanisms, it is crucial to address the key sources of fragmentation that impact SEON performance. The following are major contributors to fragmentation in SEON that require careful consideration.

• Service Edge Overlay Networks employ protection and restoration schemes to enhance network resilience against link or node failures. However, these mechanisms often lead to fragmentation, as backup path and segment allocations may not align perfectly with the original traffic demands. This mismatch creates dispersed and underutilized spectrum segments, reducing overall spectral efficiency.

Several researchers have investigated the issue of fragmentation in survivable SEON, proposing diverse strategies to mitigate its adverse effects. Notably, studies such as [\[56](#page-19-11)[–60](#page-19-13)[,145](#page-21-11)[,192,](#page-22-17)[193](#page-22-18)] and [\[24](#page-18-20)] have introduced techniques aimed at optimizing backup resource allocation, thereby improving spectrum utilization while preserving strong protection and restoration capabilities. Addressing fragmentation is crucial not only for enhancing spectral efficiency, but also for reducing blocking probability and ensuring better overall network performance.

Additionally, the work in [[130](#page-21-3)] introduces a novel approach that incorporates modulation-level awareness into traffic splitting, allowing the network to adapt routing and spectrum assignment (RSA) based on real-time signal quality and modulation needs. This technique significantly improves resource utilization and meets QoS constraints. However, it introduces additional control plane complexity due to its reliance on real-time modulation feedback, which may pose scalability challenges in large, dynamic environments. Note that in SEON, QoT and QoS are both essential but serve different purposes. QoT relates to physical-layer performance, ensuring that lightpaths meet acceptable signal quality thresholds such as OSNR and BER. In contrast, QoS refers to network-layer performance, focusing on user-experienced metrics like bandwidth, latency, and reliability. While QoT determines whether a connection can be physically established, QoS ensures that it meets service expectations. Both must be considered together when designing effective survivability strategies in SEONs.

• The variability of traffic demands in a survivable network can be significant, often leading to fragmentation [\[194\]](#page-22-19). This occurs when backup resources are allocated for worst-case failure scenarios but remain underutilized during normal network operation. Mitigating fragmentation in survivable networks is a major challenge for network operators and designers, requiring a balance between ensuring network robustness and optimizing resource utilization.

To effectively address spectrum fragmentation in SEON, a range of advanced strategies can be utilized, including segment-based protection and restoration, dynamic resource allocation, and intelligent backup resource reservation. These approaches help localize protection efforts to critical network areas, minimizing inefficiencies during normal operations while enhancing responsiveness during failures. A recent development in this domain is the application of quantum computing to spectrum management. In particular, the work presented in [\[195\]](#page-22-20) explores a quantum computing based solution for RSA in flexi-grid optical networks. This method formulates RSA as a quantum optimization problem, significantly reducing path computation time and enhancing scalability for future high-speed network demands. While the benefits are promising–especially for real-time, large-scale routing– challenges remain, notably the current limitations of quantum hardware and the scalability of quantum circuits in practical network environments.

By incorporating these fragmentation-aware strategies, SEONs can achieve better survivability, spectral efficiency, and blocking probability performance, ensuring optimized functionality even under dynamic traffic conditions.

With the increasing complexity of modern optical networks, AI has emerged as a transformative tool for enhancing survivability, traffic prediction, resource optimization, and real-time fault recovery in SEON. The following section explores the integration of AI techniques into SEON architectures and highlights their role in intelligent, adaptive network management.

# <span id="page-12-0"></span>*4.4. Physical layer impairments*

PLI [\[216–](#page-22-41)[226](#page-23-0)] in SEON refer to a range of optical signal degradations that occur during light signal transmission across the network. These impairments include dispersion, interference, noise, and nonlinear effects, all of which accumulate over distance and degrade the QoT [[219](#page-23-1)]. Such impairments can significantly impact the reliability and efficiency of optical communication systems, leading to higher BER, reduced spectral efficiency, and increased signal distortions.

In SEON, effectively managing and mitigating PLI is essential for ensuring the network's resilience, failure recovery, and service availability. Several prevalent PLI types have been identified in the literature, each presenting unique challenges to network survivability. Addressing these impairments requires further investigation, particularly considering physical layer constraints, to enhance the performance, robustness, and efficiency of EON. The next section explores key PLI challenges and potential strategies for their mitigation.

- In SEON, it is common for working and protective lightpaths to traverse different physical links, often resulting in varying distances and PLIs. These discrepancies can lead to differences in OSNR [\[227–](#page-23-2)[230](#page-23-3)] between the primary and backup routes, affecting signal quality and overall network performance.
- Balancing network survivability and signal quality is crucial in SEON. Route selection, protection scheme design, and PLI mitigation strategies must be carefully optimized to ensure that backup lightpaths maintain the required QoT, minimizing degradation during failure recovery. Network operators and designers must consider these factors to uphold the desired levels of performance, reliability, and resilience in SEON deployments.
- The regeneration of optical signals [\[8,](#page-18-13)[231](#page-23-4)[–233\]](#page-23-5) in long-haul or high capacity links poses significant challenges due to its complexity and resource requirements. Signal regeneration is crucial for preserving signal integrity and ensuring reliable data transmission over long distances, as optical signals degrade due to attenuation, dispersion, and nonlinear effects.
- In long-distance and high-capacity optical networks, strategic placement of regeneration sites, efficient resource allocation, and adaptability to dynamic network conditions are essential for maintaining QoT and network reliability. By optimizing regeneration points and integrating flexible signal processing techniques, network operators can enhance SEON survivability, ensuring consistent service quality across extended transmission distances.
- While PLI are primarily managed to ensure signal transmission quality, they can also be exploited for eavesdropping and security breaches in optical communication networks. Unintended signal leakage, interference, and nonlinear effects may inadvertently expose sensitive information, making networks vulnerable to malicious attacks.
  - Managing PLI-related security risks in Survivable EON is a complex and multifaceted challenge. To strengthen optical network security, a comprehensive approach integrating technological safeguards, security policies, and proactive monitoring is essential. Network operators must continuously assess and enhance security protocols to mitigate emerging threats and vulnerabilities, ensuring robust protection against both accidental signal leaks and deliberate attacks.

Effectively addressing PLI in SEON requires a holistic strategy that integrates advanced network planning, continuous monitoring, adaptive management techniques, and seamless coordination across network layers and equipment vendors. As SEON evolve to accommodate diverse and dynamic traffic demands, the efficient management of PLI will remain a critical factor in ensuring network reliability, security, and performance. By implementing proactive mitigation strategies, optimizing resource allocation, and fostering interoperability among network components, operators can enhance SEON survivability and efficiency in the face of growing technological challenges.

#### <span id="page-14-1"></span>**5. Implementation and simulation results**

<span id="page-14-2"></span>Survivability, particularly against single-link failures, remains a critical challenge in SEON, as these failures occur more frequently than multi-link failures and can cause severe service disruptions [[6](#page-18-11)[,70](#page-19-23)[,75](#page-20-1), [129](#page-21-2)[,175,](#page-22-1)[197](#page-22-22),[198](#page-22-23)[,211,](#page-22-35)[247,](#page-23-12)[248](#page-23-13)]. To address this, we propose a novel protection scheme termed State-Aware Symmetrical Hybrid Protection (SHP). This approach integrates symmetrical traffic splitting and a hybrid protection mechanism grounded in the principles of SBPP. Unlike DPP, which allocates a separate backup path for each primary path (as discussed in Section [2.1\)](#page-3-2), SBPP enables multiple primary paths to share a common backup path, thereby significantly reducing resource overhead and enhancing spectrum efficiency.

Several studies have explored traffic demand splitting to enhance both network survivability and spectrum utilization. For example, [[249](#page-23-14)] proposed the State-Aware STD and ASTD models, which improved connection acceptance rates but did not consider failure scenarios. Similarly, the MMRSA model in [[33\]](#page-19-29) incorporated traffic splitting but lacked a strong survivability framework. Other works, such as [[12,](#page-18-16)[31](#page-19-30)], introduced PDPPS, which relies on DPP and thus misses the efficiency benefits offered by SBPP.

Our main motivation is to show that optimal resource utilization can be achieved by symmetrically splitting traffic demands, particularly when no single path can handle the entire demand. This approach also supports the spectrum management goals discussed in Section [4.2](#page-11-2). Furthermore, we demonstrate that multiple traffic demands can be effectively protected against single-link failures without incurring additional protection overhead, highlighting the strength of SBPP based strategies as a scalable and resource efficient solution for improving SEON survivability.

This work extends the framework proposed in [[249](#page-23-14)] by presenting a Hybrid Protection Scheme that integrates SBPP with a Vulnerability Scoring System (VSS). The aim is to improve survivability, maximize spectral efficiency and enable failure aware traffic engineering in SEON. Traditional SBPP enables several primary paths to share one backup path, reducing redundancy and saving resources. However, standard SBPP does not explicitly incorporate link reliability, historical outage frequency or congestion levels, which may lead to the selection of unstable or highly loaded backup paths. Such shortcomings increase blocking probability and reduce the effectiveness of recovery mechanisms. To mitigate these issues, we introduce VSS, which dynamically evaluates each link's vulnerability by accounting for slot utilization, past failure behavior and physical distance. The link score is computed as

$$V_{uv} = w_u \cdot \frac{U_{uv}}{U_{\text{max}}} + w_f \cdot \frac{F_{uv}}{F_{\text{max}}} + w_d \cdot \frac{l_{uv}}{L_{\text{max}}}, \tag{1}$$

where is the number of occupied slots on link (*,* ), normalized by the maximum slot capacity max. The variable denotes the historical failure count for link (*,* ), normalized by the largest observed value in the network, max. The distance of each link, , is normalized by the longest link length in the topology, max. The coefficients *, ,*  are tunable parameters constrained by + + = 1, ensuring proportional influence of utilization, reliability, and physical length. Since all three terms are bounded within [0*,* 1], the final score also lies within this interval, allowing straightforward comparison across heterogeneous links.

The weight assignment ( *, ,*  ) = (0*.*5*,* 0*.*3*,* 0*.*2) reflects the relative impact of the three factors. Utilization carries the largest weight (50%) as it captures instantaneous network stress and congestion, allowing quick adaptation to traffic fluctuations. Historical failures contribute 30%, ensuring that unreliable links are penalized without disproportionately biasing against single past incidents. Distance is weighted at 20% to account for physical fragility and signal degradation without overshadowing the dynamic factors. The sum of weights is always unity, which normalizes the final score and prevents dominance by any single metric. Although we adopt a 50:30:20 split as a balanced compromise, the model is flexible: operators may increase the failure weight when reliability is paramount, or emphasize utilization for higher spectrum efficiency. In this way, VSS remains adaptable to diverse operational priorities.

Each bidirectional link in our SEON is provisioned with = 320 spectrum slots, giving max = 320. Failures are normalized by the maximum recorded outage across all links, max, and distances by the longest fiber span, max. Thus, the per-link VSS is given by

$$V_{uv} = 0.5 \frac{U_{uv}}{U_{\text{max}}} + 0.3 \frac{F_{uv}}{F_{\text{max}}} + 0.2 \frac{l_{uv}}{L_{\text{max}}},$$

and the vulnerability of a path is computed as the average over its constituent links:

$$V_{\text{path}} = \frac{1}{|p|} \sum_{(u,v) \in p} V_{uv}.$$

Lower path scores indicate more reliable routes, guiding both primary and backup selection. By integrating SBPP with VSS in this manner, the proposed scheme proactively avoids fragile or congested links, dynamically adapts backup assignments, and ensures higher acceptance ratios with reduced blocking probability compared to conventional approaches.

The Hybrid Protection process begins by computing multiple candidate routes between a source and destination using the -shortest path algorithm. Because -shortest paths alone do not guarantee disjointness, we subsequently apply a link-disjoint selection procedure based on a maximum flow approach. From this, several link-disjoint primary routes and one backup route are identified. Each candidate path is ranked according to its average vulnerability score derived from VSS and the lowest-scoring primary paths are chosen for traffic allocation. The backup is then selected from the remaining disjoint options, where remaining refers to those feasible candidate paths not selected as primaries but still disjoint from them. To prioritize links, each link cost in the -shortest path computation is defined by its vulnerability score, so that paths traversing more reliable links achieve lower costs. In this way, both primary and backup paths are selected according to vulnerability-aware metrics, ensuring that they are disjoint while also minimizing overall failure risk. Traffic is symmetrically splitted evenly across the selected primaries for balanced load sharing. Symmetrical distribution ensures that the offered demand is evenly partitioned across the selected primary paths, preventing overload on any single route. While all used paths occupy slots, asymmetrical allocation can cause one path to saturate quickly, increasing blocking probability and spectrum fragmentation, while other paths remain underutilized. By keeping the slot occupancy uniform, symmetry delays congestion, improves load balancing and provides a fairer and more resilient utilization of network resources.

The shared backup path is periodically evaluated using the VSS. If it becomes unreliable due to congestion (insufficient free spectrum on any of its links) or if its constituent links exhibit high vulnerability scores reflecting repeated historical failures, it is dynamically reassigned to a more reliable, link-disjoint candidate. In case of a failure event, affected flows are rerouted over the pre-allocated shared backup path, ensuring continuity of service. During normal operation, the backup path does not carry traffic; however, its spectrum is prereserved along the selected route following the SBPP model. When risk or contiguity tests fail or when the set of protected primaries changes, the controller reassigns the prereserved backup to a lower-risk, linkdisjoint route and updates the amount of prereserved capacity on its links; these updates occur at admission/teardown events or periodic VSS checks, not at failover. Under the single link failure assumption, this pre-reservation guarantees instantaneous failover while allowing multiple primaries to share the same backup capacity. Consequently, the reservation overhead is lower than in DPP not because reservation is deferred (which would amount to restoration), but because SBPP reserves only the capacity required to withstand one failure at a time. In words, on each backup link we reserve an amount equal to the largest per-connection slot requirement among the primaries that share that backup, i.e., we take the maximum requirement, not the sum so any single primary failure can be absorbed while still enabling efficient sharing. If, during the admission of a new request, the candidate shared backup path lacks the required number of contiguous free slots on one or more links, we trigger a global re-optimization.

![](_page_15_Figure_9.jpeg)

**Fig. 15.** PDPPS [\[55](#page-19-10)].

<span id="page-15-0"></span>This process repacks existing allocations to free contiguous spectrum while preserving full protection for all already admitted connections. In our simulator, this is implemented by releasing and replaying current allocations to obtain a more compact packing; operationally, this corresponds to make-before-break reconfiguration without degrading service. If re-optimization cannot create sufficient contiguous capacity, the new request is blocked; existing flows remain fully backed up. During re-optimization, vulnerability scores are recomputed to reflect the updated network state. This differs from PDPPS, where traffic is simultaneously partitioned across all disjoint paths. In contrast, our model uses only the selected primaries for active transmission, while the backup remains idle until a failure occurs, as illustrated in [Figs.](#page-15-0) [15](#page-15-0) and [16](#page-16-0).

We compare our proposed model with PDPPS since it is one of the most widely studied protection schemes in SEON. While traditional methods like DPP provide strong protection, they suffer from high resource overhead due to idle backup paths. In PDPPS, the total available spectrum on each link is partitioned into separate working and protection pools. During normal operation, working traffic occupies only the designated partition, leaving the protection partition idle but pre-reserved. Upon a single-link failure, backup paths are activated by utilizing the reserved protection partition, but instead of allocating the full demand, PDPPS employs a squeezed restoration strategy where only a fraction of the original bandwidth is guaranteed, while the remaining portion may be dropped or degraded. This partitioning mechanism enhances spectrum utilization compared to pure DPP by avoiding complete duplication of resources; however, it also introduces significant challenges. First, the rigid partitioning of spectrum can lead to fragmentation and under utilization under dynamic traffic conditions. Second, service quality may be degraded during failure scenarios since affected connections receive only partial restoration. Finally, the reliance on static partition sizes makes PDPPS less adaptive to heterogeneous demand patterns and evolving network states.

In the PDPPS [\[55](#page-19-10)] setup shown in [Fig.](#page-15-0) [15,](#page-15-0) we pre-establish three link-disjoint lightpaths from S to D. Each path carries 60 Gb/s, so in normal operation the network provides 180 Gb/s. The offered traffic is 150 Gb/s, spread across the three paths, leaving about 30 Gb/s of headroom. Because the paths are disjoint, a single link failure can disable at most one path. If any one path fails, the two surviving paths still carry 60 Gb/s each (120 Gb/s total), so service continues without a reset. The drawback is squeezing: the missing 30 Gb/s during the outage is not served, so part of the traffic is lost as shown in the literature [\[250\]](#page-23-15).

In contrast, our proposed State-aware SHP model eliminates these inefficiencies by dynamically allocating resources based on demand size, path distance and real-time link vulnerability. Unlike PDPPS, which reserves idle capacity upfront, State-aware SHP leverages SBPP

![](_page_16_Figure_2.jpeg)

Fig. 16. State-aware SHP.

<span id="page-16-0"></span>so that a single backup path can simultaneously protect multiple primary paths, thereby minimizing spectrum duplication. Furthermore, by integrating the VSS, State-aware SHP model intelligently avoids risk-prone links, ensuring higher survivability. This adaptive, state-aware design enables proposed model to achieve higher connection acceptance rates, lower blocking probability and improved spectrum utilization, while maintaining stronger service guarantees than PDPPS under both normal and failure conditions.

Note that, although the proposed model may distribute traffic across multiple paths, splitting applies only to the working traffic on link-disjoint primaries and is invoked only when no single low-risk path with the required contiguous slots is available. Protection capacity is prereserved once along a single shared backup path. This design avoids PDPPS rigid spectrum partitions and its squeezed restoration under failure. The reduction in resource duplication results from shared reservation on the backup, not from deferring reservation. Our contribution is to pair SBPP with VSS guided path ranking, symmetrical splitting across disjoint primaries when necessary and an admission-time reoptimization step tailored to SEON contiguity constraints, thereby minimizing unnecessary splitting overhead (e.g., additional guard bands, coordination, and differential-delay compensation).

As an illustrative example, the State-aware SHP model shown in Fig. 16 handles a traffic demand of 100 Gbps by symmetrically splitting it into two primary paths of disconnected links–P1: 1-2-3-4 and P2: 1-5-6-4—each carrying 50 Gbps. In the event of a failure (e.g., 2–3 link in P1), the affected traffic is immediately rerouted through the shared backup path B1: 1-7-8-4, ensuring uninterrupted service.

These model is designed to protect traffic against single link failures by accepting a connection only if both primary and shared backup paths can be successfully established. If the setup of the primary path fails, the backup path is not attempted [251–256]. This selective mechanism offers a moderate level of protection [257], reflecting a non-joint SBPP approach [258], where primary and backup paths are computed independently. This decoupled strategy simplifies the operational complexity while still maintaining a reasonable trade-off between protection and resource utilization.

In the event of a single-link failure affecting any of the primary paths, the impacted traffic is immediately rerouted to the pre-reserved shared backup path. This fast restoration process eliminates the need for real time path computation, thereby reducing downtime. To maintain long term efficiency and adaptability, the system performs periodic re-optimization. This process involves recalculating all vulnerability scores based on updated network metrics, defragmenting links where necessary and reassigning both primary and backup paths to maintain optimal operating conditions.

By addressing these scenarios, the proposed model provide a robust framework that strengthens network resilience and ensures efficient recovery in the face of failures, while optimizing spectrum use through traffic splitting, and shared-backup protection. #### <span id="page-18-0"></span>**6. Conclusion**

This study presents a comprehensive survey of SEON, analyzing a wide spectrum of strategies and technologies designed to enhance network survivability. The techniques reviewed – including Dedicated and Shared Protection Schemes, Path and SR, Segment-based Strategies, and Hybrid Models – have been carefully selected through an in-depth literature review and current domain expertise. A detailed investigation into failure scenarios, such as single-link, multi-link, node, and hardware failures, highlights the need for adaptive and intelligent survivability mechanisms.

We have also identified critical future challenges and research directions, such as centralized network control, spectrum fragmentation management, AI/ML-driven network intelligence, PLI, hardware adaptability, and disaster resilience. Beyond current methodologies, this paper underscores the growing significance of BVTs, traffic splitting models, and real-time decision-making algorithms in optimizing spectrum utilization, fault recovery, and service continuity.

Importantly, as SEON continues to evolve, it is influenced by hardware and software advancements that are shaping the future of optical networking. Technologies such as SDM, holographic optical communications, and quantum computing-based RSA are emerging as transformative solutions that promise higher scalability, spectral efficiency, and fault tolerance. These innovations have the potential to radically expand the capabilities of SEON, enabling it to meet the ever-growing demands of next-generation applications and critical infrastructure.

In conclusion, this study not only consolidates the state of survivability in SEON but also lays the groundwork for future innovation. By bridging foundational techniques with cutting-edge research trends, we aim to inspire the development of resilient, intelligent, and adaptive SEON architectures that are better equipped to serve the increasingly dynamic and data-driven needs of our society.
