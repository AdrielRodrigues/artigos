# Key Concepts

Reference notes synthesizing the core concepts needed to read this collection of papers on machine learning for optical networks — spanning intelligent network resource allocation, failure/QoT management, short-reach system equalization, hollow-core fiber transport, and accelerated RL research tooling.

## Reconfigurable and Elastic Optical Networks

### Reconfigurable Optical Networks (RONs)
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks | gen: a7496cd5478b -->
Reconfigurable optical networks adapt topology in data centers and capacity in wide-area networks (WANs) to observed demand, replacing the historical practice of statically over-provisioning redundant fiber and DWDM channels for worst-case traffic surges. The motivation is the growth of traffic from AI, machine learning, and data-centric applications, which favors optical transport for its bandwidth and energy efficiency. Two architectural models dominate: IP-over-OTN (ITU-T G.709), in which hosts connect to routers that ride an optical transport network built from optical cross-connects (OXCs) carrying wavelengths ("lambdas") as circuits, and hybrid electric-optical data center fabrics in which host-to-host bandwidth changes periodically through optical circuit switching. In data centers the emphasis is on changing the physical topology between Top-of-Rack switches, while in metro and WAN settings the emphasis is on adapting capacity (via bandwidth-variable transponders) and steering light paths over fixed fiber edges. Scope is limited to enterprise and core networks; last-mile passive optical networks and mobile front-haul are excluded. A clustering analysis of networking journals shows a divide between physical-layer optical research and higher-layer networked-systems research, and the field is framed as bridging hardware (WSS, ROADMs, transponders, silicon photonics) with the algorithms and systems that control them. Open issues include standardized models of reconfiguration cost, approximation algorithms for NP-hard topology problems, and whether centralized or decentralized control is preferable.

### Elastic Optical Networks (Flex-grid)
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks, a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks, deep_reinforcement_learning_for_infrastructure_as_a_service_over_flexible_optical_networks, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint, resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach, survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 807d81ace13b -->
Elastic optical networks (EONs), also called flex-grid networks, drop the rigid fixed-grid frequency spacing of traditional WDM networks (50 GHz or 100 GHz channels) in favor of channel widths and center frequencies that are multiples of smaller slots, typically 6.25 GHz and 12.5 GHz. Fixed-grid WDM wastes spectrum because every demand receives a full channel regardless of its size. EONs, commonly built on Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM), allocate variable-width frequency slots according to traffic demand and modulation format, which improves spectral efficiency, and they pair with bandwidth-variable transponders that choose modulation format and baud rate per connection. In a 12.5 GHz-slot EON, slots are grouped to form channels of varying capacity, and the classic resource allocation problem is Routing, Modulation Level and Spectrum Allocation (RMLSA). Each connection must obey two constraints on its Frequency Slot Units (FSUs): continuity (the same FSUs on every link of the route) and contiguity (adjacent FSUs within the block). The spectrum continuity constraint is also the setting in which survivability must be designed, since backup and restoration resources must satisfy it as well. The number of FSUs a request needs follows from its bitrate and the most efficient modulation format (from BPSK up to 16-QAM in some studies) that meets the quality-of-transmission requirement at the path length, plus one FSU as a guard band. These constraints make embedding hard, including for virtual network requests. A common benchmark substrate is NSFNET (14 nodes, 21 links) with 100 FSUs per link, where each virtual link of a request may need only a few FSUs (e.g., 2 to 4); RSA and RMSA studies under dynamic traffic also use COST239, USNET and JPN48. Dynamic traffic is typically modeled as a Poisson arrival process with exponentially distributed holding times, with bitrates such as 25–100 Gbps; spectrum may also be extended to the full C-band (320 FSUs). The cost of this flexibility is spectrum fragmentation: as connections of differing widths are set up and torn down, scattered unusable slivers of spectrum appear and block new requests, which makes routing and spectrum assignment harder than in fixed-grid networks. The elasticity also shapes how much headroom allocation policies leave: relative to a defragmentation-based lower bound on blocking, the best heuristic's supported traffic at 0.1% blocking could be raised by 19–36% in elastic (variable-width) request scenarios, versus only 1–8% when requests have fixed width. Realizing flex-grid in the node requires flex-grid-capable ROADMs (CDC-F) and wavelength selective switches able to filter non-uniform passbands. Together with SDN control, EONs allow data centers and WANs to move from static provisioning to demand-responsive infrastructure, and EON-specific problems such as embedding Virtual Network Function service chains using ranking heuristics, or Virtual Optical Network Embedding for IaaS, are studied on top of them. Because a single failure in such a network can disrupt many high-capacity lightpaths, survivability (protection and restoration of lightpaths) is a distinct line of research on top of RSA and centralized control. To raise capacity further, EONs are extended with Spatial Division Multiplexing (SDM) over multi-core fibers (SDM-EON, or MCF-EON); this adds a core-choice dimension to the allocation problem, turning RMLSA into Routing, Modulation, Spectrum and Core Allocation (RMSCA) and introducing inter-core crosstalk as the main additional physical impairment.

### Optical Circuit Switching in Data Centers
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks | gen: 6bc58dd7a4b3 -->
To avoid the cost of massive packet-switched fabrics, reconfigurable data centers use optical circuit switching (OCS) so that bandwidth between Top-of-Rack switches can change periodically, either on a fixed deterministic schedule or in a demand-aware way driven by connectivity requests. Designs differ by fabric (hybrid electric-optical versus all-optical) and control plane (demand-aware versus oblivious): hybrid designs handle short-lived flows well because the packet-switched part absorbs them, whereas all-optical oblivious designs such as Sirius achieve nanosecond reconfiguration but can struggle with skewed traffic. Free-space optics (FSO) propagates light through air using Digital Micromirror Devices and ceiling-mounted mirrors ("disco-balls"), reducing cabling but remaining exposed to atmospheric attenuation and physical misalignment. Short distances permit microsecond or even nanosecond switching, but exploiting this requires adjusting TCP buffer sizes and host behavior to avoid throughput drops. Cost models comparing against Packet Switched Only (PSO) networks indicate that optical circuits can reduce rack-to-rack traffic by up to 50% relative to static topologies. The main remaining hurdle is scaling demand-aware control planes to match the speed of the underlying hardware.

*Fontes: [A Survey of Reconfigurable Optical Networks](md/a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks_summary.md)*

### Demand-Aware vs. Oblivious Reconfiguration Algorithms
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks | gen: 362d48262d92 -->
Algorithms for configuring optical data center topologies fall into several families. Matching-based approaches use maximum-weight or b-matching to pick circuits that maximize throughput and reduce latency for the current demand. Oblivious approaches such as RotorNet (and the Sirius design) cycle through a fixed set of matchings, providing periodic any-to-any connectivity without computing real-time demand, at the price of inefficiency under skewed traffic. Traffic matrix scheduling computes schedules from snapshots of demand, often using Birkhoff-von Neumann decomposition to express the matrix as a sequence of permutation (circuit) configurations. Self-adjusting data structures, inspired by splay trees and Huffman coding, make local and rapid topology changes that balance reconfiguration cost against routing benefit. Machine-learning methods, for example neural-network traffic-driven topology adaptation as in xWeaver, learn to adapt the topology. Further aspects include edge-coloring to avoid signal interference in shared media and optical splitters for multicast. Many of the underlying topology problems are NP-hard, and constant-factor approximation algorithms, standardized reconfiguration-cost models, and online predictive routing are identified as open problems.

*Fontes: [A Survey of Reconfigurable Optical Networks](md/a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks_summary.md)*

### Cross-Layer IP/Optical WAN Traffic Engineering
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks | gen: 01620e1b8797 -->
In WANs, fiber is extremely expensive, so reconfigurability focuses on shifting bandwidth across fixed fiber edges rather than changing physical topology. Joint optimization of the IP and optical layers has been shown to reduce transponder requirements by 40–60%. Algorithmic work includes shortest-lightpath routing and Integer Linear Programs for SLRG-diverse routes that provide fault tolerance. For bulk transfers, OWAN uses simulated annealing for sub-second wavelength allocation, and DaRTree uses Steiner Tree heuristics for deadline-constrained multicast. OptFlow introduces "fake links" in the IP layer so that standard traffic engineering tools can optimize dynamic optical capacities without redesigning the TE system. Deep learning has been applied to circuit activation. Notable systems include RADWAN (rate-adaptive transceivers, about 40% throughput gain), CORONET (Bandwidth-on-Demand), and Iris, which reduces complexity by switching at the fiber-strand level and so avoids frequent amplifier reconfiguration. Physical impairments must be managed across layers: chromatic dispersion, where wavelengths travel at different speeds and broaden pulses, must be actively compensated in DWDM systems, and ASE noise requires adjusting amplifier gain when channels are added or removed to maintain SNR, for which Case-Based Reasoning has been used to optimize settings quickly. Effective WAN reconfigurability thus requires intense cross-layer coordination to meet performance metrics while respecting physical constraints.

### Software Defined Networking for Optical Networks
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks, a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: cc43378cc51e -->
SDN decouples the control and data planes, enabling centralized management of optical paths and allowing the physical layer to be adapted in a demand-aware manner, with better utilization of bandwidth and enforcement of latency policies. Software Defined Optical Networking (SDON) provides the programmability that makes ML-driven reconfiguration practical, and together with streaming telemetry and high-fidelity monitoring devices (advanced DSP, OTDR) it is one of the enabling technologies for ML in optical networks. In the resulting architecture, an intelligent module made of functional elements collects and preprocesses raw data from the physical network and feeds ML agents that act on the controller. Combined with elastic optical networks, SDN supports the shift from static provisioning to flexible infrastructure in both data centers and WANs. The industry has been moving from Generalized Multi-Protocol Label Switching (GMPLS) distributed control toward SDN, whose global visibility enables proactive fault management and survivability mechanisms. Southbound management in metro/WAN settings relies on protocols such as SNMP and NETCONF, and on a 3-way handshake (3WHS) for reserving paths over an out-of-band optical supervisory channel. Open questions include whether centralized or decentralized control planes are more effective in future deployments, the need for standardized white-box system stacks (e.g., OpenConfig), and automation of power coordination across long-haul amplifiers. Standardized protocols beyond OpenFlow that can handle elastic spectrum resources are also needed, and the integration of blockchain has been proposed for decentralized security and tamper-resistant logging. ML and DL, notably for routing optimization in SDN and for failure management, serve as the intelligence layer on top of this centralized control, automating the configuration of optical networks from telemetry gathered by the controller; for example, logistic regression can map traffic matrices to pre-computed optimal routing sets for real-time SDN configuration.

## Optical Switching and Transmission Hardware

### Wavelength Selective Switches (WSS)
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks, a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks | gen: b4cfe357678e -->
Wavelength selective switches steer individual wavelengths between ports and are the building block for creating optical circuits. Three technologies are compared. Polymer waveguide devices, built around Array Waveguide Gratings (AWG), are low-cost, and the use of fluorocarbons has reduced signal loss, enabling switching times below 820 ps. Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) use tiny mirrors to reflect light; they are slower than polymer waveguides because the mirrors must physically move, but offer lower insertion loss and higher flexibility. Liquid crystal on silicon (LCOS) uses diffraction gratings and voltage-controlled pixels to steer light with no moving parts, giving a modular design with response times of roughly 10–100 µs. The trade-offs are therefore speed (polymer fastest), insertion loss and flexibility (MEMS), and modularity without mechanical parts (LCOS). In proposed SDM-ROADM designs, WSS stages sit between an SDM demultiplexer and multiplexer: the first WSS performs fine-grained switching to redirect circuits among the single-core fibers, and the second feeds the multiplexer that recombines them into an output multi-core fiber, potentially shifting a circuit to a different core. Photonic-lantern multiplexers (PLM) can ease the transition between bundles of single-core fibers and MCFs. Mitigating signal loss at WSS modules, for example through silicon photonics, remains an open hardware challenge.

### ROADMs (C/CD/CDC/CDC-F)
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks, a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks, hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks, survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 2c07d3b38bf2 -->
Reconfigurable Optical Add-Drop Multiplexers (ROADMs) let operators add or drop wavelengths at a node without electronic conversion, and variants are classified by added capabilities. A C-ROADM (colorless) is independent of specific light frequencies at its add/drop ports. A CD-ROADM adds directionless operation, allowing a wave to be routed in any direction, though it can suffer port contention. A CDC-ROADM (contentionless) uses shared add/drop ports to eliminate that contention. CDC-F adds flex-grid support, handling non-uniform grid alignment as required by elastic optical networks. Studies analyze the capacity gains of CDC-ROADMs over C-ROADMs, and the main challenge identified is the high cost of CDC-F ROADMs, which silicon photonics and lower-loss WSS modules are expected to ease. For multi-core fiber networks, an SDM-ROADM architecture has been proposed to perform spatial lane changes (switching a circuit between cores at a node): an SDM demultiplexer splits the MCF into single-core fibers, a first wavelength-selective switch redirects circuits, and a second WSS with an SDM multiplexer recombines them into an output MCF, possibly on a different core. These architectures remain largely hypothetical, and precise definitions together with financial and energy cost analyses are lacking in the literature. In QoT models, ROADM insertion losses for add, drop and intermediate nodes enter the lightpath OSNR budget. Interconnecting hollow-core fiber with ROADMs adds connection and splice losses, which is one of the factors limiting HCF competitiveness. From a survivability standpoint, the ROADM is one of the critical hardware components whose malfunction disrupts traffic, together with the WSS, SBVT and EDFA; its dynamic software-controlled routing and CDC characteristics are what make flexible rerouting after failures possible.

### Bandwidth-Variable Transponders
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks, a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks, survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 0afe1e7a0b90 -->
Transponders map bits onto optical symbols using modulation formats such as OOK, QPSK, and QAM, with the achievable bits per symbol determined by the signal-to-noise ratio. Bandwidth Variable Transponders (BVTs) make modulation format and baud rate programmable, trading capacity against reach: for example QPSK for long distances and 16-QAM for short reaches. Sliceable BVTs (S-BVTs) reduce wasted capacity by propagating multiple independent channels simultaneously from one device, supporting several optical flows at rates from 10 Gb/s to 1 Tb/s. In WANs this enables rate-adaptive capacity; RADWAN, which uses rate-adaptive transceivers, reports throughput increases of about 40%. Joint IP/optical optimization that exploits such flexibility has been reported to cut transponder requirements by 40–60%, and regenerator placement models aim to improve service velocity. In survivability, this flexibility enables "bandwidth squeezing": after a failure, BVTs can adjust modulation and bandwidth to the severity of the failure, restoring a reduced rate and avoiding unnecessary resource consumption. S-BVTs are also proposed as the basis of adaptive communication infrastructure in disaster scenarios, where redundancy cannot be pre-allocated for every catastrophic event. ML supports this flexibility: ANNs calibrate fiber nonlinearity models and optimize symbol rate and modulation format selection in bandwidth-variable transceivers, while MFI and QoT estimators provide the SNR-related information needed to choose the format. In network assessments, transceiver modes based on the OpenROADM MSA (800, 600 and 400 Gbps) are defined by required OSNR (ROSNR) thresholds, adjusted for polarization-dependent loss (PDL) and a 1.0 dB system margin. Comparing the lightpath GSNR with these thresholds determines the mode, and hence the number of channels, each link can support. In SuperC+L, HCF Best enables the most 800 Gbps channels, up to 326.

### Silicon Photonics
<!-- sources: a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks | gen: 216e7ac3846b -->
Silicon photonics (SiP) leverages CMOS manufacturing processes to lower the cost of optical components, and is regarded as an accelerator for high-radix, low-cost reconfigurable optics alongside fast WSS, flexible ROADMs, and S-BVTs. Silicon faces challenges such as coupling loss between chip and fiber, and it is not a natural light emitter; integrating Indium Phosphide (InP) lasers on chip has significantly reduced power loss and improved in-line amplification. Open challenges include reducing the cost of CDC-F ROADMs and mitigating signal loss at WSS modules through SiP integration.

*Fontes: [A Survey of Reconfigurable Optical Networks](md/a_survey_of_reconfigurable_optical_networks_summary.md)*

### Optical Amplifiers: EDFA and Hybrid EDFA/Raman
<!-- sources: hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks | gen: b6b2b25b2b99 -->
Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers (EDFAs) are the standard lumped amplifiers in optical line systems, while hybrid EDFA/Raman amplification (HFA) adds distributed Raman gain to improve noise performance. SSMF and PSCF can use both EDFA and HFA. Hollow-core fiber is limited to EDFAs, because the absence of SRS makes Raman amplification non-viable. To exploit HCF's low nonlinearity, EDFAs with high maximum total output power are needed. Four HCF amplifier configurations have been defined with increasing maximum total output power, from 27 to 36 dBm in SuperC, and with correspondingly higher noise figure (NF), reflecting the cost and complexity trade-off of high-power devices. This trade-off between amplifier power and NF is one of the main technological bottlenecks for HCF. In network-level comparisons, a 'Worst' HCF configuration pairs 0.21 dB/km loss with the lowest-power amplifier, and a 'Best' one pairs 0.11 dB/km with the highest-power amplifier. In SuperC, PSCF with hybrid amplification provides the most 800 Gbps channels, with HCF Best slightly better than SSMF with HFA but below PSCF with HFA.

## Advanced Fibers and Physical-Layer Modeling

### Spatial Division Multiplexing and Multi-Core Fibers
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks, machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey, resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach | gen: baef95a272b4 -->
Spatial Division Multiplexing (SDM) raises transmission capacity by carrying multiple spatial channels within a single fiber structure; alternatives include multi-mode fibers and fiber bundles, but multi-core fibers (MCF) are the focus of SDM elastic optical network studies. The motivation is the 'capacity crunch' expected as global Internet traffic grows with high-consumption applications and 5G rollout: elastic optical networks improve spectral efficiency, while MCF adds capacity by providing several transmission cores in one cladding. Their combination, dynamic MCF-EONs, is regarded as necessary for future 6G services such as tactile Internet and high-definition 3D streaming. The core layout determines how much interference each core suffers. In 7-core fibers the cores form a hexagonal array, so the central core has six neighbors and is the most vulnerable, while peripheral cores have three. A 3-core fiber with a triangular arrangement, in which each core neighbors the other two, is used in evaluations of DRL-based resource allocation. In 12-core fibers the cores sit in a ring, each with two neighbors, giving uniform average crosstalk. 19-core fibers offer high density but higher crosstalk incidence and are typically limited to short distances (about 10 km). Trench-assisted MCFs, which add low-index trenches around cores to limit power leakage, reduce crosstalk by roughly 20 dB relative to standard MCFs. Performance also depends on physical parameters such as core pitch (distance between cores), cladding diameter and coating thickness. Using MCF in a network requires nodes that can switch circuits between cores (spatial lane changes), as in proposed SDM-ROADM designs. Open challenges include hardware realization, fibers with higher crosstalk immunity, and economic viability: the cost of SDM components must fall enough for an n-core MCF to be competitive with n separate single-core fibers. For ML-based monitoring, few-mode fiber (FMF) multiplexing introduces mode coupling (MC); ANN classifiers using IQH features identify modulation formats with high accuracy under low MC, but performance drops as MC and chromatic dispersion increase. Existing studies often ignore mode-dependent loss, and SDM (multi-core and few-mode fiber) is seen as requiring new ML algorithms that handle mode coupling and mode-dependent loss for OPM and MFI.

### Inter-Core Crosstalk in Multi-Core Fibers
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks, resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach | gen: 11d0fb5b8ee3 -->
Inter-core crosstalk is the main physical impairment in multi-core fibers: power leaks between adjacent cores at Phase-Matching Points (PMP), influenced by fiber curvature and torsion. It is modeled statistically from coupling coefficients, bending radius, core pitch and fiber length. In simulators for triangular and hexagonal core arrangements, an approximation formula gives the mean crosstalk affecting a connection from the coupling coefficients and link length, and an XT calculator applies it to each candidate allocation. When crosstalk exceeds a threshold, the signal is effectively turned into noise and the circuit becomes unusable, so the request is rejected. Thresholds depend on modulation format: simulations with the ONS simulator, relating modulation level to transmission reach, give about -22.75 dB for BPSK down to about -37.81 dB for 64QAM, since high-order formats with shorter reach tolerate far less interference; these results indicate that earlier literature often overestimates the thresholds. Crosstalk therefore has to be learned alongside spectrum availability by DRL agents: a route, core and slot block that is free may still fail because intercore XT would exceed the threshold of the selected modulation format, and rejections due to XT can be distinguished from those due to spectrum shortage or optical reach when designing rewards. The crosstalk value also depends on the number n of neighboring cores considered interferers. A static-n model assumes the worst case (e.g., n = 6 for a central core of a 7-core fiber), which simplifies evaluation but may overestimate interference. A dynamic-n model counts only neighbors carrying active circuits in the same slot index, giving a more precise estimate. On a USA topology, dynamic n without reassessing already established circuits yields the lowest blocking ratio, whereas dynamic n with crosstalk reassessment performs worst, because new circuits can push previously allocated high-efficiency modulations above their strict thresholds and cause them to be blocked. Only about 30% of surveyed RMSCA proposals are explicitly crosstalk-aware.

### Hollow-Core Fiber (HCF)
<!-- sources: hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks | gen: 05b267a3ebe1 -->
Hollow-core fibers guide light in a near-vacuum air core rather than in silica, and are proposed as an alternative to standard single-mode fiber (SSMF) and pure-silica-core fiber (PSCF). Their physical advantages are attenuation potentially below 0.11 dB/km, a wider low-loss spectral region that supports ultra-wide band (UWB) transmission, approximately 33% lower latency, negligible nonlinearity and zero stimulated Raman scattering (SRS), and a large effective area (417 µm²) with a much lower nonlinear coefficient than silica fibers. Deployment challenges include the need for optical amplifiers with high maximum total output power, penalties from inter-modal interference (IMI), which becomes significant above about -60 dB/km, and higher connection and splice losses when interfacing HCF with conventional components such as ROADMs and EDFAs. Evaluations sweep attenuation from 0.11 to 0.31 dB/km, splice loss from 0.05 to 0.5 dB per 2 km, and IMI between -45 and -60 dB/km to identify which combinations of fiber properties and amplifier specifications make HCF competitive. Results indicate that HCF's main value lies in wideband SuperC+L transmission, where its immunity to SRS gives an edge over both SSMF and PSCF. It is already competitive against standard SSMF/EDFA setups, but outperforming high-end PSCF with hybrid amplification would require lower attenuation through scalable manufacturing and low-noise, high-power amplifiers.

### Inter-Modal Interference (IMI) in Hollow-Core Fibers
<!-- sources: hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks | gen: 3bfce07b5e4b -->
Inter-modal interference arises in hollow-core fibers from coupling between the fundamental mode and higher-order modes, and it adds a third noise term, the IMI power, to the generalized SNR of an HCF link alongside ASE and nonlinear interference. It is expressed in dB/km and becomes significant when it exceeds about -60 dB/km; studies model it between -45.0 and -60.0 dB/km. Parity curves in the (attenuation, IMI) space show where HCF matches SSMF. For SuperC transmission, HCF generally requires IMI of at most -50 dB/km to beat SSMF. In SuperC+L, HCF remains competitive even with IMI of -45 dB/km, because the lack of SRS outweighs the IMI penalty. IMI therefore interacts with attenuation, splice loss and amplifier power in determining whether HCF is competitive against silica fibers.

### Stimulated Raman Scattering and Ultra-Wideband Transmission
<!-- sources: hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks | gen: 08a097e9b947 -->
Stimulated Raman Scattering transfers power from shorter to longer wavelengths along a fiber, tilting the power profile across the band. In silica fibers this effect grows with the occupied bandwidth, so it becomes a main capacity-limiting impairment in wideband SuperC+L systems. SSMF and PSCF have peak Raman gain coefficients of 0.38 and 0.22 1/(W·km), respectively, and launch power is therefore optimized with a pre-tilted profile to counteract SRS. Hollow-core fiber has its SRS effect set to zero, which is the source of its advantage in wideband transmission: in a single 80 km span, HCF outperforms SSMF in 59% of cases for SuperC with EDFA, and the advantage increases significantly in SuperC+L because SRS severely penalizes SSMF. HCF also stays competitive in SuperC+L even with IMI as high as -45 dB/km. The same absence of SRS has a cost, since Raman amplification is not viable in HCF, which restricts it to EDFAs. In the SuperC band, by contrast, HCF never outperforms PSCF with hybrid amplification, given PSCF's lower attenuation and favorable Raman gain properties.

*Fontes: [Hollow-Core Fiber Properties and System-Level Specifications for Next-Generation Optical Transport Networks](md/hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks_summary.md)*

### GSNR-Based QoT Modeling and Launch Power Optimization
<!-- sources: hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks | gen: b725b829efad -->
Quality of Transmission of a lightpath can be measured with the generalized signal-to-noise ratio (GSNR), which aggregates ASE noise and nonlinear interference (NLI) powers, with NLI accumulated incoherently across spans, together with OSNR contributions from transmitters and ROADMs (with specific insertion losses for add, drop and intermediate nodes). For hollow-core fiber the GSNR includes a third term, the inter-modal interference power. Reference studies focus on SuperC (6.1 THz) and SuperL (5.5 THz) bands, using 120 Gbaud signals on a 150 GHz grid. For silica fibers, launch power is optimized with a pre-tilted profile that counteracts SRS. For HCF, launch power is constrained mainly by the maximum output power of the amplifier rather than by nonlinearity. Using ΔGSNR heatmaps for a single 80 km span, HCF performance is comparable to SSMF with hybrid amplification in SuperC, and shows a distinct advantage in the combined SuperC+L scenario, where strong SRS degrades wideband silica systems.

## Short-Reach Systems and Equalization

### Passive Optical Networks (PON) and Short-Reach IMDD Systems
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: b3574c41cb76 -->
Short-reach optical transmission, typically up to about 100 km, underpins inter-data-center links, local area networks and industrial automation, where high bandwidth and low latency are required. Passive Optical Networks (PONs) are a cost-effective access solution built on passive splitters, which avoid powered intermediate equipment but introduce power losses and signal impairments such as chromatic dispersion (CD) and fiber nonlinearity. Coherent systems deliver high capacity through complex modulation formats and DSP, but Intensity-Modulated Direct-Detected (IMDD) systems are preferred in short-reach scenarios because of their simplicity and low cost; the trade-off is that IMDD is directly exposed to fiber impairments, since it lacks the phase and polarization information that coherent receivers use for compensation. Integrating machine learning into the receiver DSP is proposed as a way for these links to adapt dynamically to stochastic phenomena and nonlinearities without costly hardware upgrades. ML applications in short-reach systems fall into five tasks: bandwidth request and prediction, subcarrier allocation, power budget limitations (predicting energy consumption, constrained by the lack of public datasets), equalization, and fault detection. Practical deployment, particularly at Optical Network Units (ONUs), depends on balancing model performance against hardware complexity.

*Fontes: [Machine Learning in Short-Reach Optical Systems: A Comprehensive Survey](md/machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey_summary.md)*

### DSP Equalization Techniques for Optical Links
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: 9a428064ea18 -->
Conventional equalizers are the baselines against which ML equalizers are compared. Zero Forcing is a linear equalizer that minimizes inter-symbol interference (ISI) but suffers from noise enhancement when the channel frequency response has deep valleys. The Feed-Forward Equalizer (FFE) processes the signal without feedback and is valued for simplicity and stability. The Decision-Feedback Equalizer (DFE) reduces ISI by subtracting previously detected symbols, at the risk of error propagation when incorrect decisions are fed back. The Viterbi equalizer uses a trellis and dynamic programming to find the most likely transmitted symbol sequence, with complexity O(T·N^2) for sequence length T and N states. The Volterra equalizer is a nonlinear equalizer that applies Volterra kernels in stages to compensate high-order nonlinear distortions arising from the fiber channel and transmitter bandwidth limitations. Adaptive filtering iteratively modifies filter parameters to track time-varying channels, typically with complexity O(t) per update. Linear methods therefore struggle with nonlinear impairments, while the nonlinear ones (Viterbi, Volterra) grow costly, which motivates learned equalizers.

*Fontes: [Machine Learning in Short-Reach Optical Systems: A Comprehensive Survey](md/machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey_summary.md)*

### Sequential and Time-Series Neural Architectures for Equalization
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: 441192180918 -->
Equalization of short-reach links is treated as a sequence-modeling problem, and the architectures fall into traditional sequential, temporal-convolutional, Transformer-based and Fourier-domain families. Traditional baselines are RNNs, which handle variable-length sequences but suffer from exploding and vanishing gradients; LSTMs, which add a cell state and input, forget and output gates to retain long-term dependencies; GRUs, a simplified variant with only update and reset gates, useful against CD and nonlinearities in high-speed coherent systems; and CNNs, which use weight sharing and convolutional kernels to extract temporal features, including Inception-style blocks with multiple kernel sizes and 1x1 convolutions for dimensionality reduction. The motivation for more advanced models is the distortion mix in PAM-based short-reach systems: chromatic dispersion (a linear transformation attenuating high frequencies in the time domain), jitter (sampling-time fluctuations producing high-frequency amplitude perturbations) and chirp (frequency varying over time). Because these effects alter the time and frequency domains concurrently, operations restricted to a single domain cannot remove them. Temporal convolutional networks are proposed as hardware-efficient alternatives to LSTMs: FC-SCINet uses series decomposition to separate low- and high-frequency components and SCIBlocks that learn interactively over odd-even sampled sub-sequences, enlarging the receptive field; DLinear decomposes the input into trend and seasonal parts with moving-average kernels followed by a linear combination, giving very low complexity; and LightTS uses interval sampling (periodic patterns) and continuous sampling (temporal continuity) processed by MLP-based Information Exchange Blocks. Transformers use scaled dot-product attention over Queries, Keys and Values to capture global dependencies, but vanilla attention is quadratic in sequence length; Reformer's LSH attention hashes similar items together to cut complexity, and Autoformer replaces the encoder with series decomposition and an auto-correlation mechanism to handle trend and seasonality. Fourier-domain models exploit energy compaction, the bijective nature of the transform and O(n log n) complexity: TimesNet reshapes 1D signals into 2D tensors according to dominant frequencies to capture intra- and inter-period variation using Inception networks, and FreTS is an MLP network applying nonlinear transformations directly in the frequency domain via a Frequency Temporal Learner.

*Fontes: [Machine Learning in Short-Reach Optical Systems: A Comprehensive Survey](md/machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey_summary.md)*

### Machine Learning for Nonlinearity Compensation in Optical Fiber
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: a4026ac2f8c2 -->
ML and DL equalizers are used to mitigate Kerr nonlinearity, nonlinear phase noise (NLPN), laser phase noise, modulator imperfections and I/Q imbalance in coherent fiber links. Support vector machines improve M-ary detection (e.g., 16-QAM) over conventional detectors and handle NLPN and laser phase noise. ANNs perform nonlinear equalization (NLE): RBF networks suit linear data and MLPs nonlinear data, and both raise the Q-factor relative to Volterra filters. kNN detectors address non-Gaussian impairments such as ASE noise, with distance weighting improving linewidth and nonlinearity tolerance. Lasso regression in spatial division multiplexing (SDM) selects the significant perturbation terms for Kerr compensation at low computational cost. Clustering approaches include hierarchical and fuzzy-logic C-means (the latter generally better at optimum launch power for BPSK/QPSK), K-means with time windowing for Nyquist-WDM tracking and for quantization in radio-over-fiber, expectation maximization (EM) for laser amplitude/phase noise, and independent component analysis (ICA) for blind polarization demultiplexing and PMD mitigation without training symbols. Deep models extend this: DNNs compensate linear and nonlinear effects, predict Raman gain profiles with >99% accuracy and enable end-to-end transmitter/receiver optimization with constellation shaping; LSTM/GRU recurrent networks capture temporal dependencies, cutting complexity versus ANNs at similar BER, with transfer learning adapting them to new operating conditions; CNNs in orbital angular momentum (OAM) systems predict mode coefficients and equalize nonlinearity, outperforming Volterra filters by up to 3 dB; and deep reinforcement learning searches for Volterra-based NLE structures that lower complexity without losing performance. In short-reach links, shallow DL models have been shown to work as effective post-equalizers for both IMDD and coherent signals. Centralized pre-equalization at the transmitter (e.g., at the OLT in a PON) is an alternative that moves compensation away from the receiver and can reduce the computational burden on the ONU.

### Model Compression for Edge Optical Receivers
<!-- sources: machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey | gen: 4fdb5b433007 -->
Deploying large ML models on single GPUs or edge devices such as ONUs is constrained by memory, compute and latency, and two compression strategies are discussed for optical equalizers. Knowledge distillation transfers knowledge from a complex teacher model to a compact student model. In optical systems it is suggested as a means of parallelizing the inherently sequential computation of RNNs, making their behavior compatible with high-speed hardware, and it can exploit privileged information (extra information available only at training time) to improve predictions when datasets are small. Vector quantization (VQ) reduces computational load by representing data with a small set of prototype vectors forming a codebook. It is identified as an effective way to compress the essential information of RNNs or CNNs, enabling faster inference in high-speed networks. Both techniques address the gap between the performance of deep equalizers and the hardware feasibility of running them at line rate in resource-limited receivers.

### Optical Wireless Communication (FSO/VLC) with ML
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: 2b908afef49d -->
Optical wireless communication (OWC), comprising free-space optics (FSO) and visible light communication (VLC), uses ML/DL to handle atmospheric turbulence, ambient light interference and positioning. SVMs improve signal detection in VLC/FSO and mitigate residual phase offset after CMA equalization. ANNs perform wavefront correction against FSO turbulence and filter fluorescent-light interference in VLC, achieving error-free transmission at high rates. kNN gives robust VLC indoor positioning with much lower location error than trilateration, and kernel ridge regression with sigmoid preprocessing improves visible light positioning accuracy over linear models. Ensemble methods (RF/XGBoost) predict RSSI to enable seamless FSO-to-RF switching and identify objects from CSI in VLC links. Hierarchical clustering supports robust routing in FSO MANETs, while K-means helps user identification and phase retrieval. EM clustering gives near-perfect channel estimation in FSO/VLC, often removing the need for pilots (blind detection), and ICA separates users in multi-user FSO links. Deep models include low-complexity DNN detectors for FSO that need no CSI and compensate nonlinearities in underwater VLC; LSTM/GRU networks that predict FSO channel behavior from weather data and mitigate ISI in camera-based VLC; and CNNs for OAM mode demodulation under strong turbulence, wavefront correction and ambient light filtering. In FSO systems based on orbital angular momentum (OAM), CNNs are also used to monitor atmospheric turbulence severity and detect OAM modes, providing feedback for transmitter correction. RL and DRL optimize VLP height updates, power allocation in SDM-FSO, resource management in hybrid LiFi-WiFi networks, relay selection in cooperative FSO, hybrid RF/VLC power allocation and beamforming policies that prevent eavesdropping. Closely related, modulation format identification in hybrid radio-over-fiber (RoF) networks uses ANNs with AAH features or auto-encoders with preprocessing to classify RF formats over fiber, with performance degrading beyond about 70 km because of chromatic dispersion; RoF monitoring must account for both electrical RF noise and optical fiber impairments. Open issues include extending FSO monitoring to pointing errors and signal scattering alongside turbulence, and addressing hybrid channel complexities such as RF noise and fading in RoFSO and VLC networks.

## Machine Learning Foundations for Optical Networks

### Machine Learning Paradigms for Optical Networks
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: 44d2f456bb73 -->
ML use cases in optical networks fall into three paradigms: regression (predicting continuous values, e.g., monitoring and traffic or QoT prediction), classification (assigning data to discrete categories, e.g., failure identification) and decision-making (learning strategies through environmental interaction, e.g., resource allocation, typically with reinforcement learning). In performance monitoring, OPM generally calls for regressors for continuous impairment parameters, while modulation format identification calls for classifiers for discrete formats; multi-task learning can combine both. Adoption is motivated by the use of large historical datasets, which improves robustness to noise compared with Bayesian or heuristic methods; the decoupling of expensive offline training from fast online execution; and reduced feature engineering, since deep learning extracts features automatically from raw data such as eye diagrams. Compared with likelihood-based and hand-crafted feature-based classical approaches, ML offers real-time adaptability through online learning, flexibility across operating conditions without prior channel knowledge, the ability to detect security breaches as changes in physical-layer parameters, and better handling of non-linear behavior than closed-form formulas. Supervised learning maps inputs to targets using labeled data. k-Nearest Neighbor (k-NN) is nonparametric and predicts by majority vote of the closest known samples, but is storage-intensive for real-time use. Support Vector Machines (SVMs) classify by finding a maximal-margin hyperplane, with kernel functions for non-linearly separable data; they are accurate but their meta-parameters are hard to tune. Artificial/deep neural networks use layers of neurons with nonlinear activations and backpropagation to minimize a loss such as MSE; variants include the multi-layer perceptron and Probabilistic Neural Networks (PNN), which approach Bayes-optimal solutions via radial basis functions, while deep networks model complex non-linearities but need large datasets. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) use convolution and pooling layers to detect local patterns, which suits grid-like data such as eye diagrams and constellation images. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) handle sequential data, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units use gates to mitigate vanishing gradients and capture long-term dependencies. Decision trees make hierarchical binary decisions but tend to overfit; random forests are ensembles that vote across trees to increase accuracy and reduce overfitting. Unsupervised learning finds patterns in unlabeled data: K-means groups data into K clusters by minimizing the Euclidean distance to centroids, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) reduces dimensionality by projecting data onto eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, maximizing retained variance. In reinforcement learning, an agent learns a policy π by trial and error to maximize expected discounted return; Q-learning updates action-value (Q) functions without requiring state-transition probabilities. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) replaces Q-tables with deep networks (e.g., CNNs or RNNs) to handle the very large state and action spaces of real optical network configurations. Although RL is widely used for routing and resource allocation, it has not yet been significantly applied to OPM and MFI. Algorithm choice depends on the trade-off between accuracy, dataset size and computational complexity, e.g., CNNs for images and LSTMs for sequences.

### Unsupervised Learning and Dimensionality Reduction for Optical Signals
<!-- sources: machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: 79f14cd4e381 -->
Unsupervised learning extracts structure from unlabeled data through clustering and dimensionality reduction (DR), which makes it attractive for MFI and OPM where labeled data are costly or training should be avoided. Clustering families include partition-based methods such as k-means, which iteratively update centroids; distribution-based methods such as Gaussian Mixture Models fitted with Expectation-Maximization (EM) or Variational Bayesian EM (VBEM); and density-based methods such as DBSCAN and OPTICS, which separate core points from outliers by local density, with CFSFDP as an updated variant that detects density peaks. These are applied to constellation or Stokes-space points to identify modulation formats without training, including in OFDM systems. DR serves as preprocessing to avoid the curse of dimensionality and to ease visualization. Linear techniques include Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which finds directions of maximum variance, and Independent Component Analysis (ICA), which extracts linearly independent components. Non-linear techniques include Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) and Stochastic Proximity Embedding (SPE), which preserve pairwise distances; auto-encoders, which use an ANN to compress data into a hidden layer; and t-SNE, which projects high-dimensional data onto t-distributions to preserve both local and global structure. In practice PCA reduces ADTS diagrams for joint OPM, auto-encoders preprocess signals for radio-over-fiber MFI, and clustering underpins non-training-based identification. Unsupervised clustering and deep learning are considered to have room for growth relative to the dominance of supervised learning and ANNs in this area.

*Fontes: [Machine Learning Techniques for Optical Performance Monitoring and Modulation Format Identification: A Survey](md/machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey_summary.md)*

### Multi-Task Learning for Joint OPM and MFI
<!-- sources: machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: 2244c8066cb4 -->
Optical performance monitoring is naturally a regression problem (OSNR, CD, DGD, bit rate) while modulation format identification is a classification problem, and conventional single-task techniques require separate monitors for each. Multi-task learning (MTL) lets one model perform both at once, sharing a representation across tasks and reducing hardware and computational cost. In direct detection, PCA applied to ADTS or asynchronous constellation sampling features supports simultaneous identification of bit rate, modulation format and impairments, and MTL-based ANNs use AAH features to classify the format and regress the impairment values in a single network. In coherent detection, amplitude histograms with DNNs or CDF features with SVMs provide joint OSNR estimation and MFI, and cascaded DNNs first identify the format and then estimate the impairment with a format-specific regressor. For OFDM, k-NN regression has been used for joint OSNR/MFI, and CNNs on constellation images jointly identify format and OSNR. Selecting such an algorithm involves trade-offs among accuracy, multitasking capability, acquisition hardware cost, ease of implementation, computational complexity and latency, and the dynamic range of impairments handled. Designing comprehensive features and a single multitasking algorithm that can monitor wide ranges of impairments and identify diverse formats simultaneously is identified as a key open research direction.

## Monitoring and Signal Analysis

### Optical Performance Monitoring and QoT Estimation with ML
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, failure_management_in_optical_networks_with_ml_a_tutorial_on_applications_challenges_and_pitfalls_invited, machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: f05966c2e043 -->
Optical performance monitoring (OPM) and quality-of-transmission (QoT) estimation are among the most common ML applications in optical networks. OPM estimates signal quality parameters such as OSNR, chromatic dispersion (CD), polarization mode dispersion (PMD)/differential group delay (DGD) and polarization dependent loss, so that elastic networks can trade off spectral efficiency, reach and signal quality. Commercial OPM still relies on non-ML tools: optical power meters, optical spectrum analyzers (OSA), dedicated CD/PMD monitors (following ITU-T G.650) and vendor DWDM OSNR monitors; no commercial ML-based OPM product exists. Conventional OSNR monitoring uses out-of-band noise measurement or in-band techniques (polarization nulling, delay interferometers, electrical sampling), CD is monitored via RF tones or clock phase detection, and PMD via eye diagrams or the RF power spectrum, usually limited to a single impairment. ML pipelines follow sampling, feature extraction (e.g., amplitude histograms, CDF), offline training and online estimation, and OPM is generally posed as regression. In direct detection, ANNs and SVMs are trained on eye-diagram features (Zernike moments, Q-factor, jitter). To avoid costly timing recovery, asynchronous sampling is used: asynchronous amplitude histograms (AAH), asynchronous delay-tap sampling (ADTS) and parametric asynchronous eye diagrams (PAED) provide statistical features for estimating OSNR, CD and DGD, and CNNs can be applied directly to ADTS images for joint OSNR/CD monitoring. Direct-detection OPM is the more cost-viable option at intermediate nodes, though asynchronous features are sensitive to CD/PMD. In coherent detection, ANNs use asynchronous constellation features, SVMs use I/Q histograms (IQH), and deep models (DNN, LSTM-RNN) work on raw data without manual feature engineering; transfer learning accelerates training by reusing pre-trained weights. Frequency-domain (RF spectrum) features are effective for OSNR monitoring under high CD, while image-based features give high accuracy at higher computational cost. For lightpath QoT, estimation assesses feasibility before establishment to prevent service disruption and optimize spectrum use. Analytical models typically rely on conservative fixed margins to cover uncertainties such as connector losses; ML-based estimation instead learns the effects of physical-layer impairments from historical data, without needing precise individual parameters, and thereby reduces excessive system margins. A hybrid 'input refinement' approach keeps the traditional analytical model and uses ML only to refine specific uncertain input parameters. Modern DNN and CNN approaches extract features automatically from AAHs or sampled signal vectors, removing the need for manual clock recovery and expert-defined features. PCA reduces the dimensionality of asynchronous delay-tap diagrams, so that impairments are estimated through Euclidean-distance comparison with a reference database at lower computational cost; it also provides feature extraction for joint OPM, spectrum distortion analysis and perimeter intrusion detection. Random Forests and Extreme Learning Machines (ELM) estimate BER and blocking probabilities from path features, while case-based reasoning (CBR) exploits similarity to historical samples in a knowledge base. ANNs and kernelized Bayesian regression map WDM channel ON/OFF states to OSNR and EDFA power excursions, aiding stable power adjustment during defragmentation. SVMs reach up to 99.9% accuracy in QoT estimation and failure-risk prediction, outperforming semi-analytical methods. kNN performs joint modulation format identification (MFI) and OSNR monitoring in OFDM systems with low overhead. Ensemble methods such as random forests balance accuracy and complexity for MFI and QoT estimation and often beat DNNs in real-time implementations. Linear regression improves the accuracy and speed of fiber fault localization compared with traditional reflectometry. Deep models add further capability: DNNs for MFI and OSNR monitoring, LSTM/GRU networks for time-varying OSNR estimation, and CNNs that process constellation images to extract EVM and jointly identify modulation format and OSNR. CNN-based device fingerprinting is also used to authenticate legitimate ONUs in PONs. Current ML-based OPM is mostly limited to linear impairments; monitoring nonlinear effects (FWM, SPM, XPM) is an open direction. Overall, these learned estimators replace computationally heavy analytical models and fixed design margins with faster data-driven inference.

### Modulation Format Identification (MFI)
<!-- sources: machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: 79232b193cfc -->
Modulation format identification (MFI) is the receiver-side classification of the modulation type of an incoming signal, a requirement for adaptive, elastic optical networks in which transmitters change format according to channel conditions (it is less needed in current static networks). Formats are grouped by detection method: intensity modulation–direct detection (OOK in NRZ/RZ, optical duobinary), phase modulation–direct detection (DBPSK, DQPSK) and coherent detection, which recovers amplitude and phase with a local oscillator and supports M-ary PSK, M-QAM and dual-polarization variants. Conventional MFI is split into likelihood-based (LB) methods, which need exact mathematical models, and feature-based (FB) methods, which rely on hand-crafted features and manual thresholds such as normalized power distributions, higher-order cyclic cumulants, amplitude deviation, histogram entropy or nonlinear power transformations in the frequency domain via FFT. ML replaces these rigid rules with data-driven classifiers. In direct detection, AAH features feed ANNs, optionally tuned with genetic algorithms, and higher-order cumulants (HOC) feed decision trees or SVMs in low-OSNR scenarios. In coherent detection, supervised and unsupervised approaches are used: Stokes-space features clustered with GMM, VBEM or CFSFDP, or classified with PNN and CNNs; fractal dimension with SVM for robustness to CD and DGD; intensity-fluctuation features with SVM; random forests with amplitude histograms for M-QAM; and image-processing approaches in which CNNs classify constellation diagrams or Radon transforms combined with SVD achieve high accuracy even at low OSNR. For multiplexed signals, OFDM MFI uses ANNs with amplitude histograms taken at the FFT stage or non-training-based CFSFDP clustering, and modulus mean square (MMS) features allow identification of hybrid formats across subcarriers. In few-mode fiber, ANN classifiers using IQH features are accurate under low mode coupling but degrade as mode coupling and CD increase. In radio-over-fiber links, ANNs with AAH features or auto-encoders classify RF formats, though performance degrades beyond about 70 km because of CD. MFI generally requires coherent receivers for phase recovery and DSP preprocessing, and most impairment mitigation is modulation-independent except carrier phase/frequency offset recovery, which needs prior knowledge of the format. Open issues include multitasking with OPM, and non-standard constellations: probabilistic constellation shaping and geometric shaping may be misidentified by tools designed for standard QAM/PSK.

### Stokes-Space Features for Signal Analysis
<!-- sources: machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey | gen: c16b9e7ab283 -->
Stokes-space analysis maps the received polarization-multiplexed coherent signal onto a three-dimensional space defined by the Stokes parameters. The resulting representation is independent of carrier phase noise, frequency offset and polarization rotation, so it can be used for modulation format identification without carrier recovery or prior polarization demultiplexing, which is a key advantage since conventional carrier phase/frequency offset recovery itself requires knowledge of the format. Each modulation format produces a characteristic cluster pattern in Stokes space (or on a Stokes plane projection), and ML is used to recognize it. Unsupervised approaches cluster the Stokes-space points without labeled training data: Gaussian mixture models fitted with Expectation-Maximization or Variational Bayesian EM (VBEM), and the density-peak algorithm CFSFDP, with the number or structure of clusters indicating the format. Supervised approaches use Probabilistic Neural Networks (PNN) or convolutional neural networks applied to images of the Stokes plane. The main limitation is sensitivity to ASE noise, which blurs the clusters and reduces discrimination at low OSNR. Within the broader set of OPM/MFI features, Stokes-space features are therefore preferred when phase noise and polarization dynamics dominate, whereas asynchronous histogram features suit low-cost direct detection and coherent amplitude histograms or CDFs suit multi-level QAM.

## Traffic Prediction and Service Provisioning

### Optical Traffic Prediction with ML
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: 0f7faf5695bc -->
Traffic and resource-requirement prediction allows optical networks to reconfigure proactively rather than react to congestion. It is framed as a time-series forecasting (regression) problem: a model maps past traffic values over a window w to future values over a horizon s. Non-linear ML methods are preferred over linear statistical models such as ARIMA because network traffic is bursty and self-similar. Regression is favored over classification in flexible-grid networks, since predicting into predefined classes would restrict the forecast and lead to suboptimal resource allocation. Prediction can be made at the level of base stations, hubs/links, or the full Traffic Demand (TD) matrix. Input features include historical traffic values and metadata such as day of week or node identity. Typical preprocessing discretizes time into slots, builds datasets with sliding windows, and scales the data to improve training convergence. Studies use either synthetic traces (Poisson or log-normal) or real traces from networks such as Abilene and GEANT. Several model families are compared. Feedforward NNs and DNNs map features to outputs, with DNNs generally outperforming shallow NNs. Recurrent networks (RNN, LSTM, GRU) handle sequential data: LSTMs solve the vanishing-gradient problem of plain RNNs, and GRUs have lower computational cost. Encoder-decoders enable multi-step-ahead prediction, which helps limit service disruptions during optimization. CNNs/DCNNs capture spatial dependencies and suit TD-matrix and link-load prediction. Graph NNs represent the network as vertices and edges to model spatio-temporal dependencies. Bayesian learning, such as Gaussian process regression, yields probabilistic outputs that express uncertainty rather than only point forecasts. Surveying bit-rate, arrival/holding-time and link-load predictors indicates that RNNs are best for temporal data, while CNNs and GNNs are best for spatial data. Transformers, which parallelize and train faster than RNNs, and Bayesian deep learning are identified as promising further models. In inter-data-center and intra-data-center networks, DNNs are used to predict bandwidth requirements, while LSTM networks specifically address the heavy-tail characteristics of data center traffic by capturing long-term dependencies in sequential data. In hybrid architectures combining Optical Circuit Switching (OCS) and Electrical Packet Switching (EPS), Nonlinear Autoregressive Neural Networks (NARNN) help decide how flows are steered: heavy, long-lived flows are routed through OCS, whereas bursty flows are sent through EPS. In PONs, bandwidth request prediction for dynamic bandwidth allocation (P-DBA) uses historical network data to forecast future bandwidth demand, with k-nearest neighbor, ANN and XGBoost models reducing latency and optimizing allocation for Optical Network Units (ONUs). Prediction thus feeds downstream resource allocation, such as routing and spectrum assignment, and is typically trained offline on historical data and run quickly online. Open challenges include multi-step prediction (most work addresses only single-step-ahead forecasts) and traffic disaggregation, i.e. predicting individual service flows rather than aggregated traffic, which is harder but needed for diverse SLAs. A noted vulnerability is that DNNs offered as 'ML-as-a-Service' can be susceptible to data poisoning from adversarial samples, which could corrupt the predicted demand and the reconfiguration decisions based on it.

### Traffic-Driven Service Provisioning: Reactive, Proactive and Adaptive Networks
<!-- sources: survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: 3825c3cbd080 -->
Global Internet growth and 5G/6G services (IoT, AR/VR) produce strong spatio-temporal traffic fluctuations known as tidal traffic. Static optical networks designed for worst-case demand therefore over-provision resources at high cost. Traffic-driven service provisioning instead reconfigures connections according to real-time demand and network state, and its frameworks are commonly distinguished by how much infrastructure flexibility and automation they assume. Reactive networks use static infrastructure with low automation, and reprovisioning is triggered only when utilization exceeds predefined thresholds. Proactive networks have more dynamic infrastructure under external software control and use ML to make short-term (hours to weeks) resource reservations based on predicted traffic. Their optimization is performed off-line on a multi-period basis, for example hourly: the traffic-demand matrix is predicted, a check against QoS thresholds decides whether reconfiguration is needed, a resource allocation algorithm is run, and the changes are implemented. Typical objectives are minimizing energy consumption by shutting down idle resources or reducing service disruptions. Adaptive networks have fully programmable infrastructure with embedded software control and high automation, and provisioning occurs on the fly through real-time monitoring and continuous adaptation of ML models. Their optimization is on-line at time scales of seconds to minutes, for example using predicted connection arrivals and departures to weight links and guide routing. The technological enablers are software-defined networking, flexible grids and variable bit-rate coherent optics. Moving from reactive to adaptive improves spectrum utilization and lowers energy consumption, but at the price of higher control and management overhead and a greater risk of QoS disruptions caused by frequent reconfigurations. Rule-based algorithms developed for static and dynamic networks often serve as the baseline on which the ML-aided proactive and adaptive frameworks are built.

### Predictive vs. Prescriptive Provisioning
<!-- sources: survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: b501e6ad7a27 -->
ML-aided service provisioning is divided into predictive and prescriptive approaches. Predictive provisioning relies on supervised learning for predictive analytics: a model forecasts future traffic (for example the traffic-demand matrix or connection arrival and holding times), and a separate resource allocation algorithm, such as an optimization or routing heuristic, uses the forecast as input. Prediction and optimization are thus two distinct stages, and there is no feedback from the final QoS outcome to the forecaster. In proactive networks the forecast drives off-line multi-period reservation, where energy savings from shutting down idle resources trade off against blocking probability, elastic spectrum allocation reduces unserved traffic compared with fixed allocation, and alpha-fairness can protect QoS fairness in congestion. Prediction uncertainty margins can mitigate the slight under-provisioning that proactive operation may introduce. In adaptive networks, predicted arrivals and departures weight the links in predictive RSCA, which reduces fragmentation and blocking compared with conventional dynamic provisioning, provided predictions are accurate enough. Prescriptive provisioning uses reinforcement learning for prescriptive analytics: an agent learns a policy directly through trial-and-error interaction with the environment, without labeled data, and its policy yields provisioning or reservation decisions. In proactive networks, agents advise on bandwidth reservation for future intervals and, by observing the effect of their actions on actual traffic, balance over- and under-provisioning better than predictive schemes lacking a feedback loop. In adaptive networks, policies provision requests on-line to minimize blocking probability and spectrum use. RL is often applied only to sub-problems such as routing, while other decisions use rule-based heuristics, and multi-domain settings may need cooperative DRL agents sharing state information. Prescriptive methods generally outperform predictive ones because they optimize the final QoS target rather than matching a traffic forecast. Examples are cooperative RL agents for bandwidth allocation, which manage contention between connections better than SL-based predictions, and DRL frameworks such as DeepRMSA, which beat rule-based heuristics in elastic optical networks. The main hurdle for prescriptive methods is RL convergence speed. Unsupervised learning plays a supporting role in both approaches, for instance clustering traffic trends or similar QoS profiles to guide network slicing.

## Resource Allocation in Optical Networks

### RWA and RSA with ML
<!-- sources: machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks, xlron_accelerated_reinforcement_learning_environments_for_optical_networks | gen: 203d3b2d8ff0 -->
Routing and Wavelength Assignment (RWA) in WDM networks and Routing and Spectrum Assignment (RSA) in elastic optical networks are NP-complete problems, and the joint assignment of multi-dimensional resources (fiber, wavelength, spectrum, modulation format) is computationally costly for iterative optimization. Under dynamic traffic, on-demand requests arrive with random expiry, and three constraints govern allocation: spectrum continuity (the same wavelength or frequency slot unit on every link of the path), spectrum contiguity (adjacent slots for a single request), and no reconfiguration (an established lightpath cannot be moved until it expires). Variants extend the base problems with modulation format selection (RMSA) and core or band selection (RCMSA/RBMSA). ILP gives exact solutions for static traffic but is computationally infeasible for dynamic traffic, whereas heuristics are fast and interpretable. In the supervised approach, DNNs are trained on data generated by Integer Linear Programming (ILP) solutions, so that at run time they output configurations in real time and bypass the cost of solving the ILP; routing is modeled as classification or regression. Related supervised routing work includes Bayesian networks that reduce burst loss in Optical Burst Switching, logistic regression that maps traffic matrices to pre-computed optimal route sets for SDN configuration, and LSTMs that generate inter-domain routes without requiring private intra-domain data. A predictive variant combines traffic forecasting with rule-based allocation: in predictive RSCA for adaptive networks, predicted connection arrivals and departures are used to assign weights to network links, and these weights guide routing heuristics away from future congestion. This reduces spectrum fragmentation and blocking probability compared with conventional dynamic provisioning, as long as predictions are sufficiently accurate. In the reinforcement learning approach, agents learn path and wavelength selection through Q-tables to minimize burst loss, and some models incorporate Quality of Transmission (QoT) constraints such as BER thresholds into the decision. For RSA, deep RL agents use CNNs to extract features from the EON topology and spectrum usage in order to optimize joint Routing, Modulation and Spectrum Assignment (RMSA); DRL frameworks such as DeepRMSA outperform rule-based heuristics in EONs. Multi-agent RL coordinates routing decisions through a central server to avoid link congestion and maximize joint network utility. To reduce complexity, RL is often applied only to a sub-problem such as routing, with the remaining decisions left to heuristics. Supervised methods offer fast inference but depend on the quality of the optimization-generated labels, while RL learns directly from interaction and reward, and once trained can produce near-optimal decisions in sub-second time. A survey of 97 peer-reviewed RL papers for dynamic resource allocation grouped them into RWA, RSA, RMSA and other categories such as defragmentation, survivability and multicast provisioning; DeepRMSA set the de facto problem definition and benchmark for much of this literature. On the tooling side, the JAX-based XLRON package provides configurable, GPU-compatible environments for RSA, RWA and RMSA (alongside virtual network embedding), in which the network state, including slot or wavelength occupancy, topology and precomputed shortest paths, is stored as static-shape arrays. Because the simulator and the learner both run on the accelerator with many parallel environments, RL agents for these routing and spectrum or wavelength assignment problems can be trained at a much larger scale than with CPU-based simulators, with reported speed-ups of 100x to 1000x.

### RMSCA (Routing, Modulation, Spectrum and Core Allocation)
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks, hollow-core_fiber_properties_and_system-level_specifications_for_next-generation_optical_transport_networks, resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach | gen: 44302559586b -->
RMSCA is the resource allocation problem of SDM elastic optical networks, extending RMLSA with core selection. A request is served by selecting a route, choosing a modulation level based on path distance, allocating contiguous spectrum slots, and selecting a core. Two constraints apply: continuity, meaning the same slot range must be free on all links of the route, and contiguity, meaning the slots of one circuit must be adjacent. Each request needs a number of slots set by its bitrate and modulation format, plus one extra slot as a guard band. Violations of efficient packing lead to spectral fragmentation and a higher blocking ratio. A request can also be rejected for reasons other than spectrum shortage: inter-core crosstalk exceeding the threshold of the selected modulation format, or a path longer than the optical reach of that format. Surveyed strategies include priority-based allocation (reserving slot ranges for particular bandwidths or preferring certain cores for high-capacity requests), protection schemes such as failure-independent path protecting (FIPP) and p-cycles, and defragmentation techniques such as push-pull. Most work addresses dynamic traffic with unknown future requests rather than static traffic matrices, the majority of proposals ignore core continuity (allowing core switching), and many are benchmarked only against basic First-Fit/Dijkstra baselines. Rule-based RMSCA heuristics are computationally cheap but depend on expert design. Deep reinforcement learning has also been applied to RMSCA in dynamic MCF-EONs: the agent's action is a triplet (k, c, j) of candidate route, fiber core and contiguous slot block, and its state combines per-core spectrum utilization on candidate routes, source and destination, holding time and bitrate. In a comparison on a USA topology, FF-CASC (First-Fit with a Spectrum Compactness metric) achieved the lowest circuit and bandwidth blocking ratios thanks to sequential allocation and high spectral organization; Intra-Area (bandwidth-based priority areas) performed poorly when requested bandwidths varied widely, since the spectrum was over-partitioned into many small areas; and RF-CASC (Random Fit with CASC) caused the highest external fragmentation, making large contiguous blocks hard to find as load increased. Network-level assessments of fiber types use simpler heuristics: on the Telecom Italia (TIM) topology (44 nodes, 400 spans of 80 km), k-shortest-path routing (k=3) with first-fit spectrum allocation serves random 100/400 G demands. In the SuperC+L scenario at 220 Tbps offered load, HCF Best carries 191.1 Tbps versus 171.7 Tbps for SSMF with EDFA, while in SuperC PSCF with hybrid amplification supports the most 800 Gbps channels.

### RMSCA Heuristic Baselines (KSP First-Fit and Crosstalk-Aware)
<!-- sources: resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach, xlron_accelerated_reinforcement_learning_environments_for_optical_networks | gen: a81cf6c2a3ca -->
Rule-based heuristics are the standard baselines for RMSCA in dynamic MCF-EONs. They are computationally efficient, but they depend on expert design and can miss non-obvious optimal policies, which motivates comparison with learned agents. Three baselines are commonly used. KSP-FF-FCA computes the K shortest paths and allocates the first-fit contiguous slot block on the first core (First-Fit Core Allocation), so it favors low-indexed slots and low-indexed cores. KSP-RF-RCA uses the same K-shortest-path routing but picks the slot block and the core at random (Random Fit, Random Core Allocation), which spreads load without regard to fragmentation. KSP-SCMA is a demand- and crosstalk-aware heuristic: it takes the bitrate demand and the intercore crosstalk of candidate allocations into account when selecting the core and slots, so that a connection is not placed where crosstalk would push it past the threshold of its modulation format. All three use the modulation format that meets the quality-of-transmission requirement, one guard-band slot per request, and the same XT feasibility check as the simulator, rejecting requests that lack spectrum, exceed the XT threshold or exceed the optical reach. In evaluations on NSFNet and COST239 with 3-core fibers, the heuristics were ranked KSP-SCMA best, ahead of KSP-FF-FCA and KSP-RF-RCA, and a trained TRPO agent reduced blocking probability by about a factor of four relative to KSP-SCMA at loads above 2000 Erlang in the C-band (320 FSUs). A DRL agent trained with A2C or TRPO also lowered steady-state blocking against KSP-FF-FCA in earlier training runs. K-shortest-path first-fit (KSP-FF) is also the reference heuristic for single-core RMSA: on NSFNET with 100 FSUs per link at 150 Erlang, a PPO agent trained for 10^7 timesteps (10^4 episodes) in a GPU-accelerated JAX environment matched KSP-FF performance within that training budget, which was used as the marker of a competitive learned policy. Because heuristic baselines of this kind can be weakly tuned, their strength depends on choices such as the number of candidate paths and their ordering.

### Spectral Fragmentation and Defragmentation
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_challenges_of_spatial_division_multiplexing_enabled_elastic_optical_networks, a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, deep_reinforcement_learning_for_infrastructure_as_a_service_over_flexible_optical_networks, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint, survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 334c80a1f427 -->
In elastic optical networks, spectral fragmentation arises when circuits of differing widths are established and released under the continuity constraint (same slots on every link of a route) and the contiguity constraint (adjacent slots for each circuit). Small gaps of free slots remain scattered across the spectrum and are too narrow or misaligned for new requests, increasing the blocking ratio even when total free capacity is sufficient. Allocation policy strongly affects the outcome. Sequential First-Fit combined with a spectrum compactness metric (CASC) keeps the spectrum well organized and gave the lowest blocking among compared algorithms, whereas random placement produced the highest external fragmentation, making large contiguous blocks hard to find as load grew. Priority-based schemes that reserve slot areas per bandwidth can over-partition the spectrum when requested bandwidths vary widely. Utilization heatmaps over links and FSUs make the effect of policy visible in virtual network embedding: first-fit heuristics such as kSP-FF always take the lowest-indexed available FSUs and favor particular links, which concentrates load, fragments the spectrum and congests certain paths. A deep reinforcement learning agent, by contrast, spreads utilization more evenly across both links and FSUs, which accounts for its lower blocking probability. Defragmentation techniques reorganize existing circuits to consolidate free spectrum; push-pull mechanisms reallocate circuits without shutting them down. In SDM-EON, fragmentation interacts with crosstalk, since moving or adding circuits changes which neighboring cores are active in a given slot index. Learning-based approaches have also been applied: DNN-based routing and spectrum assignment in EONs is reported to reduce spectrum fragmentation by up to 20%, and Reward-RMSA modifies the RL reward to account for spectral fragmentation. Defragmentation has also been used as an analytical tool: a resource-prioritized defragmentation algorithm sorts active requests by resource requirement (number of slots × number of hops) and re-allocates them sequentially, relaxing the no-reconfiguration constraint. The resulting omniscient baseline approximates the theoretical limit of network capacity and serves as a lower bound on blocking probability against which heuristics and RL policies can be compared. In survivable EONs, fragmentation is also treated as an open challenge, since protection and restoration resources must be placed in the same fragmented spectrum. Proposed mitigations include modulation-level-aware traffic splitting, the potential use of quantum computing to solve RSA as an optimization problem and so shorten path computation time, and global re-optimization that repacks existing allocations during admission when contiguity constraints would otherwise block a request.

### Blocking Probability Lower Bounds for Dynamic Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint | gen: 2e22915d60a0 -->
Under dynamic traffic, the no-reconfiguration constraint prevents an established lightpath from being moved until it expires, so any online allocation policy, whether heuristic or learned, blocks some requests that a rearrangeable network could accommodate. To estimate how much room for improvement remains, a lower bound on blocking probability can be obtained by relaxing this constraint and allowing defragmentation. In the resource-prioritized defragmentation method, active requests are sorted by resource requirement (number of spectral slots × number of hops) and re-allocated sequentially, which yields an omniscient baseline approximating the theoretical limit of network capacity. Comparing the best-performing heuristic (KSP-FF with K=50 and hop-count ordering) against this bound, measured as the traffic load supported at 0.1% service blocking probability, showed a substantial gap for flex-grid cases: supported traffic could in principle be increased by 19–36%. For fixed-width requests, as in the PtrNet-RSA setting, the gap was smaller, about 1–8%. Because published RL solutions mostly underperform tuned heuristics, this optimality gap is the main argument that further RL research on dynamic resource allocation remains justified, for instance through holistic network objectives or methods that close the gap between heuristics and the bound.

## Virtual Optical Network Embedding

### Virtual Optical Network Embedding (VONE)
<!-- sources: deep_reinforcement_learning_for_infrastructure_as_a_service_over_flexible_optical_networks, xlron_accelerated_reinforcement_learning_environments_for_optical_networks | gen: cc9da58cd3d1 -->
Virtual Optical Network Embedding (VONE) is the resource allocation problem faced by Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) providers when a customer leases an entire virtual network, that is, a topology of virtual nodes and links with specified compute/storage and bandwidth requirements, rather than a single connection. The provider must map the request onto an Elastic Optical Network (EON) substrate by jointly (i) assigning each virtual node to a substrate node with enough compute capacity and (ii) mapping each virtual link to a substrate path with a continuous, contiguous block of Frequency Slot Units (FSUs). The substrate is modeled as an undirected graph of N^s nodes and L^s bidirectional links, each with defined capacities and FSUs, while the request is an undirected graph of N^v nodes and L^v links with FSU and compute demands. Requests are dynamic: they typically arrive following a Poisson process and depart after exponentially distributed holding times, so load is expressed in Erlangs. The main objective is to minimize blocking probability, the fraction of requests that cannot be embedded. Exact approaches such as integer linear programming give optimal solutions but are computationally impractical for large networks or dynamic traffic, so practice relies on hand-crafted heuristics or distributed protocols that decompose the problem into node mapping followed by link (path) mapping. More recent work uses deep reinforcement learning to search the combinatorial solution space, either with separate node and link agents or with a single agent that selects the full embedding. Simulation throughput is a practical obstacle for learning-based VONE because of the size of the state and action spaces. A JAX-based VONE environment, with the substrate state held as static-shape arrays, was benchmarked on NSFNET and CONUS topologies with 100 and 320 FSUs per link. On CPU it was consistently about 10x faster than a Stable-Baselines3 setup with the optical-rl-gym environment, and with 2000 parallel environments on GPU the speed-up exceeded 1000x. In the largest case (CONUS, 320 FSUs per link), one million environment steps took 14 seconds, against 2 hours and 10 minutes for the baseline, a speed-up of about 560x.

### Heuristic Node and Path Mapping for VONE
<!-- sources: deep_reinforcement_learning_for_infrastructure_as_a_service_over_flexible_optical_networks, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint, resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach | gen: 252991d60c79 -->
Heuristics for Virtual Optical Network Embedding (VONE) typically decompose the problem into node mapping and path (link) mapping, and are combined as pairs. Node-mapping heuristics include Consecutiveness-Aware Local Resource Capacity (CaLRC), which ranks substrate nodes by local resource capacity while accounting for the consecutiveness of free spectrum on adjacent links; NSC; and TMR. Path-mapping heuristics include kSP-FF (k-shortest paths with First-Fit spectrum assignment), kSP-FDL, and MSP-EF. Benchmarking all nine node/path combinations, plus a Random Masked baseline, shows that kSP-FF was the best path-mapping heuristic across all node mappings, and CaLRC+kSP-FF was the strongest overall baseline. Such heuristics are fast and simple but myopic: because First-Fit always takes the first available FSUs, they favor specific links and low-indexed slots, causing spectrum fragmentation and congestion on certain paths. Even the best combination was outperformed by a single deep reinforcement learning agent, whose blocking probability was an order of magnitude lower at 40 Erlangs and 15% lower at 100 Erlangs on NSFNET. The same heuristic families serve as baselines for plain RSA/RMSA under dynamic traffic. KSP-FF (select the path first, then the first-fit slots among K shortest paths) and FF-KSP (search first-fit slots across the K paths) are the strongest, with FF-KSP better on larger topologies (USNET, JPN48) and KSP-FF on smaller ones (NSFNET, COST239). Their performance depends on tuning: blocking probability decreases monotonically with the number of candidate paths K and stabilizes around K=50, and ordering candidates by hop count rather than physical length in km significantly reduces blocking, since fewer hops occupy fewer spectral resources. KSP-FF with K=50 and hop ordering is therefore proposed as a robust benchmark, and it matched or exceeded the published results of several RL-based RMSA and RSA methods.

Resource allocation in optical networks is typically formulated as a decision-making problem in which an agent selects actions and updates its policy from a reward signal, learning by trial-and-error interaction to maximize expected discounted return. The problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process defined by states S, actions A, transition dynamics T, reward function R and a discount factor γ, and the goal is a policy π that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward. RL algorithms fall into three families: value-based methods that estimate value functions (for example DQN), policy-based methods that optimize policy parameters directly, and actor-critic methods in which an actor proposes actions and a critic evaluates them. Actor-critic methods are the most popular for high-dimensional optical networks, often using GCNs or RNNs as function approximators to capture spatiotemporal dependencies. Q-learning, a common method, updates action-value (Q) functions without prior knowledge of state-transition probabilities. Policy-based RL enables self-healing routing that recovers from failures or attacks and lowers blocking probability in buffer-less optical burst switching (OBS) networks through deflection routing. Value-based RL optimizes deflection routing in OBS and resource allocation in optical wireless systems, adapting to changing network conditions. In OBS, RL resolves wavelength contention without fiber delay lines or wavelength converters: RLDRS selects deflection routes dynamically, IRLRCR merges proactive routing (RLAR) and reactive deflection (RLDRS) in a single global table, and PQDR adds link 'recovery rates' to predict congestion more accurately. Multi-agent RL (MARL) routing coordinates decisions through a central server to prevent link congestion and maximize joint network utility. Deep RL replaces Q-tables with deep networks (CNNs or RNNs) to handle the large state and action spaces of real configurations: it is applied to routing and spectrum assignment (RSA) in elastic optical networks (EONs), where CNNs extract features from topology and spectrum usage for routing, modulation and spectrum assignment (RMSA), as well as survivable routing and virtual network slicing, and it is commonly reported to reduce blocking probability relative to heuristic methods. Earlier DRL work on EONs covered RMSA in single-domain, multidomain, multiband and survivable networks, whereas work on multicore fiber was limited to fixed-grid networks or to supervised ML used as an auxiliary predictor of traffic or crosstalk rather than as the decision-maker. DNN-based RSA is reported to reduce spectrum fragmentation by up to 20%, and RNNs predict multi-domain routing with about 98% accuracy and forecast future traffic demand to support proactive allocation. DRL is also used to optimize global network performance rather than per-request metrics. Design choices matter: to avoid the curse of dimensionality, states and actions must be abstracted, for example by representing the network at the lightpath level rather than the physical link level, and the reward is the primary learning signal, so inappropriate reward scaling can lead to poor policy convergence. In multi-layer networks, auxiliary graphs simplify the state space and let DRL agents optimize wavelength utilization. Evaluation uses cumulative reward over episodes, training time (convergence speed) and targeted metrics such as blocking probability against rule-based baselines. For RMSCA in dynamic multicore EONs (MCF-EONs), the problem is modeled as an MDP {S, A, T, R, s0, γ}. The state is a 1D array with one-hot encoded source and destination nodes, holding time, bitrate, and, for each route/core combination, block size, first slot index, required FSUs (set by modulation), average available FSUs and total available FSUs. The action is a (route, core, slot block) triplet, the reward is binary (+1 for an accepted request, -1 for a rejection), and the initial state is an empty network. Because the action space is multi-discrete, four Stable Baselines algorithms were compared: the actor-critic methods A2C and ACKTR (the latter using a Kronecker-factored approximation to speed up gradient updates), and the policy-gradient methods PPO2 and TRPO, where TRPO bounds each update with a Kullback–Leibler trust region. On NSFNet and COST239 with 3 cores, 100 FSUs and BPSK to 16-QAM, trained over 160,000 requests, A2C and TRPO were the most effective, reaching rewards near the episode maximum of 50, while PPO2 and ACKTR with default parameters often stalled in local optima or behaved randomly; TRPO improved most monotonically. TRPO converged after roughly 130,000 to 150,000 timesteps regardless of load (500 to 3000 Erlang), with blocking growing linearly with load. Against the heuristics KSP-FF-FCA, KSP-RF-RCA and KSP-SCMA in the C-band (320 FSUs), TRPO cut blocking by about a factor of four relative to the best heuristic (KSP-SCMA) at loads above 2000 Erlang. Agents generalized across traffic loads and spectrum resources but not across topologies: an agent trained on NSFNet performed poorly on COST239 and vice versa. In OFDM-based PONs, subcarrier allocation is formulated as an integer linear programming (ILP) task to optimize spectral efficiency, and Deep Reinforcement Learning is used for dynamic subcarrier sharing to improve throughput and energy efficiency. For Virtual Optical Network Embedding (VONE), earlier DRL work often used multi-agent designs, with separate agents for node and link mapping, because the joint action space was considered too large for one agent; these designs are more complex and can yield sub-optimal solutions because of coordination difficulties between agents. A single agent trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) has instead been shown to handle the joint problem. Its observation comprises the current virtual network request, the FSU status of all links, and the remaining node capacities. Its action space has a node section and a path section of size L^v × k·N^f (k candidate paths, N^f FSUs per link), and multi-step invalid action masking keeps the action space tractable. The reward is deliberately sparse: 0 for a successful allocation and -10 for a failure, avoiding excessive guidance so the agent can discover its own policy. In experiments on NSFNET (14 nodes, 21 links, 100 FSUs per link, 30 compute units per node) with 3-node ring virtual networks, trained for 100 episodes of 10^4 timesteps at 60 Erlangs (γ = 0.8, GAE λ = 0.9), the agent's mean blocking probability was an order of magnitude lower than the best heuristic (CaLRC+kSP-FF) at 40 Erlangs and still 15% lower at 100 Erlangs. Reported gains must be read against benchmark quality, however. A survey of 97 RL-for-dynamic-resource-allocation papers found that many claim 20–30% improvements over heuristics, often against weakly implemented baselines. Re-creating five influential studies under the DeepRMSA problem settings (DeepRMSA, which uses a neural network to pick among K-shortest paths with first-fit allocation; Reward-RMSA, with a fragmentation-aware reward; GCN-RMSA, using graph convolutional networks and RNNs for state features; MaskRSA, with invalid action masking; and PtrNet-RSA, using pointer networks to select nodes without precomputed K-shortest paths) showed that a tuned heuristic (50 shortest paths, first-fit, paths ordered by hop count) matched or exceeded the published RL results in nearly every case, in many instances reducing blocking probability by more than an order of magnitude. The field has accordingly been characterized as having 'hype' from deficient benchmarking, while a measurable gap between the best heuristics and a theoretical blocking bound leaves room for future RL work. Sample efficiency is a separate bottleneck: with CPU-based simulators, training budgets are limited by environment throughput. GPU-accelerated simulators that run thousands of environments in parallel provide a high-data regime in which PPO trained on RMSA over NSFNET (100 FSUs per link, 150 Erlang) for 10^7 timesteps over 10^4 episodes matched the K-shortest-path first-fit (KSP-FF) heuristic within that budget, and it has been argued that such throughput will make it easier to move beyond heuristic-level performance on resource allocation problems. The main open challenges concern convergence and scope. Convergence speed is the primary hurdle, and the huge number of training episodes required can be reduced by seeding agents with supervised learning through imitation learning before switching to RL, by transfer learning and multi-task learning, which allow policies to be carried across network topologies, and by accelerated simulation. Further directions are truly cooperative multi-agent RL (for example cooperative DRL agents sharing state information in multi-domain networks) and moving from simulated environments with synthetic traffic to real, non-stationary deployments, along with security against adversarial manipulation of the traffic data agents observe.

### Invalid Action Masking in Reinforcement Learning
<!-- sources: deep_reinforcement_learning_for_infrastructure_as_a_service_over_flexible_optical_networks, reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint | gen: b778f9ea549b -->
Invalid action masking is a technique for RL problems in which many actions are infeasible in a given state: the logits (or probabilities) of invalid actions are set so that the policy assigns them zero probability, so the agent never samples them and does not need to learn to avoid them through penalties. In optical network embedding this matters because the action space is large and most choices are infeasible at any moment, such as nodes without enough compute capacity, links whose FSUs are already occupied, or FSU blocks that violate the continuity and contiguity constraints. Multi-step invalid action masking extends the idea to requests that require several sequential decisions. After each partial choice, the mask is recomputed recursively, so that resources already allocated earlier within the same virtual network request (nodes already hosting a virtual node, links and FSUs already taken by a previously mapped virtual link) are removed from the remaining options along with resources unavailable in the substrate. This makes it feasible for a single agent, for example one trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to select the complete embedding over an action space with a node section and a path section of size L^v × k·N^f, a size earlier work considered too large and therefore split across separate node and link agents. Because infeasible actions are excluded, the reward can stay minimal (0 on success, -10 on failure) without extra shaping. A "Random Masked" baseline that samples uniformly among unmasked actions is used to separate the effect of masking from that of learning. In RSA, MaskRSA applies masking so that the agent can choose among all available slots on K candidate paths rather than a fixed first-fit slot. When re-benchmarked under the DeepRMSA problem settings, however, MaskRSA's reported blocking probability was matched or beaten by a tuned KSP-FF heuristic (K=50, paths ordered by hop count), indicating that masking alone does not guarantee an advantage over a strong baseline.

### Optical RL-Gym and Simulation Environments for Optical RL
<!-- sources: resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach, xlron_accelerated_reinforcement_learning_environments_for_optical_networks | gen: 28f7534f8279 -->
Optical RL-Gym is a toolkit of OpenAI Gym-style environments for applying reinforcement learning to optical network problems, in which the agent's observation, action and reward are defined on top of a network simulator. It has been extended into DeepRMSCAEnv for the RMSCA problem in dynamic MCF-EONs, composed of a dynamic MCF-EON simulator and a feature engineering module. The simulator holds the network information and utilization (graph topology, capacities, precomputed candidate routes, modulation formats, and the used/available state of every slot in every core), a traffic generator that randomly produces connection establishment and release requests, a request processor, and an XT calculator. The request processor selects the most efficient modulation format that meets the quality-of-transmission requirement, derives the slots needed, checks resource availability and queries the XT calculator for crosstalk feasibility. The feature engineering module has three parts: a reward generator, which gives +1 for an accepted request and -1 for a rejection and knows whether the rejection was caused by spectrum shortage, crosstalk threshold or optical reach; a routes-utilization component, which consolidates slot utilization of the K candidate routes into a 1D vector for the agent; and an experience data generator, which fills an experience buffer with (a_t, s_t, r_t, s_{t+1}) tuples for training. The environment's action space is multi-discrete (route, core, slot block), so agents were trained with the Stable Baselines library, which provides A2C, ACKTR, PPO2 and TRPO. In the reported experiments, agents were trained over 160,000 connection requests on NSFNet and COST239 with 3 cores per link, 100 FSUs, BPSK to 16-QAM and Poisson traffic with exponentially distributed holding times and bitrates of 25–100 Gbps. Episodes of 50 requests gave a maximum reward of 50. Separating the simulator from the feature engineering layer allows the reward design (for example, differentiated penalties per blocking cause) and the state representation to be changed without altering the network model. A limitation of this style of toolkit is its compute profile: the simulators run as sequential CPU code, so training typically requires CPU clusters to generate experience paired with GPU servers for inference and gradient updates, which complicates setup and debugging, and the lack of shared standard environments pushes researchers to reimplement simulators and makes results hard to reproduce and compare. XLRON addresses this by reimplementing both the simulator and the RL training loop in JAX, so that environments run on GPU or TPU hardware. Network state (frequency slot unit occupancy, node resources, service durations, topology and precomputed shortest paths) is held as arrays with static shapes, and the package offers configurable environments for RMSA, routing and spectrum assignment (RSA), routing and wavelength assignment (RWA) and virtual optical network embedding (VONE). It is built with test-driven development and modularity, with over 800 unit tests. Compared with Optical RL-Gym used with Stable-Baselines3 (SB3) on an Apple M1 Pro CPU, XLRON on an Nvidia A100 GPU reported speed-ups of roughly 10x on CPU and 100x to 1000x on GPU, depending on the number of parallel environments.

### JAX-Based GPU-Accelerated Reinforcement Learning
<!-- sources: xlron_accelerated_reinforcement_learning_environments_for_optical_networks | gen: 05bed133601f -->
Conventional RL pipelines for optical networks split the work between CPU-based simulators that generate experience and GPU-based learners that perform inference and updates, which makes training costly, slow and difficult to debug. JAX-based approaches remove this split by implementing both the environment and the training algorithm in JAX, a numerical computing framework with automatic differentiation and Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation through XLA, following RL architectures developed at Google DeepMind. The whole training loop (environment stepping, policy inference, advantage computation and parameter updates) is then compiled into a single program that runs on GPU or TPU hardware without moving data back and forth to the host. This imposes constraints on how the simulator is written. All data must be scalars or arrays whose dimensions are static and known at compile time, so the network state is represented as a series of fixed-shape arrays: link-slot (FSU) occupancy, node resources, service departure times, topology and precomputed shortest paths. Code must follow the functional programming paradigm required by JAX, with pure functions and explicit state passed in and returned rather than mutated in place. Because environments are pure functions, they can be vectorized, so hundreds or thousands of environment instances run in parallel on one accelerator and produce a high-data regime in which the agent sees far more experience per unit of wall-clock time. In the reported benchmarks, PPO on an RMSA task over NSFNET (100 FSUs per link, 150 Erlang) was run for 10^7 timesteps on an Nvidia A100 GPU against Stable-Baselines3 with the optical-rl-gym environment on an Apple M1 Pro CPU. The speed-up exceeded 100x with 250 parallel environments and was about 500x with 2000 parallel environments. For VONE, one million environment steps on CONUS with 320 FSUs per link took 14 seconds, against 2 hours and 10 minutes for the baseline (about 560x), with speed-ups above 1000x possible at 2000 parallel environments and about 10x on CPU alone. JAX's explicit, deterministic pseudo-random number generation, in which keys are split and passed through the program, also makes runs reproducible, and the functional design lends itself to unit testing of the environment logic.

## Failure Management and Survivability

### Optical Network Failure Management (ONFM) with ML
<!-- sources: failure_management_in_optical_networks_with_ml_a_tutorial_on_applications_challenges_and_pitfalls_invited, machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, machine_learning_in_short-reach_optical_systems_a_comprehensive_survey, survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 31776b1f10a6 -->
Optical network failure management (ONFM) is critical for availability across core, metro and access segments, and ML is used to automate what traditionally relies on slow rule-based algorithms and manual intervention. The lifecycle spans failure detection and prediction, identification, localization and magnitude estimation. For detection, ML monitors signal behavior such as BER over time and uses statistics like mean and standard deviation rather than simple threshold crossing, enabling failure prediction (anticipating an event) or early detection (flagging it immediately) so that traffic can be proactively rerouted or modulation downgraded. In PON architectures, where conventional monitoring is limited by the complexity of passive splitter trees, Random Forest, ANN and SVM models are used to monitor BER and predict equipment failures and fiber cuts. One prediction scheme works in two steps: board performance indicators are forecast with Double Exponential Smoothing (DES), and an SVM then classifies whether a failure is imminent. Support Vector Machines, Naive Bayes classifiers and Bayesian networks have likewise been used to predict board failures and to detect mechanical stress on fibers. Once a failure is detected, identification determines the root cause (e.g., fiber bend vs. EDFA malfunction), where overlapping QoT metrics require ML to find hidden discriminating signatures. ANNs combined with Extreme Studentized Deviate (ESD) tests identify abnormal behavior in transport SDN architectures; decision trees and SVMs distinguish 'filter shift' from 'filter tightening' using spectral asymmetry and rounding; and Bayesian networks give the probability of specific failure types from received power and pre-FEC BER. Localization pinpoints where a failure occurred to cut troubleshooting and repair time. Deep Neural Evolution Networks (DNEN) use crossover and mutation to analyze massive alarm sets and localize root causes, avoiding the local optima common in gradient descent, while K-means clustering groups paths by BER trends to help human operators. Magnitude estimation assesses severity (e.g., 1 GHz vs. 5 GHz filter tightening) to decide between a software reconfiguration and a technician visit. On the protection side, deep reinforcement learning has been used to optimize working and protection schemes dynamically in survivable EONs. Research often treats these tasks separately, although cause, location and magnitude interact in real networks. Several design choices must be fixed before data collection: the data type (power, BER, OSNR, alarms), which drives the choice between time-series and tabular methods and the storage and compute needs; monitor placement, a trade-off between deployment cost and model performance; the network domain and technology (WDM, EON, PON); the failure type, distinguishing hard failures (sudden events such as fiber cuts) from soft failures (gradual degradation) and accounting for concurrent failures; decision authority (operator, vendor or collaborative multi-party, with or without a human in the loop); confidentiality; and the time horizon, where sampling frequency should match metric volatility (routing information is stable, OSNR varies often) and retraining and inference frequency trade downtime against compute cost. Tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost) are generally preferred for tabular data, while neural networks excel on time-series or image-like data.

### Failure Scenarios in Optical Networks (Link, Node, Path, Hardware)
<!-- sources: survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: eadb7104892d -->
Failures in survivable elastic optical networks are classified hierarchically into single-link, multi-link, node and path failures, with hardware failures as an underlying cause. Single-link failures result from fiber cuts or device malfunctions. Multi-link (including dual-link) failures involve simultaneous disruption of several fibers, which raises complexity because of the interdependencies among the lost links; mitigation includes Adaptive Survivability (AS) via SDN and optimized Routing, Modulation and Spectrum Assignment (RMSA) for anycast and unicast traffic. Node failures occur when routing or forwarding hardware malfunctions and are more severe than link failures, since one outage disrupts every path crossing the node; recovery requires rerouting over alternatives that completely bypass the failed node. Path failures occur when an entire route becomes non-operational and are generally viewed as the consequence of cascading link or node failures, fiber cuts or hardware malfunctions. Hardware failures affect critical components. The ROADM allows dynamic routing and swapping under software control and is improved by colorless, directionless and contentionless (CDC) features. The wavelength selective switch (WSS) manipulates light signals without physical port separation. The Sliceable Bandwidth Variable Transponder (SBVT) enables adaptive modulation and supports multiple optical flows at rates from 10 Gb/s to 1 Tb/s. The EDFA amplifies optical signals using erbium-doped fiber. Simulations show that hardware degradation, such as thermal stress in amplifiers, strongly affects performance: blocking probability rose from 5.7% at 125 Erlangs to 55.0% at 200 Erlangs. Node failures were found to be the most challenging recovery scenario.

### Survivability in Elastic Optical Networks: Protection and Restoration
<!-- sources: survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 39a16682c45e -->
Survivable elastic optical networks (SEONs) address the ability of flex-grid networks to keep serving traffic after link, node, path or hardware failures. Because a single fiber or node carries many high-capacity lightpaths, failures can disrupt large volumes of traffic, and the spectrum continuity and contiguity constraints of EONs make recovery harder than in fixed-grid WDM networks. Survivability mechanisms fall into two families. Protection pre-allocates backup resources before any failure occurs, which gives fast failover but a higher resource overhead. Restoration calculates recovery routes dynamically after the failure, which uses resources more efficiently but lengthens recovery because paths must be computed in real time. Protection schemes include dedicated path protection and shared backup path protection, segmented protection, and pre-deployed structures such as ring covers and p-cycles. Segmented protection divides the network into smaller segments (subsets of nodes and links) so that a protective lightpath is deployed only for a specific primary segment, localizing the effect of failures; it draws on Segment Routing (SR), Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS) and SDN control via OpenFlow to manage traffic grooming and reduce service blocking. Restoration schemes are divided by scope. Path Restoration (PR) rebuilds the entire end-to-end route from source to destination after a failure. Span Restoration (SR) repairs only the failed segment, either a single connection or consecutive links between nodes, which is faster and cheaper for localized failures. In simulations on the USNET topology, PR achieved about 25% lower blocking probability than span restoration under moderate to high load, because span restoration is limited by congestion on the few alternative local routes. Open challenges in the field include centralized SDN control, spectrum fragmentation, machine-learning-based resource allocation and failure management, physical layer impairments (such as the OSNR difference between primary and backup routes of different lengths), hardware development, and disaster management, where pre-allocating redundancy for every catastrophic scenario is economically impractical.

### Dedicated and Shared Backup Path Protection (DPP, PDPP, SBPP)
<!-- sources: survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 06074e48a609 -->
Dedicated Path Protection (DPP) allocates an exclusive backup lightpath for every connection and exists in three forms. In 1+1 protection, traffic is transmitted simultaneously on the working and backup paths, so recovery is instantaneous. In 1:1 protection, traffic is sent only on the working path and the backup is activated after a failure, which lengthens recovery. In quasi 1+1 protection, critical paths are grouped to optimize spectrum use. Partitioned DPP (PDPP) divides the transmission rate of a demand into multiple link-disjoint paths so that less capacity is wasted on backup resources, though it relies on rigid spectrum partitions and, in restoration, on "squeezed" recovery in which part of the traffic is dropped. Shared Backup Path Protection (SBPP) lets several primary paths share a single backup resource, on the assumption that only one failure occurs at a time, so primaries sharing a backup must be link-disjoint. SBPP is more complex to provision than DPP, since the sharing of spectrum must be tracked and the backup contiguity and continuity constraints still hold, but it is more spectrum-efficient. Simulations on the USNET topology indicate that SBPP reduces blocking probability by approximately 30% compared with DPP.

### p-Cycle and Ring Cover Protection
<!-- sources: survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 24953fedb716 -->
Ring cover protection uses pre-deployed circular backup rings, such as unidirectional path-switched rings (UPSR) or bidirectional line-switched rings (BLSR). To preserve spectrum continuity during recovery, it requires dual opposite fiber pairs. p-Cycle protection instead uses precomputed protection cycles, which offer greater flexibility and resource efficiency than ring covers because a cycle can protect both the links on it and straddling links that have both end nodes on the cycle. Practical designs constrain the maximum cycle length (for example, five links). Advanced variants include Failure Independent Path-Protecting (FIPP) p-cycles, in which the protected paths are end-to-end and the protection does not depend on the failure location, and Spectrum Shared p-cycles (SS-p-cycles), which share spectrum among cycles. In comparative simulations on the USNET topology, p-cycle protection was significantly more efficient than ring cover, reducing blocking probability by 42%.

### Vulnerability-Scored Hybrid Protection with Traffic Splitting
<!-- sources: survivable_elastic_optical_network_a_survey_of_failure_scenarios_and_solutions | gen: 6c7fc76c7bfd -->
State-Aware Symmetrical Hybrid Protection (SHP) is a survivability scheme that combines symmetrical traffic splitting with an SBPP-based hybrid mechanism guided by a Vulnerability Scoring System (VSS). Each link receives a score computed from three terms: slot utilization (weight 0.5), historical failure count (weight 0.3) and physical distance (weight 0.2). The vulnerability of a path is the average of the scores of its constituent links, and paths with lower scores are preferred when selecting primary and backup routes. Traffic is split symmetrically across link-disjoint primary paths to balance load and delay congestion. If a shared backup becomes unreliable or congested, it is dynamically reassigned. To satisfy contiguity constraints, the system performs global re-optimization, repacking existing allocations during admission when necessary. SHP was compared against Partitioned DPP (PDPPS), which uses rigid spectrum partitions and "squeezed" restoration that drops part of the traffic, whereas SHP exploits SBPP and VSS for dynamic, full-capacity recovery. Reported results are a blocking probability reduction of 97% on USNET and 80% on NSFNET, spectrum utilization increases of 29% (USNET) and 40% (NSFNET), and traffic loss decreases of 44% (USNET) and 90% (NSFNET).

### Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Optical Network Data

Network data is confidential, and failure data is scarce and heavily class-imbalanced, so real-world open datasets are lacking and sharing raw telemetry across vendors and operators to train failure-management models is often impossible. The same constraint applies to traffic prediction, where models would ideally be trained on traffic data held at different network sites. Federated learning (FL) is proposed as a remedy: it trains a model across parties without transferring raw data, exchanging only model updates, and is complemented by standardized anonymization. Training at the edge, where the data is generated, preserves privacy and reduces communication overhead because raw traces need not be sent to a central server; this is the motivation for proposing FL for traffic-prediction models. Two settings are distinguished. In horizontal FL, the parties hold different data instances with the same features, for example several vendors whose devices produce the same alarm sets; each trains locally on its own instances and the updates are aggregated into a shared model. In vertical FL, the parties hold different features for the same instance, for example different operators controlling different spans of a single lightpath, each observing only its own segment's monitoring data; the model combines these partial views without any party exposing its raw measurements. FL complements encryption and other privacy-preserving techniques that are otherwise needed when training on a centralized server, and it fits the multi-party decision-authority scenarios in which operators, vendors and collaborators must cooperate on diagnosis.

### Uncertainty Quantification and Conformal Prediction
<!-- sources: failure_management_in_optical_networks_with_ml_a_tutorial_on_applications_challenges_and_pitfalls_invited, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: 948ad173687b -->
Incorrect ML outputs can be costly: in failure management, a wrong classification can trigger expensive, unnecessary hardware replacements, and in traffic forecasting, a wrong point estimate leads to over- or under-provisioning, so a bare predicted label or value is insufficient. Several mechanisms are used to express confidence. Uncertainty quantification, typically through probabilistic classification, makes the model output a probability or confidence level alongside each decision. For traffic prediction, Bayesian learning provides probabilistic outputs: Gaussian process regression (GPR) returns a measure of uncertainty rather than only a point prediction, and Bayesian deep learning (BDL) combines the scaling power of DNNs with principled uncertainty estimation. The resulting uncertainty margins on forecasts can be used when reserving resources, mitigating over-provisioning (wasted capacity) and under-provisioning (blocking or QoS violations) in proactive networks. Conformal prediction goes further by returning prediction sets or intervals with formal coverage guarantees, so that an ambiguous case yields several candidate failure causes rather than a single forced answer. When confidence is low or the set is large, the decision can be deferred to a human operator, keeping a human in the loop for uncertain cases while automating the confident ones.

### Active Learning for Training Data Collection
<!-- sources: failure_management_in_optical_networks_with_ml_a_tutorial_on_applications_challenges_and_pitfalls_invited | gen: 8717997a15f8 -->
ML failure classifiers can learn incorrect decision boundaries when the training data lacks diversity, for instance when some failure conditions or operating regimes are poorly covered. Active learning addresses this by identifying regions of high uncertainty in the feature space, where the current classifier is least confident. These regions indicate where new probes, experiments or labeled samples are most needed, so that data collection targets the points that most refine the boundary between classes rather than gathering data indiscriminately. This lowers the cost of building labeled failure datasets, which is significant because failures are rare and labeling or emulating them in a testbed or live network is expensive, and it complements data augmentation, digital twins and transfer learning as a strategy for obtaining valuable training data.

### Explainable AI and Hardware-Efficient ML for Optical Systems
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, deep_reinforcement_learning_for_infrastructure_as_a_service_over_flexible_optical_networks, failure_management_in_optical_networks_with_ml_a_tutorial_on_applications_challenges_and_pitfalls_invited, machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: 5593c399927f -->
Two practical barriers to deploying ML in optical systems are opacity and computational cost. Black-box models make troubleshooting and traceability difficult for network operators, and operator trust requires explainable AI (XAI): using inherently interpretable models such as decision trees and logistic regression (tree-based models are prioritized), and integrating human expert knowledge into the learning loop in a top-down, hybrid approach in which expert rules are combined with ML submodules. Human-in-the-loop (HITL) operation is also advocated for service provisioning, where human experts handle unexpected events that RL agents cannot anticipate. Post-hoc analysis is another route. SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) uses game theory to compute the marginal contribution of each feature to a specific prediction; in failure management, SHAP summary plots show which features are most impactful and whether high or low values of a feature push a decision toward or away from a given failure class. Post-hoc analysis of a trained policy also works for reinforcement learning: in deep reinforcement learning for virtual optical network embedding, link and FSU utilization heatmaps compared against those of heuristics show that heuristics favor specific links and always use the first available FSUs, whereas the agent distributes load more evenly across links and FSUs; this balanced use, which limits fragmentation and congestion, explains its lower blocking probability. Computational cost is the second barrier: real-time requirements clash with the high cost of deep learning. For real-time operation at high data rates, hardware acceleration with FPGAs is required. Complexity can be reduced through online learning, dimensionality reduction, model compression, and expert-driven feature engineering and pre-testing, accepting suboptimal solutions that fit within time thresholds, so that inference fits the latency and resource budgets of transceivers and network elements. TinyML and spiking neural networks are further proposed to reduce the computational overhead of inference at the edge. Because ML offers no absolute performance guarantees, periodic evaluation mechanisms and 'ML-aided' (human-in-the-loop) rather than 'ML-dominated' operation are also recommended.

### Adaptability and Data Challenges for ML in Optical Networks
<!-- sources: a_survey_on_machine_and_deep_learning_for_optical_communications_arxiv_preprint, failure_management_in_optical_networks_with_ml_a_tutorial_on_applications_challenges_and_pitfalls_invited, machine_learning_for_intelligent_optical_networks_a_comprehensive_survey, machine_learning_techniques_for_optical_performance_monitoring_and_modulation_format_identification_a_survey, resource_allocation_in_multicore_elastic_optical_networks_a_deep_reinforcement_learning_approach, survey_on_machine_learning_for_traffic-driven_service_provisioning_in_optical_networks | gen: b7976c863596 -->
Static ML models degrade when network infrastructure or traffic patterns change, and models are often coupled to a specific topology, which limits generalization. Research is lacking on automatic monitoring of model degradation and on adaptation to non-stationary traffic. This is observed for DRL resource allocation in multicore EONs: agents trained on one topology generalize across traffic loads and spectrum resources but not to another topology (an agent trained on NSFNet performs poorly on COST239, and vice versa). Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are proposed as a route to topology generalization, alongside hyperparameter tuning, larger core counts, and differentiated rewards by blocking cause (e.g., fragmentation versus crosstalk); explainability techniques have also been suggested to derive better human-designed heuristics from learned policies. Transfer learning and multi-task learning also reduce the large number of training episodes RL needs and allow policies to be transferred across network topologies. Most OPM/MFI work to date trains offline on static datasets; the field is encouraged to move toward real-time, self-learning and online adaptive architectures. Proposed remedies are focusing on local features rather than global network state, online learning for fine-tuning, continual learning for real-time model updates, transfer learning to carry knowledge across networks (or to reuse pre-trained weights and speed up training), and active learning to reduce the cost of data collection. In failure management, failures are rare events and datasets are heavily imbalanced, so three strategies are used for scarcity: data augmentation with variational autoencoders (VAEs) and generative adversarial networks (GANs) to synthesize failure samples; digital twins, simulated or physical network replicas in which failures are enforced to generate data; and transfer learning from a source domain (another network or lightpath) to the target. Data drift occurs as equipment ages or network status changes; continual learning updates models over time, but the optimal retraining frequency and the prevention of catastrophic forgetting of earlier data distributions remain open questions. Progress is further limited by the lack of open, real-world datasets, for three main reasons: data imbalance, privacy concerns that make operators reluctant to share data, and simulation gaps, since synthetic data lacks real-world nuance. Public ONFM resources are few and include a laboratory optical failure dataset emulating failures by altering Wavelength Selective Switch (WSS) characteristics, a Fraunhofer Institute dataset of WDM transmission scenarios suited to QoT estimation, and OTDR trace datasets covering fiber cuts, eavesdropping, dirty connectors and reflective/non-reflective events; most researchers rely on private testbeds or simulators. Suggested solutions include standardized labeling and anonymization protocols, federated learning, and GANs to synthesize realistic data. A related issue is the reality gap: most results, including those on provisioning, come from simulations with synthetic traffic or small testbeds, so closing it requires adding more physical parameters to simulators to enhance their fidelity, conducting rigorous field trials on large-scale real networks to validate generalizability and test the limits of current SDN/NFV frameworks, and moving toward real-world non-stationary deployments. The field is also encouraged to adopt tools from RF communications and image processing and to move from MATLAB to Python frameworks (PyTorch, TensorFlow) to collaborate with the wider AI community and leverage open-source tools.

### Benchmarking and Reproducibility Practices for ML in Optical Networks
<!-- sources: reinforcement_learning_for_dynamic_resource_allocation_in_optical_networks_hype_or_hope_arxiv_preprint, xlron_accelerated_reinforcement_learning_environments_for_optical_networks | gen: 7de00bc149f8 -->
Evaluating machine learning for dynamic resource allocation in optical networks has suffered from weak baselines and poor reproducibility. A survey of 97 RL papers found that many report 20–30% blocking improvements over heuristics, but the comparisons often rely on poorly implemented or untuned heuristics, and the simulation landscape is fragmented across competing toolkits (e.g., Optical-rl-gym, RSA-RL). Three obstacles have been identified: the compute intensity of training, which traditionally needs CPU clusters for simulation paired with GPU servers for learning; the absence of standard benchmarks and environments, which forces frequent reimplementation and slows reproduction and comparison; and reliability problems arising from closed-source code and the lack of comprehensive unit testing. DeepRMSA established a de facto standard for problem definitions and benchmarks that later work widely adopted. Three recommendations have been proposed for rigorous evaluation. First, benchmark selection: tune heuristic parameters such as the number of candidate paths and path ordering, and prefer deterministic algorithms over other ML models as baselines so that results are reproducible. Second, statistical rigor: use multiple random seeds and at least 100 blocking events per trial, reporting mean and standard deviation. Third, methodological transparency: release code and build on established frameworks with unit tests, such as the JAX-based XLRON simulator. XLRON is open source, developed with test-driven development and modularity, and ships with over 800 unit tests covering its RMSA, RSA, RWA and VONE environments; because it relies on JAX's explicit, deterministic pseudo-random number generation, runs can be reproduced exactly from a seed, and its GPU acceleration makes multi-seed evaluation affordable. Re-implementation can also expose flaws in widely reused code. The DeepRMSA codebase resamples service holding times that exceed twice the mean, truncating the distribution and reducing the effective mean holding time by about 31%; papers using it therefore evaluated agents under much lower traffic loads than reported. Re-evaluating five landmark studies (DeepRMSA, Reward-RMSA, GCN-RMSA, MaskRSA, PtrNet-RSA) on topologies such as NSFNET, COST239, USNET and JPN48 showed that a tuned KSP-FF heuristic (K=50, hop-count ordering) matched or beat the published RL results in nearly every case, sometimes by more than an order of magnitude in blocking probability, suggesting that earlier claims of RL superiority rested on weak benchmarks.
