# Key Concepts

Reference notes synthesizing the core concepts needed to read this collection of papers on Open RAN (O-RAN) architecture and RIC-based xApps, cell-free massive MIMO and free-duplex networks, network slicing and deep reinforcement learning for 6G resource management, optical fronthaul and WDM/PON-based Cloud and Fog-RAN (including functional splits, vBBU placement, and 6G enabling technologies such as FSO and SDM), and machine learning, federated learning, and MLOps for next-generation radio and optical access networks.

## 6G Vision and Requirements

### 6G Requirements and KPIs
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks, efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: 29c90d809139 -->
5G offers peak data rates of 10 Gbps and latency below 1 ms, which are expected to be insufficient by 2030, when global traffic is projected to exceed 3 zettabytes per year. 6G targets peak data rates of at least 100 Gbps (potentially reaching Tbps, with some formulations above 1 Tbps), user-experienced rates of 1 Gbps, and critical latencies between 10 and 100 μs, with reliability of 99.99999%. These targets support services such as holographic VR, holographic telepresence, Extended Reality, the Internet of Everything, autonomous driving, and Industry 4.0 with near-zero latency. The vision is framed around omnipresence and omniscience, with three-dimensional coverage across terrestrial, aerial, space, and sea domains (space-air-ground integration via satellites and stratospheric platforms), a massive device density of 10 million devices/km², and a reduction of CO2 emissions through energy efficiency. The challenges are commonly split into layers. At the radio layer, 6G must exploit previously unused spectrum, including the sub-THz (100–300 GHz) and Terahertz (100 GHz–10 THz) bands. At the transport layer, it needs ultra-high capacity, extreme resilience, and low latency from the cell site to the core, together with shared connections to cut deployment cost. AI/ML integration is considered imperative for autonomous network optimization. For the optical fronthaul specifically, projected peak rates of at least 1 Tbps and latency of 0.1 ms or less are regarded as reachable only with optical solutions, and later research targets 10 μs or less for critical services such as telemedicine and V2X. To meet these goals, a unified hierarchical infrastructure has been argued for that pools communication, computation, and storage resources. These requirements are expressed as per-service-type KPIs for the five envisioned 6G service types (FeMBB, umMTC, ERLLC, LDHMC, ELPC), for example peak and user-experienced rate for FeMBB and latency for ERLLC. Immersive services such as mixed reality (XR), holographic communication, and the Tactile Internet generate highly dynamic and heterogeneous traffic with stringent capacity, latency, and reliability demands, which is a main driver for ML across the network layers. Several industry initiatives frame these demands for the fixed and optical side. The Fifth Generation Fixed Network (F5G) promotes Fiber To Everywhere/Everything with a Management, Control, and Analytics (MCA) plane whose AI engine coordinates resources across enhanced fixed broadband, guaranteed reliable experience, and full fiber connection. The All-Photonics Network (APN) is an optical full-mesh network built around a photonic gateway (Ph-GW), where ML is envisioned for capacity and wavelength channel prediction to prevent contention and proactively tune transceivers. Network 2030 focuses on holographic-type communications and multi-sense services, which need distributed intelligence and ubiquitous fiber to synchronize volumetric data from multiple viewpoints. Already in 5G, the standardized service types pull the architecture in opposite directions: URLLC requires latency below 1 ms, which forces processing close to the cell site, whereas eMBB requires high bandwidth, which makes fronthaul capacity the bottleneck and favors centralized processing, while mMTC adds massive connection counts. Resolving this tension is a central design goal of RAN architectures such as Cloud Fog-RAN, where per-type latency thresholds and wavelength capacity are treated as explicit constraints. In one evaluation, a trained two-level RL model was tested over 1,000 episodes on three critical KPIs: for FeMBB, system peak data rate stayed above 1 Tbps and user-experienced rate above 1 Gbps, and for ERLLC, latency was consistently kept below 10 μs. These results were taken as showing that the approach can maintain strict 6G QoS requirements.

### Network Slicing in 6G RAN
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks, colo-ran_developing_machine_learning-based_xapps_for_open_ran_closed-loop_control_on_programmable_experimental_platforms, deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: 9df91f09e052 -->
Network slicing creates isolated virtual networks (slices) on shared physical infrastructure so that contradictory service demands can be met simultaneously. C-RAN supports slicing by centralizing RAN functions in cloud processing units, and DRL has been applied to it, for example A3C-based energy-efficient RAN slicing using SBiLSTM networks. For 6G, the literature considers five envisioned service types rather than just one or two: FeMBB (further-enhanced mobile broadband, defined by peak and user-experienced data rates), umMTC (ultra-massive machine-type communication), ERLLC (extremely reliable and low-latency communication, defined by latency), LDHMC (long-distance and high-mobility communication), and ELPC (extremely low-power communication), each with its own KPIs. A central difficulty arises when slicing is combined with cell-free massive MIMO: slices must not share resource blocks (RBs), yet there are no fixed cell boundaries or fixed user groups on which to base the isolation. Slicing is therefore split into deciding which slice type serves each incoming request and then allocating the RBs that realize that slice's QoS requirements. Approaches in this line map slice assignment to a discrete choice among the five types and treat network capacity (resource utilization) as the objective, subject to per-slice KPI guarantees. For 5G, the three standardized 3GPP slice types are URLLC (Type 1), eMBB (Type 2), and mMTC (Type 3). In Cloud Fog-RAN deployments over WDM transport, these are created as three logical networks over the shared physical infrastructure via Network Function Virtualization (NFV). Isolation is enforced in the optical layer by dedicating wavelengths to each service type: only traffic of the same type can be groomed into shared lightpaths, so slices never share a wavelength. In a reference setup with 8 wavelengths of 10 Gbps per fiber, 4 are assigned to eMBB, 2 to URLLC, and 2 to mMTC. Slice type also determines where processing happens: URLLC requests terminate at fog nodes near the cell sites, while eMBB and mMTC requests terminate at centralized BBU hotels. In O-RAN, slicing is aimed at creating virtual networks with specific SLAs. The SMO manages slice templates, and ML models in the near-RT RIC monitor slice-specific performance over the E2 interface and adjust RAN behavior to assure the SLAs. Database partitioning inside the RICs is needed to keep slices isolated from one another, potentially combined with distributed federated learning. In converged optical-wireless transport, slicing is also handled in the optical aggregation layer: Open vSwitch (OVS) instances aggregate traffic at the regional nodes and are coordinated by a Network-Slicing-as-a-Service (NSaaS) subsystem. This subsystem enables predictive slice reconfiguration and energy saving, since idle SFP-OLTs can be shut down when load allows. Experimental O-RAN work uses a smaller set of three slices: eMBB (video traffic), MTC (sensing), and URLLC (latency-sensitive). Control covers both the number of Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs) allocated to each slice and the per-slice scheduling policy, chosen among Round Robin, Waterfilling, and Proportional Fair. Evaluations of DRL agents show that joint control of PRB allocation and scheduling policy outperforms fixed slicing combined with adaptive scheduling, with the largest gains for users in the lower throughput percentiles. In infrastructure planning for sliced O-RAN, the uRLLC, eMBB, and mMTC slices each carry their own bounds on over-the-air (OTA) latency, front-haul latency, mid-haul latency, and BBU processing latency, and these bounds drive how many RUs are installed, where DUs and CUs are placed, and how much traffic is aggregated. The slice-specific OTA bound enters the UE-RU association problem through the sum of propagation and transmission latency. eMBB requires the most RUs because of its throughput demand. For uRLLC, DUs can be placed at the RU site, and 5G NR mini-slots and grant-free uplink transmission further cut latency. eMBB DUs can sit at Stage-I OLT locations, and less latency-sensitive mMTC traffic can be aggregated through a second PON stage toward larger CUs or edge clouds. Evaluations in industrial, urban, and rural scenarios with tidal-wave traffic patterns found all slice latency bounds satisfied.

### Channel Knowledge Map (CKM)
<!-- sources: closed-loop_uplink_radio_resource_management_in_cf-o-ran_empowered_5g_aerial_corridor | gen: b73e99548681 -->
A channel knowledge map (CKM) is a database of location-indexed propagation statistics, such as large-scale fading information, that lets the network predict channel conditions without measuring them in real time. In O-RAN-enabled cell-free systems, resource management normally requires global channel state information (CSI) across many distributed O-RUs, but the E2 interface between the near-RT RIC and the O-DU/O-CU has limited bandwidth and cannot support high-rate CSI exchange. To avoid this, a CKM rApp is hosted in the non-RT RIC. Telemetry data travels over the O1 interface to a global database and updates the CKM, and the resulting policy and channel data are sent over the A1 interface to a local RAN database. RRM xApps in the near-RT RIC then compute association and power decisions from this stored knowledge instead of from instantaneous global CSI. This reduces signaling overhead and dependence on real-time CSI while keeping decisions aligned with the propagation environment. The approach is particularly suited to aerial corridors, where UAV trajectories are structured and predictable, so propagation statistics along the route can be reused.

### UAV Aerial Corridors and Cellular-Connected UAVs
<!-- sources: closed-loop_uplink_radio_resource_management_in_cf-o-ran_empowered_5g_aerial_corridor | gen: b2d141c50076 -->
Aerial corridors are structured three-dimensional routes designed for beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS) UAV traffic. Providing reliable connectivity along them requires specialized re-engineering of terrestrial cellular networks, which were designed for ground users. A key difficulty is that UAVs at altitude have strong LoS links to many ground base stations or O-RUs, so their uplink transmissions cause severe interference to many receivers at once. Channel models for this setting follow 3GPP UMa-AV (urban macro with aerial vehicles), in which the LoS probability depends on UAV height. Large-scale fading combines path loss and shadowing, and small-scale fading is typically spatially correlated Rician with a dominant LoS component, expressed in mean-plus-deviation form. Cell-free massive MIMO within O-RAN is considered a suitable architecture, since serving each UAV through multiple distributed O-RUs offers macro-diversity, while the RIC provides programmability for dynamic UAV scenarios. Evaluations use dense deployments, for example 100 O-RUs over a 1 × 1 km² area with up to about 90 UAVs. Joint UAV trajectory optimization is identified as a direction beyond association and power control.

## RAN Architecture Evolution and O-RAN

### RAN Architecture Evolution: D-RAN, C-RAN, vRAN to O-RAN
<!-- sources: deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, deep_recurrent_neural_network_for_optical_fronthaul_dimensioning_and_proactive_vbbu_placement_in_cf-ran, efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives, vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: 4905efaab2ed -->
RAN architectures have evolved through successive steps of separating and centralizing functions, in order to move processing and reduce costs. In 2G, baseband and radio processing were co-located at the base station. 3G and 4G introduced the Distributed RAN (D-RAN), which separated the Baseband Unit (BBU) from the Remote Radio Head (RRH) and connected them through a fronthaul network; the BBU remains collocated with the radio at the site, which lacks the scalability needed for 5G/6G. Cloud-RAN (C-RAN) then decoupled RAN functions from individual base stations and centralized the BBUs into a shared pool of cloud processing units (BBU hotels) serving many RRHs, which reduces energy consumption, CapEx, and OpEx and improves scalability and spectral efficiency through virtualization of baseband processing. Centralization also enables advanced signal processing such as CoMP and carrier aggregation and lowers site costs, but it places high demands on the fronthaul. The architecture was motivated by the traffic growth and service diversity of 5G (self-driving vehicles, remote medicine, VR) and supports network slicing as well as IoT, Vehicle-to-Everything communications, and Mobile Edge Computing (MEC). C-RAN has also been used as a platform for distributed learning: in vertical federated learning, distributed RRHs acting as edge servers, coordinated by a central BBU pool, shorten the communication distance between devices and servers and thereby ease the straggler problem of devices with poor channel conditions. In simulations with the same total antenna count, a Cloud-RAN with N ≥ 2 RRHs outperformed a centralized massive MIMO array, because spreading antennas ensures that devices are close to at least one server. Heterogeneous C-RAN (HC-RAN) integrates small base stations with macro base stations and uses cloud computing to improve capacity and energy efficiency. Fog RAN (F-RAN) decentralizes functions closer to users through fog computing, offloading central servers and reducing latency by up to about 70%. Virtualization-based resource management in C-RAN allows dynamic pooling and sharing of resources across virtual networks, using a Virtual Base Station (VBS) pool and RRHs; proposed schemes include inter-operator cooperation based on trusted computing platforms and auction-based methods that aim at truthful resource sharing and social welfare among operators. Centralization, however, concentrates workload on the fronthaul and the cloud facility: high-capacity transport such as Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing (DWDM) is costly, and the cloud can become a processing bottleneck. Specific limitations of 2-layer C-RAN are scalability, the high fronthaul capacity demanded by eMBB, and the inability to meet the sub-1 ms latency requirement of URLLC when processing is far from the cell site. In a fully centralized C-RAN using the most centralized split (Split E), queuing congestion on the fronthaul causes sharp drops in availability once latency exceeds about 100 µs. Cloud-Fog RAN (CF-RAN) addresses this hybrid case by adding smaller fog nodes closer to the cell sites (near the RRHs) that take over RRH processing when the cloud is overloaded. In a 3-layer formulation, RRHs form layer 1, fog nodes layer 2, and BBU hotels layer 3. Time-sensitive URLLC requests are processed at fog nodes, while eMBB and mMTC requests are handled by the centralized BBUs. Compared with a 2-layer C-RAN, this reduces active nodes for URLLC (about 33% in large-network simulations), lowers URLLC fronthaul delay (about 10% in ILP results), and keeps the problem feasible under high traffic loads where the 2-layer design fails. CF-RAN relies on NFV to deploy virtualized BBUs (vBBUs): a cloud layer handles centralized baseband processing for light loads through a pool of virtual digital units (VDUs) on dedicated servers, and an orchestrator activates fog nodes only when needed, powering servers on only when in use. This creates a trade-off between energy consumption (activating more nodes) and network capacity. Virtualized RAN (vRAN) builds on Network Function Virtualization (NFV) and Software Defined Networking (SDN) to replace proprietary hardware with software on COTS servers and to decouple the control and user planes; in a DU cloud, vBBUs run on standard server hardware, allowing dynamic capacity scaling, agility, and improved service reliability. Open RAN, as defined by the O-RAN Alliance, goes further by disaggregating hardware from software into O-CU, O-DU, and O-RU blocks joined by standardized open interfaces, which prevents vendor lock-in, and by adding a hierarchical RAN Intelligent Controller (non-RT and near-RT RIC) that provides a platform for AI/ML integration. Mapping CF-RAN onto O-RAN places the O-DU on fog nodes close to the O-RUs and the O-CU in the cloud, so that the placement of these functions follows latency and capacity limits rather than full centralization. In comparisons, vRAN and O-RAN both offer low latency, low CAPEX/OPEX, and low energy consumption thanks to virtualization and edge support. O-RAN's distinguishing features are open interfaces for multi-vendor interoperability and a programmable control plane with native AI support through the dual RICs, together with disaggregated functional blocks. The progression toward O-RAN reflects a need for agility and slicing, yet the practical hurdle for 6G remains the fronthaul infrastructure, which must handle massive device density and diverse traffic demands while staying cost-effective. Earlier surveys typically concentrated on C-RAN, vRAN, or Fog RAN (F-RAN), leaving a gap in mapping existing DL-based RAN solutions onto the O-RAN framework.

### Open RAN (O-RAN) Architecture and RIC
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks, closed-loop_uplink_radio_resource_management_in_cf-o-ran_empowered_5g_aerial_corridor, colo-ran_developing_machine_learning-based_xapps_for_open_ran_closed-loop_control_on_programmable_experimental_platforms, deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran | gen: 1a41edfaf56f -->
O-RAN moves the RAN from hardware-based, vendor-closed ecosystems toward open, cloud-based, modular architectures in which hardware is disaggregated from software and functions communicate over open interfaces, preventing vendor lock-in. The architecture is built from the O-Centralized Unit (O-CU), O-Distributed Unit (O-DU), and O-Remote Unit (O-RU), which can be virtualized and run on commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) hardware. The O-CU is split into a control plane (O-CU-CP) and a user plane (O-CU-UP) and manages the RRC, SDAP, and PDCP protocols. The O-DU handles RLC, MAC, and High-PHY, including scheduling and radio resource allocation, and the O-RU handles Low-PHY and RF processing. Functional split options define the division of labor between CU, DU, and RU (for example Option 2 for the high-layer split and Options 6/7 for the low-layer split) and trade off latency against centralization cost. For fronthaul in cell-free deployments, the O-RAN splits FS8 (PHY-RF) and FS7.2x (Low-PHY) set the capacity each link must carry, and together with the number and placement of DUs they drive the trade-off among cost, capacity, and resilience; FS7.2x has been found the more scalable choice. Deployment scenarios range from fully separated RU, DU, and CU locations, which require fronthaul, midhaul, and backhaul links, to full integration of all three units at a single site. In sliced O-RAN planning, DUs and CUs are hosted on shared open access-edge servers, using split-7.2 for the RU-DU front-haul and split-2 for the DU-CU mid-haul. Intelligence is added through the RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC), split into two parts. The non-real-time (non-RT) RIC sits within the Service Management and Orchestration (SMO) framework, hosts rApps, and handles orchestration, policy management, model training, and long-term optimization on timescales above 1 s; it provides policies to the near-RT RIC over the A1 interface. The near-real-time (near-RT) RIC hosts xApps that manage QoS requirements for functions such as load balancing, handover, spectrum and resource management, and slicing policies; it controls the O-CU and O-DU over the E2 interface in loops reported as roughly 10 ms to 1 s, with 10–100 ms commonly cited for the tightest loops. This split gives ML algorithms natural hosting locations: a high-level learning agent for slice-type assignment can run in the non-RT RIC with a global view of the network, while low-level agents performing resource allocation run in the near-RT RIC. Coordination between them, which carries slice assignments downward, occurs mainly over the A1 interface, keeping communication overhead low and keeping the design aligned with O-RAN specifications. DL-based RAN functions from the literature map onto specific blocks. Resource management (traffic prediction, scheduling, power allocation) maps to the O-DU, specifically MAC for resource assignment and scheduling and High-PHY for PDSCH power allocation. Mobility management (conditional handover prediction, base-station sleep modes) maps to the O-CU-CP, within UE/gNB and cell procedure management. Spectrum management is split between the O-DU High-PHY for channel estimation, encoding/decoding, and modulation classification, and the O-RU Low-PHY for mmWave beam selection. ML deployment in O-RAN follows a five-step pipeline: data collection over the O1 interface, model design, offline training and testing, packaging of the model as an xApp, and runtime inference and control over the E2 interface. Designers must choose which RAN parameters serve as observations and which serve as controlled outputs (for example scheduling policies); trained models are then instantiated in the near-RT RIC for closed-loop control on live network conditions. On the implementation side, a lightweight containerized version of the O-RAN Software Community (OSC) near-RT RIC has been built with LXC and Docker, avoiding a full Kubernetes deployment. It connects over E2 to base stations running the SCOPE framework and srsRAN, with xApps handling ASN.1 encoding and decoding of E2 messages through a RIC interface plus an ML unit. Measured control-loop roundtrip time in this setup averages about 114 ms, which fits within the near-RT RIC budget. The same split supports closed-loop radio resource management (RRM) for CF mMIMO. A channel knowledge map (CKM) rApp in the non-RT RIC maintains propagation statistics; telemetry flows over the O1 interface into a global database that updates the CKM, and policy and CSI data are passed over A1 to a local RAN database. RRM xApps in the near-RT RIC, such as uplink power control, compute decisions from this data and send instructions over the E2 interface to the O-DU/O-CU, which forwards them to the O-RUs. Because E2 cannot carry high-rate global CSI exchange, the CKM reduces that load, and the xApp algorithms must complete within the roughly 10–150 ms near-RT budget, which motivates low-complexity solvers over generic interior-point methods. A further extension of O-RAN for CF networking replaces a traditional C-RAN, which often lacks the bandwidth and flexibility for 5G/6G, with a two-tier distributed edge made of Radio Edge and Regional Edge nodes. Following 3GPP NG-RAN disaggregation, the architecture uses a virtualized O-DU (vO-DU) for real-time functions and a virtualized O-CU (vO-CU) for non-real-time functions, with the vO-CU split into vO-CU-CP and vO-CU-UP. The vO-DU and vO-CU-UP are deployed at the Radio Edge, while the near-RT RIC and vO-CU-CP reside at the Regional Edge. CF high-PHY functions (modulation and precoding) are integrated into the vO-DU and the MAC scheduler through a 5G FAPI-like interface. The near-RT RIC performs global RRM using ML inference, and the vO-DU performs local scheduling that complements this global view. A near-RT SDN function is also proposed to react to workload variations on sub-second timescales.

### Open Access-Edge Cloud for DU/CU Hosting
<!-- sources: optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran | gen: 8bb51eedf614 -->
An access-edge cloud is a pool of general-purpose servers located at the network edge, for example at OLT or RU sites, that hosts virtualized O-RAN functions on commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) hardware. The concept is used to democratize the network edge: instead of dedicating baseband hardware to one operator or one service, Distributed Units (DUs) and Centralized Units (CUs) are run as software on shared open servers, so that multiple slices, and potentially multiple operators, can use the same infrastructure. This builds on the disaggregation of baseband processing into virtualized RUs, DUs, and CUs in O-RAN, and on network slicing realized through software-defined everything (SDx) and network function virtualization (NFV), which let resources be customized for each service type. Placement is a design choice that trades latency against aggregation and cost. DUs can be placed at the RU location, which gives the lowest latency and suits uRLLC, at the Stage-I OLT location, or aggregated in larger edge cloud servers; CUs are typically hosted at Stage-I or Stage-II OLT locations. Aggregating functions in fewer, larger servers reduces equipment cost and raises utilization, but lengthens the front-haul and mid-haul paths and therefore consumes latency budget, which is why latency-tolerant mMTC traffic is the best candidate for deeper aggregation through a second PON stage. The servers are constrained by their processing capacity, measured in GOPS, and the load of the chosen splits (split-7.2 at the front-haul and split-2 at the mid-haul) is distributed as 40% at the RU, 50% at the DU, and 10% at the CU. Planning models therefore check both communication latency and processing latency against the capacity of each server. Evaluations of this framework found that a double-stage architecture is more cost-efficient than a single stage when server capacities are limited, because CU functions can be aggregated better, and that the whole design is 21%-28% cheaper than mesh-based OTN frameworks while meeting the front-haul, mid-haul, and BBU processing latency bounds of all slices.

## Cell-Free Massive MIMO

### Cell-Free Massive MIMO
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks, closed-loop_uplink_radio_resource_management_in_cf-o-ran_empowered_5g_aerial_corridor, decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex, fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: a117b116915a -->
Cell-free massive MIMO (CF mMIMO) replaces the traditional cellular layout with a distributed set of access points that coherently serve users jointly, without cell boundaries. This improves spectral and energy efficiency, increases diversity, eases interference management, and reduces latency, making it a strong candidate physical layer for 6G. Serving single-antenna UEs through a large number of distributed APs managed by a central processing pool also removes the cell-edge problem and improves coverage. The concept has evolved from all APs serving all UEs to a user-centric approach, in which only a dynamic cluster of APs serves each UE, selected according to channel quality and distance. Because users are served through multiple distributed access points, the architecture provides macro-diversity and robust connectivity, which is attractive for scenarios with strong line-of-sight (LoS) uplink interference such as cellular-connected UAVs in aerial corridors. The benefit of distribution is also visible in learning workloads: in over-the-air vertical federated learning, a distributed deployment with the same total number of antennas split across N ≥ 2 RRHs outperformed a single centralized massive MIMO array, because devices with poor channels (stragglers) are more likely to be near at least one RRH, which improves the channel conditions of the worst devices and overall performance. In an O-RAN-enabled uplink model, K single-antenna UAVs are served by L distributed O-RUs, each with N_ℓ antennas, operating in TDD mode. Channels are estimated with MMSE estimation at the O-DU/O-CU, but because the number of orthogonal pilots is limited, pilots are reused among UAVs and cause pilot contamination; this also limits the number of UAVs each O-RU can serve to the pilot length τ_p. Each O-RU applies local MMSE (L-MMSE) combining, and a central CPU merges the local estimates using maximal ratio combining (MRC) weights, with per-UAV uplink spectral efficiency (SE) derived from the resulting SINR. Coordination is a core difficulty: high SE requires substantial information sharing such as channel state information (CSI), but full centralization does not scale because of fronthaul bandwidth constraints. Fully centralized processing at a single unit scales poorly with the number of APs and users, which has motivated a hierarchical cell-free radio access network (CF-RAN). In this structure a Cloud Computing Unit (CCU) manages AP association and duplex mode selection, Edge Distributed Units (EDUs) perform uplink detection and downlink precoding for the APs attached to them, and the APs handle radio-frequency transmission and reception. Cooperation among APs can then be partial, distributed, or centralized, and resources are allocated jointly across the spatial, time, and frequency domains. A typical system model assumes M APs with N antennas each, X EDUs, K downlink UEs and J uplink UEs, with frequency-flat fading composed of large-scale coefficients and small-scale Rayleigh fading. A complementary scalability route is sequential processing at the AP level, as in Radio Stripes and Radio Weaves, which reduces the load on the CPU. Another is to fragment the CF CPU into multiple DUs and form dynamic RU clusters, so that computation and coordination are distributed between RUs and DUs. Clustering can be driven by machine learning trained on real channel measurements rather than theoretical channel models, in order to optimize peak data rates and avoid the suboptimal results of analytical divide-and-conquer decompositions. Because re-forming clusters at every Transmission Time Interval (TTI) would impose heavy overhead, the lifetime of a cluster is analyzed and clusters are re-formed predictively, which maintains performance for mobile users. Deployment in ultra-dense networks is also limited by the cost and capacity of the fronthaul links that connect the APs to the central processing units (CPUs/DUs). Dedicated fiber to every AP is reliable but expensive and hard to scale, so fronthaul planning has been treated as a design problem of its own: APs placed at random in a 2D area are partitioned into G groups by K-Means clustering, each group is served by one of W DUs, and APs within a group share fronthaul infrastructure rather than each having a point-to-point link. Larger groups lower overall fronthaul cost but raise the processing load on each DU, and the cost per AP of such shared architectures is lower than that of point-to-point small-cell connections, especially in centralized deployments with few DUs (W < 4). The same boundaryless operation conflicts with network slicing, which requires precise resource isolation: slices must not share resource blocks, but with no cells and no fixed user groups there is no natural unit over which to partition resources. Combining CF mMIMO with O-RAN is therefore treated as an open problem, addressed in some work by letting learning agents decide slice assignment and RB allocation dynamically rather than via static partitioning. Communication link clustering within the allocation logic has been proposed as a further refinement.

### Linear Precoding in CF Networks (MRT, ZF, RZF)
<!-- sources: machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks | gen: cefb6755b6fe -->
Linear precoders determine how the distributed RUs of a cell-free network weight their transmitted signals toward the served users, and the choice strongly affects both system throughput and fairness. Maximum Ratio Transmission (MRT) maximizes the signal gain toward each user and does not suppress interference between users, whereas Zero Forcing (ZF) uses channel knowledge to cancel inter-user interference. Regularized Zero Forcing (RZF) adds a regularization term to the ZF inversion. In a simulation of 64 RUs and 10 UEs at 4 GHz, with a Q-learning algorithm selecting the serving RUs for each PRB, three schemes were compared. CF ZF achieved the highest system spectral efficiency at 89.4 Mb/s/Hz, significantly outperforming CF MRT at 74.3 Mb/s/Hz and the SISO Round Robin baseline at 58.6 Mb/s/Hz. The differences were larger in coverage, measured as the spectral efficiency of the bottom 5% of users: ZF provided 5.93 Mb/s/Hz, whereas MRT gave only 0.32 Mb/s/Hz. MRT performs poorly here because it prioritizes users already in good radio conditions and leaves those with weak channels underserved. While ZF is highly effective, RZF and Deep RL applied to MRT were noted as potential areas for further improvement.

### Max-Min Fairness Power Control and AP Association in CF Networks
<!-- sources: closed-loop_uplink_radio_resource_management_in_cf-o-ran_empowered_5g_aerial_corridor | gen: c780d76f3e05 -->
Max-min fairness in cell-free networks maximizes the minimum spectral efficiency (SE) across users by jointly choosing the user-to-access-point association matrix and the uplink transmit powers. In an O-RAN cell-free UAV setting, the constraints are that every UAV connects to at least one O-RU, each O-RU serves at most τ_p UAVs (limited by pilot orthogonality), and each UAV meets a minimum SE (SE_k ≥ SE_k^min) for QoS. Because of the non-convex log2(1+Γ_k) term and binary association variables, this is a mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP) and NP-hard; exhaustive association search scales as O(2^(KL)). The association is handled by a three-stage heuristic of roughly O(KL) complexity: UAV-centric initialization, where each UAV joins its strongest O-RU by large-scale fading; O-RU-centric load balancing, where under-capacity O-RUs take their strongest unassigned UAVs; and QoS-driven refinement, where UAVs below SE^min are iteratively linked to further O-RUs in descending channel strength until QoS is met or a limit of ⌈L/2⌉ is reached. Power is set by bisection-guided fixed-point power control (BG-FPPC): an outer bisection searches for the maximum feasible target SINR, and an inner fixed-point iteration finds the minimum powers achieving it, with the target feasible if the largest power does not exceed p^max. Its complexity is O(K²), versus O(K^3.5) for interior-point solvers such as CVX. Simulations report minimum-SE gains of up to 440% over a baseline, BG-FPPC matching the CVX solution, runtime reductions of up to 99.1% that fit the 10–150 ms near-RT RIC range, a 100% QoS success rate for K ≤ 90 UAVs with the proposed association, and Jain's fairness of 100% (a 70% improvement) with max-min power allocation. The results indicate that association is the dominant factor for meeting QoS, while power control drives fairness.

### Distributed Processing and Team MMSE with Edge Distributed Units
<!-- sources: decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex | gen: 09abebad47fa -->
To reduce the computational burden on the Cloud Computing Unit (CCU), uplink detection and downlink precoding in a cell-free RAN can be moved to Edge Distributed Units (EDUs), each serving a subset of APs. Because each EDU only has partial observation information S_x (local channel estimates plus any side information shared by other EDUs), the receivers cannot be computed with a centralized MMSE formula. The EDU-TMMSE (Team MMSE) scheme uses team theory, in which decentralized decision makers cooperate on a common objective under different information, to design local receivers. Each receiver has a two-layer structure: an instantaneous EMMSE receiver A_x computed from local information, and a compensation matrix B_x that accounts for the information the EDU lacks. An adaptive sharing mechanism sets each EDU to a Shared (On) or Non-shared (Off) state. Shared EDUs exchange instantaneous equivalent channel information, whereas non-shared EDUs rely on statistical information supplied by the CCU. This creates a tunable trade-off between signaling overhead and spectral efficiency (SE). Simulation shows a performance hierarchy: PDBCD-FULL (all EDUs sharing) ≈ centralized MMSE > PDBCD-HALF > PDBCD-NO. EDUs also cooperate to suppress inter-AP interference through reconstructed signal cancellation, and the uplink SINR is finally aggregated at the CCU from the data processed by all EDUs. Downlink precoding is updated with a serial-parallel scheme, serial across APs within an EDU and parallel across EDUs.

## Network-Assisted Free-Duplex

### Network-Assisted Free-Duplex (NA-FD)
<!-- sources: decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex | gen: 7e237b291dc6 -->
Network-assisted free-duplex (NA-FD) lets access points in a cell-free RAN operate in the same frequency band as either transmitting APs (TAPs) or receiving APs (RAPs), so that uplink and downlink traffic are served simultaneously rather than separated in time as in TDD. The architecture supports full, hybrid, and flexible duplex modes, and each AP must be either a TAP or a RAP at any given time. Because the choice of which APs transmit and which receive is a per-AP, network-coordinated decision, NA-FD can be treated as a specialized form of spatial-domain duplex scheduling within the joint spatial, time, and frequency resource allocation of the CF-RAN. The gain is higher spectral efficiency (SE), since both link directions reuse the same resources, but the cost is cross-link interference (CLI), in the form of inter-AP and uplink-to-downlink user interference, together with heavy centralized processing if all computation sits at the Cloud Computing Unit (CCU). In a hierarchical deployment, the CCU handles AP association and duplex mode selection while Edge Distributed Units (EDUs) perform uplink detection and downlink precoding. Simulations with 48 APs and 4 EDUs show that SE grows monotonically with the number of UEs and with antennas per AP, and that NA-FD benefits more from additional antennas than TDD because of better interference suppression. Under limited backhaul capacity per EDU, a greedy NA-FD scheme (G-NAFD) achieved significantly higher SE than TDD, for example a 67% improvement at lower capacities. Performance depends on how well CLI is managed, through transceiver design and duplex mode selection, particularly in high-interference scenarios.

### Cross-Link Interference in Free-Duplex Networks (IAI and IUI)
<!-- sources: decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex | gen: 5e0e43a355f8 -->
Simultaneous transmission and reception in the same band creates cross-link interference (CLI) that does not exist in TDD. Two components are distinguished. Inter-AP interference (IAI) is the signal that transmitting APs (TAPs) leak into receiving APs (RAPs), degrading uplink detection; it acts on the uplink SINR at the RAPs. Uplink-to-downlink user interference (IUI) is caused by uplink UEs transmitting while downlink UEs receive, so the signal at a downlink UE contains the desired signal from TAPs, interference from other downlink users, and IUI from uplink users. The downlink sum-rate is defined from the SINR γ_{D,k} of each downlink UE, and the uplink sum-rate from the SINR γ_{U,j} aggregated at the CCU after the Edge Distributed Units (EDUs) forward their processed data. Since the TAP signals are known to the network, EDUs can cooperate to suppress IAI by reconstructing and cancelling it. Cancellation is imperfect, however: channel estimation errors leave residual IAI, which is the main limiting factor and the quantity that duplex mode selection and receiver design must manage. Simulations indicate that choosing TAP/RAP roles by a greedy search mitigates the effect of residual IAI and outperforms random or fixed duplex configurations, with the advantage most visible in high-interference scenarios. Uplink power control also matters for IUI, since each UE's uplink transmit power is constrained and enters the downlink interference terms.

### WMMSE Sum-Rate Maximization
<!-- sources: decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex | gen: 9ce878a94764 -->
Maximizing the joint uplink and downlink sum-rate in a free-duplex cell-free network is non-convex, subject to per-AP transmit power constraints, per-UE uplink power limits, and binary AP duplex mode constraints. The weighted minimum mean squared error (WMMSE) approach exploits the relationship between MMSE and SINR to convert the sum-rate objective into an equivalent weighted MSE minimization. The transformed objective, written as χ_D + χ_U for downlink and uplink, is minimized over several variable blocks: auxiliary weights α, downlink receive filters u_D, downlink precoding vectors W, and uplink receiving vectors V (plus uplink transmit powers p_U). Each block has a tractable update when the others are fixed, so block coordinate descent can be applied. In the decomposition used for CF-RAN with network-assisted free-duplex, subproblem 1 optimizes V, subproblem 2 optimizes W, α_D, and u_D, and subproblem 3 optimizes p_U and α_U. The last is solved centrally at the CCU by setting the partial derivative of the MSE with respect to p_U to zero, giving a closed-form local optimum under the UE power constraints. A fully centralized WMMSE is computationally heavy for large networks, so the first two subproblems are solved in a distributed manner at the Edge Distributed Units, and the duplex mode variable μ is handled outside the continuous WMMSE loop by a separate search. A centralized MMSE solution serves as the upper-bound benchmark.

### Greedy Bit-Flipping for AP Duplex Mode Selection
<!-- sources: decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex | gen: 7e8fac6276ca -->
Selecting which APs act as transmitting APs (TAPs) and which as receiving APs (RAPs) is a binary integer programming problem in the duplex mode vector μ, with one binary variable per AP. Exhaustive search over all configurations grows exponentially with the number of APs and is computationally prohibitive. A greedy bit-flipping algorithm replaces it: starting from a random configuration, it iterates through each AP in turn and flips its mode (TAP ↔ RAP), keeping the flip only if the overall sum rate increases. This reduces complexity from exponential to linear in the number of APs while maintaining near-optimal performance. In the cell-free RAN setting, the greedy search is combined with the distributed transceiver optimization (PDBCD), giving the GPDBCD scheme, and the sum-rate is evaluated for each candidate mode. Simulations with 48 APs show that greedy mode selection outperforms random or fixed duplex modes, particularly in high-interference scenarios where residual inter-AP interference is significant, and that GPDBCD-FULL stays within 3 bps/Hz of the centralized upper bound.

## Optical and Wireless Fronthaul Technologies

### TWDM-PON Optical Fronthaul and Virtual PON Channels
<!-- sources: deep_recurrent_neural_network_for_optical_fronthaul_dimensioning_and_proactive_vbbu_placement_in_cf-ran, energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran, fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: b537951f2011 -->
Passive Optical Networks (PONs) use a point-to-multipoint architecture that reduces fiber cost through sharing, and PON is regarded as a primary candidate for 6G mobile fronthaul because of its cost and capacity benefits. The technology has evolved from early A-PON/GPON through NG-PON2 (TWDM) and the current higher-speed PON (HSP, up to 50 Gbps) toward a future super-PON targeting more than 100 Gbps. Several variants are compared for fronthaul. TDM-PON is cost-effective but limited by dynamic bandwidth allocation (DBA) processing delay, which makes microsecond-level latency hard to reach. WDM-PON offers high capacity and low latency because it needs no DBA, but is expensive due to wavelength filters. TWDM-PON combines TDM and WDM, offering high flexibility and coexistence with legacy GPON. OFDM-PON has high spectral efficiency and suits Tbps targets, but requires expensive coherent detection. OCDM-PON uses unique codes for security and asynchronous transmission. NOMA-PON uses power-domain multiplexing to raise user density but suffers from self-induced intermodulation interference. PDM-PON uses polarization diversity to reach at least 100 Gbps per wavelength. Time-and-Wavelength Division Multiplexing PON (TWDM-PON) is proposed as a lower-cost alternative to DWDM for the optical fronthaul of C-RAN and CF-RAN, combining wavelength channels with time-division sharing on passive infrastructure. Meeting 6G goals (above 1 Tbps, microsecond latency) is expected to require ML integration across all network layers, including dynamic bandwidth allocation on the shared PON uplink. In a CF-RAN deployment the fronthaul has three levels of multiplexing built from splitters. The S1 splitter is internal to a fog node and connects the optical network units (ONUs) through dedicated fibers. The S2 splitter connects multiple fog nodes to the level-3 splitter. The S3 splitter forwards the traffic coming from the S2 splitters to the Optical Line Terminal (OLT) located in the cloud. On top of this physical tree, virtual PON (VPON) channels allow RRHs to share an optical channel (wavelength) through Time Division Multiplexing (TDM). In O-RAN-oriented formulations, the interfaces between O-RU, O-DU, and O-CU (F1, Xn, and E1) share the channels of a VPON through TDM, so that user, control, and synchronization traffic of different interfaces compete for the same optical capacity. VPONs can be created or modified dynamically according to demand, so that fronthaul capacity follows the number of active RRHs and their traffic. Each VPON has a bandwidth capacity that bounds the total demand of the RRHs mapped to it, and line cards and backplane switches that carry the channels contribute to power consumption only when active. A VPON is activated only if it hosts demands. Dimensioning the fronthaul therefore amounts to deciding how many VPONs and wavelengths to activate and which RRH traffic each one carries, a decision coupled with where the corresponding vBBUs are placed and which functional split each RU uses. VPON bandwidth and line-card power are included directly in the optimization model, and the queuing delay on the channel depends on the optical capacity and the offered traffic rate. For cell-free massive MIMO fronthaul, the fiber option is modeled with XGS WDM-PON technology, which provides symmetric uplink and downlink rates. Fiber links are treated as lossless with a constant capacity of 10 Gbps, and the equipment comprises ONUs at the APs, optical add-drop multiplexers (OADMs), and optical network terminals (OTNs) at the DU side. The number of OTNs needed, and hence the cost, scales with the splitter ratio Θ. Fiber is the preferred option for APs close to their DUs because of its low cost and reliability, whereas more distant APs tend to use mmWave where availability permits; adding DUs shortens distances and therefore increases the share of fiber. In a converged optical-wireless architecture, 10-Gigabit-Symmetrical PON (XGS-PON) is used to backhaul dense RU deployments at the radio edge, while the midhaul uses a ring of Reconfigurable Optical Add Drop Multiplexers (ROADMs) connecting the radio edge to the regional edge. A Software Defined Transport Network (SDTN) controller uses RESTCONF messages to shift unused capacity dynamically from point-to-multipoint (PtMP) segments to point-to-point (PtP) segments according to load. For the shared uplink of a PON used as mobile fronthaul, a 10 km 10G-EPON evaluation has shown that RL-based dynamic bandwidth allocation can keep uplink latency around 200 μs. For sliced O-RAN, a two-stage TWDM-PON tree has also been proposed for the front/mid-haul. RUs reach OLTs through ONUs, with split-7.2 on the RU-DU front-haul and split-2 on the DU-CU mid-haul. Stage-I OLTs terminate the front-haul and, for some slices, the mid-haul, while Stage-II OLTs aggregate the traffic of several Stage-I OLTs toward higher-level CUs or edge clouds, which is particularly efficient for latency-tolerant mMTC. Fiber lengths must stay within TWDM-PON reach limits of about 20 km, and each RU connects to exactly one Stage-I OLT. Planning minimizes the total cost of OLTs, fiber, and processors under latency bounds. Results show that the double-stage design is cheaper than a single stage when server capacities are limited, because CU functions can be aggregated better, and that raising the Stage-II data rate lowers cost only up to a point (N=3). Uplink latencies come out slightly higher than downlink ones because of PON waiting times. Compared with mesh-based Optical Transport Network (OTN) frameworks, the TWDM-PON design saves 21%-28% of the cost, since the tree-and-branch topology needs fewer fiber links and nodes and avoids the ROADM and electrical-switch equipment that a mesh OTN requires.

### Cloud Fog-RAN (CF-RAN) over WDM
<!-- sources: efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran | gen: 3654b43ba2da -->
Cloud Fog-RAN over a Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) transport network organizes the access network into a hierarchy flowing from Cell Sites (CS) to Fog Nodes, then to BBU Hotels, and finally to the Core Central Office (CO). RRHs sit at the cell sites, fog nodes are placed closer to the cell sites than the centralized BBU hotels, and the BBU hotels are located at central offices. The design aims to resolve the tension between the sub-1 ms latency of URLLC and the high fronthaul bandwidth demand of eMBB. Processing is distributed by service type. Type 1 (URLLC) traffic follows fronthaul to a fog node, where it is processed, then backhaul to the core CO. Type 2 and 3 (eMBB and mMTC) traffic follows fronthaul to a BBU hotel, where it is processed, then backhaul to the core CO. Reconfigurable Optical Add-Drop Multiplexers (ROADMs) at fog nodes let eMBB and mMTC wavelengths pass through without processing, so fog nodes handle only the traffic that needs low latency. Slice isolation is obtained by assigning each service type its own wavelengths. Routing relies on pre-computed paths that form virtual links, and the total path delay combines propagation, transmission, interface (dependent on the functional split), and electronic switch delays. Fog nodes and BBU hotels are activated only when needed, so deployment cost is expressed as a weighted sum of active BBU hotels and active fog nodes. Every RRH must be associated with exactly one fog node and one BBU hotel, and a node can serve an RRH only if it is active. In a reference evaluation, a 30-node network (13 CS, 7 CO, 10 fog nodes) over 150 km² used 8 wavelengths of 10 Gbps per fiber. The 3-layer architecture activated fewer nodes for URLLC than a 2-layer C-RAN, improved URLLC fronthaul delay by about 10%, remained feasible under high loads where the 2-layer design found no solution, and, in a 140-node, 5k-request network, showed a 33% reduction in active nodes for URLLC and up to 20% higher request service rates with fewer active BBU hotels. eMBB and mMTC fronthaul delay was similar across the two architectures. Reported future directions include integrating an SDN controller and tuning the cost weight between node types. The same hierarchy can be described in O-RAN terms, with O-RUs at the cell sites, O-DUs hosted on fog nodes, and O-CUs in the cloud. In this formulation, the CF-RAN layer is combined with flexible functional splitting and a queuing-based latency model, and is compared with C-RAN and with CF-RAN without flexible splitting. Reported results show that the flexible-split CF-RAN improves network availability by 35% and reduces power consumption by 9.93% compared with previous CF-RAN studies. The optical transport in this variant is a TWDM-PON-based virtual PON layer rather than pure WDM lightpaths.

Space Division Multiplexing (SDM) uses multicore or few-mode fibers to overcome the capacity limit of single-core fibers by carrying parallel spatial channels in the same fiber. ### Free-Space Optics (FSO) for 6G Fronthaul
<!-- sources: toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: 0211ba60dd12 -->
Free-Space Optics (FSO) transmits data through the atmosphere with lasers, offering high bandwidth (about 100 Gbps) on unlicensed spectrum and avoiding the cost of cabling in remote or hard-to-reach areas. Its use cases include terrestrial towers, non-terrestrial networks (UAVs, high-altitude platforms, and satellites), and underwater communication, which makes it relevant to the space-Earth integration expected of 6G. The main limitation is the need for line of sight combined with atmospheric attenuation from fog and rain, as well as turbulence and beam wander. To reach 99.999% availability, hybrid FSO/mmWave systems switch between the two modalities according to weather, since FSO fails in fog but tolerates rain while mmWave penetrates fog. Research directions for FSO fronthaul include coherent FSO and higher-order modulation with multi-dimensional signaling to reach Tbps levels, and spatial modulation combined with massive MIMO (SM-MIMO) to improve spectral efficiency and mitigate turbulence and beam wander. Robustness improvements aim to make wireless optical links a cheaper alternative to trenching fiber, and adaptive FSO systems are envisioned that reroute traffic autonomously during failures or extreme weather. For security, physical-layer techniques such as Quantum Key Distribution are proposed to prevent eavesdropping on FSO links. In cost-optimized cell-free fronthaul planning, FSO is nevertheless rarely selected because of its cost and lower reliability compared with fiber and mmWave.

### Hybrid Fiber/mmWave/FSO Fronthaul and TCO Optimization
<!-- sources: fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: f4404a45afe9 -->
Fronthaul for cell-free massive MIMO can combine three link technologies: fiber, millimeter-wave (mmWave), and free-space optics (FSO). Combining optical backbones with mmWave or microwave links increases resilience, since the wireless link can act as a backup if a fiber is cut. Hybrid FSO/mmWave architectures are likewise proposed to mitigate weather-induced outages, because FSO fails in fog but works in rain while mmWave penetrates fog; switching between the two according to weather is intended to reach 99.999% availability. Fiber offers reliability but is costly and scales poorly, and the links are modeled as lossless with a constant 10 Gbps capacity using XGS WDM-PON. The mmWave link follows the 3GPP Urban Microcell street-canyon model with separate LoS and NLoS path loss and Gaussian shadowing; the DU uses analog beamforming with quantized phase shifters toward the APs, and capacity follows from the SNR and the available bandwidth. The FSO link is a point-to-point transceiver pair assumed to operate in LoS, with capacity limited by atmospheric effects: visibility-dependent scattering (average visibility 0.4 km), turbulence and scintillation from refractive-index variations, geometric spreading, and rain or fog attenuation. The planning problem is split into two tiers that minimize total cost of ownership (CAPEX + OPEX). Tier 1 covers the shared infrastructure inside each group serving non-leading APs, namely fiber cable per meter and ONU/OADM hardware; it follows from the topology produced by the clustering and NOFAC steps and stays fixed in the second tier. Tier 2 selects the technology for the link between each leading AP and its DU. The fiber cost includes ONU/OADM equipment, cabling, and DU-side OTNs, whose number depends on the splitter ratio Θ; the mmWave cost includes receive hardware at the AP and a massive MIMO antenna array at the DU, incurred only if at least one AP in the group uses mmWave; and the FSO cost aggregates point-to-point deployment and maintenance. Constraints require that each leading AP choose exactly one technology, that the link meet the capacity threshold set by the functional split (FS7.2x or FS8), and that the network satisfy a minimum average availability, the SLA target ζ^SLA. The result is an Integer Linear Program solved with branch-and-bound to global optimality. In numerical results, fiber is chosen for APs near the DUs, mmWave for distant APs where reliability allows, and FSO rarely because of its cost and lower reliability. All-mmWave deployments are the cheapest but are often infeasible because they violate capacity constraints. The hybrid optimized scheme provides substantial surplus capacity at much lower cost than all-fiber networks, and mixed-technology deployments outperform single-technology ones. With non-homogeneous traffic modeled by a Gaussian mixture of hotspots, high-demand regions push the optimizer toward fiber, and links whose demand exceeds every technology's capacity are flagged as infeasible, which indicates where network densification is needed.

### Radio Stripes and Hierarchical Fronthaul Topologies for CF-mMIMO
<!-- sources: fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks | gen: a1597b1d6a16 -->
In cell-free massive MIMO, the APs of a group must be connected to a DU, and two shared-fronthaul topologies have been studied as alternatives to dedicated point-to-point links. In Radio Stripes (RS), APs within a group are chained serially over shared fiber in a bus configuration: a leading AP communicates directly with the DU and forwards data to and from the non-leading APs. Because processing is performed sequentially along the stripe at the AP level, the load on the central CPU is reduced, and the same idea underlies the related Radio Weaves concept. In a converged optical-wireless design, the RUs of a stripe are connected to CF DUs at the radio edge and backhauled over XGS-PON, so the serial approach allows RUs to be scaled cost-effectively. Fragmenting the CF CPU into several DUs supports this arrangement by distributing computation and coordination between RUs and DUs. In the Hierarchical Scheme (HS), the group is connected as a tree rather than a chain, with a leading AP at the root, which removes the single points of failure inherent to the serial layout. Both are built from the same preliminary steps. APs are first grouped by K-Means clustering (KMC), each group being associated with one DU. The clusters are then balanced with Split-Merge Rules (SMR): groups larger than a maximum size g_s are split, and groups smaller than a minimum g_m are merged with neighboring groups, preventing oversized or undersized clusters. For RS, the serial path in each group is chosen to minimize total distance by solving the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), optimally for small groups and with a Nearest Neighbor (NN) approximation for larger ones. For HS, a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST), computed with Prim's or Kruskal's algorithm, minimizes the inter-AP distances, and the leading AP is the node with the highest degree in the tree. The Near-Optimal Fronthaul Association and Configuration (NOFAC) algorithm then iteratively optimizes the DU positions and the selection of leading APs, stopping when the maximum movement of any DU falls below a threshold ε. The topologies differ mainly in resilience. In RS a single link break disconnects every AP downstream in the stripe, producing cascading failures. HS limits the damage because of its branched structure, and increasing the number of groups G, which reduces group size, further reduces failure interdependence and approaches the robustness of point-to-point architectures. Both topologies reduce the average total cost of ownership per AP relative to traditional point-to-point small-cell fronthaul by sharing infrastructure within groups, particularly in centralized deployments with fewer than four DUs, and HS offers a balance of cost efficiency and resilience over RS.

### Limited-Capacity Fronthaul Quantization
<!-- sources: vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: 1eff3794f694 -->
In Cloud-RAN, the digital fronthaul links between the RRHs (edge servers) and the central BBU/central server have limited capacity, so signals received or to be transmitted must be compressed. In the uplink, each edge server quantizes the received superposed signal before forwarding it; in the downlink, the central server quantizes the beamformed signal sent to each edge server. Using rate-distortion theory, this compression is modeled as additive Gaussian quantization noise with a covariance matrix (Q_UL in the uplink, Q_DL in the downlink), and the covariances are constrained by a total fronthaul capacity C. The quantization noise adds to the AirComp aggregation noise and enters the effective uplink and downlink noise terms that determine the optimality gap in federated learning. Quantization is therefore optimized jointly with beamforming rather than set uniformly. In the uplink, with beamforming fixed the quantization problem is convex and is solved with CVX. In the downlink, the capacity constraint is non-convex because of a log-determinant term; a lemma linearizes it into a convex constraint, and the problem is solved by alternate convex search over Q_DL, the transmit beamformer u, and an auxiliary variable Σ. Numerical results show that joint optimization clearly outperforms baselines with uniform quantization and beamforming. At low fronthaul capacity, optimizing quantization yields the largest gain, whereas as capacity grows, optimized beamforming becomes more important.

### eCPRI and Functional Splits for Fronthaul
<!-- sources: efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran, fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: 00d7e4cf0d03 -->
Functional splits define how baseband processing is divided between the radio-side unit and the centralized unit, and thereby determine the fronthaul bandwidth and latency requirements. The fronthaul interface spans several functional layers: RRC (control plane), PDCP (header compression and security), RLC (reliable transmission), MAC (scheduling and HARQ), and PHY (modulation and beamforming). Splits are trade-offs between centralization gains and fronthaul bandwidth. Option 8 (RF/PHY) is fully centralized, giving the lowest RU complexity but the highest bandwidth and the most stringent latency and jitter constraints; high-end configurations can demand more than 800 Gbps with latency below 250 µs. Options 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, and 2 progressively move more PHY/MAC functions to the RU, reducing the fronthaul bitrate while increasing RU complexity, and Option 1 (PDCP/RRC) is the most distributed, with the lowest bandwidth but the highest RU complexity. O-RAN refines Option 7 into a 7.x family: 7.1 carries frequency-domain I/Q symbols and has a high bandwidth requirement; 7.2 carries combined antenna-port signals and needs less bandwidth than 7.1; 7.3 moves modulation/demodulation to the RU, which sharply cuts the bitrate but raises RU complexity. In one example configuration, Option 7.1 requires about 4.3 Gbps whereas 7.3 requires only about 134 Mbps. The choice of split therefore sets both the hardware requirements of the RUs and the capacity needs of the optical transport network, and O-RAN's flexible splitting lets operators tune it to the use case. The Common Public Radio Interface (CPRI), corresponding to 3GPP Split Option 8, transports packet-intensive raw samples and demands very high fronthaul capacity, which is especially burdensome for eMBB traffic. The enhanced CPRI (eCPRI), corresponding to 3GPP Split Option 7, moves part of the physical-layer processing to the radio side, which reduces the required fronthaul bandwidth compared with standard CPRI. In Cloud Fog-RAN over WDM, eCPRI is used for fronthaul traffic so that more requests fit within the capacity of each wavelength. One evaluation considers four eCPRI split options. Split E is the most centralized, with only RF functions in the O-RU, and has the highest bandwidth demand. Split I centralizes part of the PHY, performing FFT and modulation locally, which reduces the bit rate to about 40% of Split E. Split D is a MAC/PHY split in which users are scheduled at fog nodes, lowering bandwidth while increasing latency sensitivity. Split B is the PDCP-RLC split, the most distributed option, which eases latency constraints considerably but limits coordinated features such as CoMP. Bandwidth for each split is computed from the number of antennas, sampling rates, and MIMO layers, and falls drastically from 1966 Mb/s at Split E to 44 Mb/s at Split B. The choice of split point also affects latency: the interface delay depends on the split and is one component of the total fronthaul delay, together with propagation, transmission, and electronic switch delays. The total must stay at or below a per-request-type threshold, so the split interacts with where processing is placed, and selecting the split per O-RU trades latency against energy. Under this scheme, URLLC fronthaul terminates at a fog node near the cell site to meet its strict latency bound, while eMBB and mMTC fronthaul crosses fog-node ROADMs transparently and terminates at a centralized BBU hotel. Other split options appear elsewhere in the RAN literature, for example Option 2 for the high-layer split and Options 6/7 for the low-layer split in O-RAN, trading latency against centralization cost. For cell-free massive MIMO fronthaul planning in O-RAN, two splits are compared. FS8 (PHY-RF split) leaves only RF processing at the AP, so raw time-domain signals must be carried and the required fronthaul capacity is high. FS7.2x (Low-PHY split) shifts some PHY functions to the AP and significantly reduces the required data rate. Capacity thresholds are defined per split, and a control-plane overhead factor α is added proportionally to the user-plane rate. Non-homogeneous, spatially varying traffic raises the demand in hotspot regions. In cost-optimization results FS7.2x is consistently more cost-effective than FS8 and leaves more surplus capacity (headroom for future traffic), which makes it the more scalable split for 6G ultra-dense networks. In an O-RAN-aligned CF architecture, the 7.2 split is adopted for the Open Fronthaul because it balances transport bandwidth against antenna scalability. In sliced O-RAN planning over a TWDM-PON, the splits are fixed as split-7.2 between RU and DU (front-haul) and split-2 between DU and CU (mid-haul), and processing latency is evaluated through an explicit load model. Maximum RU throughput follows from the number of MIMO layers, the modulation order, and the numerology. The total baseband (BBU) processing effort is expressed in Giga Operations Per Second (GOPS) per Transmission Time Interval (TTI) as a function of the number of antennas, modulation bits, and coding rates. For these two splits, the computational load is distributed as 40% at the RU, 50% at the DU, and 10% at the CU. Processing latency then results from comparing the load assigned to each server with its available GOPS, and it is added to the communication latency (propagation and transmission) when checking the front-haul, mid-haul, and BBU processing bounds.

### Flexible Functional Splitting (FFS) for Energy-Latency Trade-off
<!-- sources: energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran | gen: 218a21b36496 -->
Flexible Functional Splitting (FFS) lets operators select, per O-RU, the functional split option dynamically according to available resources and QoS needs, rather than fixing a single split for the whole network. It addresses a basic trade-off: centralizing baseband operations (for example Split E) reduces energy consumption because processing is pooled, but increases fronthaul traffic and latency, whereas dispersing functions toward fog nodes (for example Split B) raises energy usage because more processing nodes are active, but relaxes capacity and latency demands. In the eCPRI-based formulation, four options (E, I, D, B) are considered, with fronthaul bandwidth falling from 1966 Mb/s at Split E to 44 Mb/s at Split B. The split chosen for each O-RU must satisfy the latency bound derived from a queuing model, and the objective is to minimize total power, which includes active processing nodes, line cards, and the chosen splits. In the heuristic, the most centralized split that satisfies the constraints is selected. In a static scenario, flexible splitting reaches nearly 100% availability and is more energy-efficient than CF-RAN without flexible splitting, because it avoids unnecessary fog node activation. In a dynamic 24-hour urban traffic profile (peak load about 120 Gb/s, peak at 2 PM, peak-to-average ratio about 3.14), it adapts the split as load increases and prevents demand blocking, whereas C-RAN blocks demands under latency violations and non-FFS CF-RAN blocks them when fog nodes saturate. Overall gains are reported as 35% higher network availability and 9.93% lower power consumption than previous CF-RAN studies. Frequent split changes introduce signaling and orchestration overhead. Since changes are triggered only by significant load variations, oscillations are considered limited, and hysteresis functions are proposed as future work to reduce the burden further. Centralized features such as CoMP are limited under the more distributed splits. For deployment, the approach is suggested as xApps in the Near-Real-Time RIC or as SMO services, with testing on O-RAN compliant testbeds such as OpenAirInterface and ML-based prediction of configurations as next steps.

### Queuing Delay Models for Fronthaul Latency Bounds (D/D/1, TBAC)
<!-- sources: energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran | gen: b0d7036de4fc -->
Queuing delay models provide the per-split upper bounds on fronthaul latency that feed placement and split-selection optimization, and they are built on the eCPRI standard. The model distinguishes two cases according to the split option. For Splits E and I, traffic is characterized as Constant Bit Rate (CBR) and modeled with D/D/1 queues, so the delay depends on the optical channel capacity and the traffic rate. For Splits B and D, the total delay is the sum of two segments, O-RU to O-DU and O-DU to O-CU. Control and synchronization traffic stay CBR and are again modeled as D/D/1, while user data, which is bursty, is handled with Token Bucket Admission Control (TBAC), which bounds burstiness and applies M/M/1-style logic for peak events. Because user, control, and synchronization connections share the optical channel, the worst-case delay D_max^s for a split s is defined as the maximum of the delays experienced by the three traffic classes. This bound is then used as the latency constraint in the optimization: a split can be chosen for an O-RU only if its worst-case delay satisfies the end-to-end requirement. In an illustrative small example with two fog nodes, one cloud node, and three O-RUs, Split E gives low delay when capacity is high, but the delay grows and the assignment shifts to other splits as the arrival rate λ approaches the service rate μ. In evaluations, this effect explains why fully centralized C-RAN loses availability once Split E latency exceeds about 100 µs under queuing congestion.

## Optimization Methods for Resource Placement

### vBBU Placement and Wavelength Assignment (VB-PWA)
<!-- sources: deep_recurrent_neural_network_for_optical_fronthaul_dimensioning_and_proactive_vbbu_placement_in_cf-ran | gen: d3413afd6ad0 -->
The Virtual BBU Placement and Wavelength Assignment (VB-PWA) problem consists of allocating one vBBU to every active RRH demand while minimizing the number of activated processing nodes, VDUs, and VPONs. It is modeled as a bin-packing problem, in which traffic must be fitted into the fewest resources possible so as to reduce power consumption. The objective function sums the power costs of active nodes (C_n), line cards (C_lc), VDUs (C_vdu), and backplane switches (C_switch). Constraints require that every RRH is mapped to exactly one VDU, one processing node, and one VPON; that bandwidth demands do not exceed VPON capacity (B_w); that processing demands do not exceed node capacity (P_n) or VDU capacity (I_w); and that resources such as nodes and switches are marked active only if they actually host a demand. Operationally, the problem is handled by a centralized workload orchestrator in the cloud that monitors active RRHs, fronthaul capacity, and node utilization. It prioritizes placing vBBUs in the cloud; when cloud capacity is exhausted it activates fog nodes and assigns VPONs, and it deactivates resources when RRHs go offline to save power. Demand that cannot be accommodated by the available resources is blocked. Because the orchestrator must re-solve the problem as traffic changes, the execution time of the solution method determines whether the resulting configuration is still valid when applied.

### Traffic Grooming, Routing and Wavelength Assignment (TGRWA) in WDM Networks
<!-- sources: efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network | gen: bcd20bbb0682 -->
In WDM networks, traffic grooming aggregates multiple lower-rate requests onto shared lightpaths, while routing selects the path and wavelength assignment selects the wavelength for each lightpath. Treated jointly, they form the TGRWA problem. In a Cloud Fog-RAN setting, the model works on pre-computed routed paths that form virtual links between nodes, and decides for each fronthaul and backhaul request which path and wavelength carry it, how many lightpaths are needed per wavelength, and how many fibers are deployed on each link. The main constraints are capacity (the traffic of fronthaul and backhaul requests on a virtual link must not exceed the available wavelength capacity), a limit of K fibers per link, flow constraints that make URLLC traffic terminate at fog nodes and eMBB/mMTC traffic at BBU hotels with backhaul continuing to the core CO, and end-to-end latency bounds D_t defined per request type. Isolation-aware routing is added so that network slices stay separated: each service type is assigned its own wavelengths, and only traffic of the same type may be groomed into the same lightpath. Prior work on C-RAN and RU-DU-CU architectures has studied throughput and energy efficiency, but network slicing in CF-RAN over WDM, in particular slice isolation combined with joint grooming, routing, and wavelength assignment, had received little attention, which is the gap that this formulation targets.

### ILP and LP Relaxation for Network Resource Placement
<!-- sources: deep_recurrent_neural_network_for_optical_fronthaul_dimensioning_and_proactive_vbbu_placement_in_cf-ran, efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran, fronthaul_network_planning_for_hierarchical_and_radio-stripes-enabled_cf-mmimo_in_o-ran, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran | gen: cef563fa1a4b -->
Integer Linear Programming (ILP) is the exact approach to resource placement problems such as VB-PWA: it yields optimal solutions but scales poorly, and for large networks some loads took over an hour to solve, so the configuration may be outdated by the time it is computed. ILP also consumes the most CPU and memory, both growing sharply with network size. The same holds for placement of BBU hotels and fog nodes in Cloud Fog-RAN over WDM, which is formulated as an ILP with binary vectors for BBU hotel and fog node activation, binary association matrices between RRHs and fog nodes or BBUs, routing indicators for fronthaul and backhaul requests, lightpath counts per wavelength, and fiber counts per link. The objective minimizes a weighted sum of active BBU hotels and active fog nodes, Z = α·ΣB + (1−α)·ΣX, subject to association, activation, fiber-count, capacity, latency, and routing-flow constraints. The problem is NP-hard, which motivates heuristics for larger scales. It was solved with AMPL and CPLEX, whose Branch & Bound algorithm served as the optimal reference against which a greedy heuristic was compared. Varying α shifts the priority: lower α favors fewer fog nodes, higher α favors fewer BBU hotels, and α = 0.5 balances the two. A different ILP formulation targets power minimization with joint selection of the functional split and placement. Its objective is the sum of the costs of active processing nodes, line cards, and chosen splits. Its constraints require that each O-RU be associated with exactly one split option, that VPON bandwidth and node processing capacities not be exceeded, that processing nodes and VPONs be active only if they host demands, and that the split selected for an RU satisfy the latency bound derived from a queuing model. This ILP gives optimal solutions but the number of decision variables grows exponentially with network size: the solver fails, from memory or time limits, for networks above about 60 O-RUs, and runtimes reached up to 30,000 s, compared with under 1 second for a graph-based heuristic. A further ILP addresses fronthaul technology selection for the leading APs of a cell-free massive MIMO network. Each leading AP must choose exactly one of fiber, mmWave, or free-space optics, subject to capacity thresholds and a minimum average availability, with the objective of minimizing total cost of ownership. The binary technology-selection variables and the hardware required at the DUs are solved to global optimality with branch-and-bound. Over 500 random deployment realizations, this optimized approach shows a lower median cost and narrower variance than heuristic methods, and the advantage grows as the number of DUs increases, indicating that joint optimization matters most in complex, decentralized deployments. For sliced O-RAN with a TWDM-PON front/mid-haul, planning is split into two ILPs solved in sequence. The first (P1) associates UEs with RUs and minimizes the number of installed RUs and the total over-the-air latency, subject to a coverage constraint (each UE within a maximum distance of its RU), an association constraint (each UE connects to exactly one active RU), and a latency/throughput constraint (propagation plus transmission latency not above the slice-specific OTA bound). The second (P2) minimizes the total installation cost of OLTs, fiber, and processors while placing DU and CU functions and designing the front/mid-haul. Its constraints keep fiber lengths within TWDM-PON limits of about 20 km, connect each RU to one Stage-I OLT, allow DUs at the RU or at Stage-I OLTs and CUs at Stage-I or Stage-II OLTs, and bound both communication latency and processing latency (computational load against the GOPS available in each server). Both problems are NP-hard, so heuristics are used at scale, with the IBM Gurobi solver providing the optimal benchmark. Linear Programming (LP) relaxation improves scalability by replacing binary variables with continuous or semi-continuous ones, solving the resulting problem in polynomial time, and then applying a rounding function to return to an integer solution. This can yield slightly higher costs than pure ILP and, in general, may produce infeasible solutions after relaxation and rounding, but in static evaluations of VB-PWA the gap between LP relaxation and ILP was near zero, confirming LP as a viable near-optimal alternative. Its runtime is much lower than that of ILP but higher than that of the ML-based heuristic. Related work has also used LP relaxation for large-scale networks, though often for other optimizations such as caching or sub-carrier allocation. The three approaches (ILP, LP relaxation, and ML-based prediction) are thus positioned along a trade-off between optimality, execution time, and resource consumption.

### Min-Cost Max-Flow Graph Formulation for Function Placement
<!-- sources: energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran | gen: ce911b242832 -->
A scalable alternative to the ILP for placing baseband functions and choosing the split models the network as a directed flow network G = (V, E). Vertices represent the processing levels (O-RU, then Fog, then Cloud) together with the specific baseband functions, and edges are annotated with costs, corresponding to latency, and capacities, corresponding to bandwidth. Placement is then cast as a minimum-cost maximum-flow problem. A no-backtracking constraint on function indexing keeps the chain consistent with the protocol stack: if function f is processed at level i, every subsequent function g > f must be processed at a level j ≥ i, so processing can move from the O-RU toward the cloud but never back. The on-line heuristic built on this graph has four steps. First, initialization directs all traffic to the Cloud. Second, redistribution shifts flow to Fog nodes when Cloud capacity or latency constraints are violated. Third, split selection picks the most centralized split that satisfies the constraints. Fourth, final routing forwards the processed traffic to the sink node. Its complexity is O(|V|·|E|² + |S|·m), where S is the set of split options, so it runs in milliseconds regardless of network size, while the ILP fails beyond about 60 O-RUs and can take up to 30,000 s. Results closely match the ILP optimum in the static scenario, with execution under 1 second, though a slight degradation in solution quality occurs under extreme constraints. The heuristic is described as the only practical option for real-time, large-scale deployment.

### Greedy Heuristics for Service-Aware Node Activation (SB-FAA)
<!-- sources: efficient_network_slicing_for_5g_services_in_cloud_fog-ran_deployment_over_wdm_network, energy-efficient_flexible_functional_splitting_in_latency-constrained_o-ran, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran | gen: a72fcc4a7661 -->
The Service-aware BBU hotel and Fog node Activation Algorithm (SB-FAA) is a greedy heuristic for the NP-hard node activation problem in Cloud Fog-RAN over WDM. It prioritizes minimizing the number of active nodes while limiting request blockage, and processes requests one at a time according to their service type. For eMBB and mMTC, it checks whether the source RRH is already associated with an active BBU hotel; if not, it searches for reachable central offices that satisfy the delay and capacity constraints and activates a BBU hotel only if necessary. For URLLC, it first tries to route through central offices that are already active, if latency permits; otherwise it looks for fog nodes near the RRH that can be activated to handle the request. Its computational complexity, counted in FLOPS, is approximately O(R·max{(10N²)B, 8B + 7F}), where R is the number of requests, N the number of nodes, B the number of BBU hotels, and F the number of fog nodes, which is far lower than exhaustive search. Implementation used Python with Networkx, while the ILP was solved with AMPL and CPLEX. Evaluation on a larger network of 140 nodes and 5k requests showed a 33% reduction in active nodes for URLLC versus 2-layer C-RAN, up to 20% higher request service rates with fewer active BBU hotels, and similar fronthaul delay for eMBB and mMTC, with clear URLLC gains in the 3-layer setup. Compared with the Branch & Bound solution of the ILP, the heuristic performed near-optimally. Convergence was measured by the ratio c of active nodes to requests served; c drops sharply as the request index grows, indicating that a high share of requests is served with only a small increase in active nodes. Greedy node activation is one of several scalable alternatives to the ILP. A graph-based heuristic for flexible functional splitting, built on a minimum-cost maximum-flow formulation, has complexity O(|V|·|E|² + |S|·m), where S is the set of split options, runs in milliseconds, and tracks the ILP solution closely, while the ILP can need hours. A greedy heuristic of the same family solves the DU/CU placement and front/mid-haul design problem of sliced O-RAN over TWDM-PON. It iteratively activates OLTs and associates RUs with them by minimum distance, verifying at each step that all communication latency (propagation and transmission) and processing latency constraints are met. For each RU it compares the cost of placing the DU at the RU location with the cost of placing it at an OLT location and selects the cheaper option. A theorem establishes that this heuristic is an O(log_e(O × ΣB_s)) approximation to the optimal solution, a logarithmic guarantee on cost, where O is the number of OLTs and B_s the bandwidth demand of slice s.

### Lagrangian Relaxation and Sub-gradient Heuristics
<!-- sources: optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran | gen: f2bad8fa93a8 -->
Lagrangian relaxation is a decomposition technique for hard optimization problems in which a set of complicating constraints is removed and moved into the objective with penalty weights, the Lagrange multipliers. For a minimization problem, the relaxed problem is easier to solve and its optimal value is a lower bound (LB) on the optimum of the original problem. The multipliers are then tuned to tighten this bound, commonly with the sub-gradient method, which updates them iteratively according to the violation of the relaxed constraints. In sliced O-RAN planning, the technique is applied to the UE-RU association problem, an integer program that minimizes the number of installed RUs and the total over-the-air latency and is NP-hard. The constraint that each UE connect to exactly one RU is relaxed to obtain the lower bound on cost. A separate heuristic then searches for a feasible solution, which gives an upper bound (UB). The sub-gradient method adjusts the Lagrange multipliers at each iteration until the LB and UB converge or a maximum number of iterations is reached, which yields near-optimal solutions together with an analyzable optimality gap. In evaluations across industrial, urban, and rural scenarios, the Lagrangian heuristic (LgR) produced results very close to those of the IBM Gurobi optimal solver while being significantly faster on large datasets, which makes it a scalable alternative to exact ILP solving. The same bound-based approach contrasts with greedy and graph-based heuristics, which offer speed but need separate arguments for solution quality.

### Alternating Optimization for Non-Convex Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: closed-loop_uplink_radio_resource_management_in_cf-o-ran_empowered_5g_aerial_corridor, decentralized_optimization_of_spectral_efficiency_for_scalable_cf-ran_with_network-assisted_free-duplex, vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: b97e8c0ad22f -->
Alternating optimization (AO) tackles non-convex joint resource allocation problems by splitting the variables into blocks and optimizing each block while the others are held fixed, repeating until the objective stops improving. It is used when the joint problem, often mixed-integer and NP-hard, has no tractable direct solution. In a cell-free O-RAN uplink max-min SE problem, the joint problem is decoupled into a UAV-to-O-RU association sub-problem, solved by a low-complexity three-stage heuristic, and a transmit power sub-problem, solved by bisection-guided fixed-point power control. The procedure starts from maximum transmit power and alternates between the two. Convergence follows from monotonic improvement of an objective that is bounded above, and in practice occurs within about 3–5 iterations. Compared with interior-point solvers such as CVX applied to the power problem (O(K^3.5)), the fixed-point approach has O(K²) complexity and reached the same minimum SE while cutting runtime by up to 99.1%, which brings the solution within the 10–150 ms budget of a near-RT RIC xApp. A distributed variant, partial distributed block coordinate descent (PDBCD), applies the same block-wise principle to transceiver design in a cell-free RAN with Edge Distributed Units (EDUs). Uplink receivers are updated with a team-MMSE scheme, and downlink precoders use a serial-parallel update: transmitting APs are updated serially within each EDU, while different EDUs update in parallel through the CCU. Because simultaneous parallel updates can fluctuate, a Jacobi best-response smoothing step stabilizes the iterations. PDBCD converges quickly, typically within 2–3 outer-loop iterations, and the variant combined with greedy duplex selection (GPDBCD-FULL) reaches sum-rates within 3 bps/Hz of the centralized upper bound. AO has also been applied to over-the-air federated learning in Cloud-RAN, where the objective is to minimize the convergence optimality gap under fronthaul capacity and transmit power constraints. The problem decomposes into independent uplink and downlink sub-problems. In the uplink, receive beamforming and quantization covariance are optimized alternately: beamforming is cast as a min-max problem that minimizes the worst-case noise across devices and is solved by successive convex approximation (SCA), which converts complex variables to the real domain and linearizes concave functions, while the quantization step is convex given fixed beamforming and is solved with CVX. In the downlink, the non-convex capacity constraint is convexified by linearizing a log-determinant term, and an alternate convex search (ACS) cycles through the quantization covariance, the transmit beamformer, and an auxiliary variable. The resulting algorithm shows linear convergence of the optimality gap in simulation. AO therefore serves as a practical route to sub-second, deployable resource management, though it generally guarantees convergence to a stationary solution rather than a global optimum of the joint problem.

### Traditional Resource Allocation Methods in C-RAN
<!-- sources: resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: ac2cf809f49c -->
Resource allocation (RA) in C-RAN aims to assign radio and computational resources so that QoS requirements are met while system capacity and energy efficiency are maximized. Conventional research emphasizes power distribution, user association, and virtual machine placement to enhance capacity and reduce latency, and it is typically formulated as mathematical optimization that requires precise channel state information. Joint tuning of radio and computational resources targets latency by optimizing RF and computing resources together: examples include offloading resource-intensive tasks from mobile devices to cloud servers with successive convex approximation in distributed frameworks, and integrating MEC with blockchain so that energy consumption and time-to-finality are balanced by decoupling the optimization variables for user association, data rates, and computational RA. Virtualization-based resource management pools and shares resources across virtual networks using a Virtual Base Station (VBS) pool and Remote Radio Heads (RRHs), with inter-operator cooperation schemes (IOCS) built on trusted computing platforms and auction-based methods that promote truthful sharing and social welfare among operators. QoS-aware RA for delay-sensitive applications uses cooperative game theory with weight-based policies to ensure fairness across baseband units, and Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Discrete Particle Swarm Optimization (DPSO) for load balancing by dynamically re-mapping RRHs to baseband unit sectors. Energy-efficiency schemes minimize power consumption through sleep-mode optimization, base-station clustering, and optimized BBU-RRH mapping combined with user association. A recurring limitation is that these model-based and heuristic approaches depend on accurate channel information and do not scale well with the heterogeneity and dynamics of 5G networks, which is the main motivation cited for learning-based alternatives such as deep reinforcement learning.

## Deep Reinforcement Learning for RAN

### Deep Reinforcement Learning for Wireless Resource Management
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks, colo-ran_developing_machine_learning-based_xapps_for_open_ran_closed-loop_control_on_programmable_experimental_platforms, deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, deep_recurrent_neural_network_for_optical_fronthaul_dimensioning_and_proactive_vbbu_placement_in_cf-ran, machine_learning-based_cell-free_support_in_the_o-ran_architecture_an_innovative_converged_optical-wireless_solution_toward_6g_networks, machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: 5914cf082b15 -->
Reinforcement learning is preferred over supervised or unsupervised learning for wireless resource management because agents learn policies through interaction with a dynamic environment, without labeled optimal decisions. Traditional resource allocation (RA) relies on mathematical optimization that needs precise channel state information (CSI) and often fails to scale with the heterogeneity of 5G; deep reinforcement learning (DRL) instead learns decision-making policies directly from data without human intervention. DRL combines deep neural networks as function approximators with RL, in which an agent maximizes a cumulative reward through observations, a policy, and a discount factor that balances immediate and future rewards. It suits wireless networks because it can adapt to non-stationary conditions such as interference and mobility and can handle tasks like spectrum management that are too complex to program explicitly. In Cloud-RAN (C-RAN), RA aims to meet QoS requirements while maximizing capacity and energy efficiency. The literature applies deep RL widely: DQN and DDPG for power allocation to maximize sum-rate, Q-learning and actor-critic methods for base-station sleep modes under QoS constraints, RL and DNNs for mmWave beam selection under blockage, and combinations of DNNs and RL for dynamic resource scheduling. Research on 6G RAN slicing frequently applies DQN, Double DQN, and DDPG to optimize resource allocation and QoS. DRL has also been identified as a direction beyond supervised proactive schemes: an LSTM-based forecaster paired with a polynomial-time heuristic for vBBU placement and optical fronthaul dimensioning in CF-RAN performs near the ILP optimum, and Deep Reinforcement Learning is proposed as future work to improve on it by learning allocation policies directly rather than relying on a separate prediction step. In cell-free networks, Q-learning has been used to optimize the set of serving RUs for each Physical Resource Block (PRB). In a simulation of 64 RUs and 10 UEs at 4 GHz, this was evaluated with Round Robin (SISO), Maximum Ratio Transmission (MRT), and Zero Forcing (ZF) precoders. CF ZF reached the highest system spectral efficiency at 89.4 Mb/s/Hz, compared with 74.3 Mb/s/Hz for CF MRT and 58.6 Mb/s/Hz for SISO Round Robin. Regularized Zero Forcing (RZF) and Deep RL for MRT were identified as potential areas for further improvement. RL is also applied to optical access: in PON-based mobile fronthaul, a Q-learning agent defines its reward as the negative latency at the ONU-RU and iteratively updates a Q-value function that associates specific uplink grant sizes with rewards, so that the grant for each unit is learned without prior knowledge of traffic patterns. A common criticism is that most of these works target a specific problem or use simplified network models that lack the architectural entities needed to host ML algorithms in a real deployment, for example where agents would run and how they would exchange information. Reviews of DRL in C-RAN describe a systematic implementation pipeline: defining the problem, designing the state, action, and reward spaces, selecting an algorithm, implementing a simulation environment, training and fine-tuning hyperparameters, and finally evaluating and deploying. Network dynamics are handled through online learning, and scalability is needed for real-time decisions in large networks. Open directions include more realistic modeling of the interplay between base stations, CPUs, and fronthaul, reward design that weighs spectral efficiency against energy, new exploration-exploitation strategies, feasibility studies on real C-RAN hardware, and Multi-Agent RL (MARL) for collaborative optimization across heterogeneous devices. In O-RAN, RL agents are deployed at the near-RT RIC and interact with the RAN environment (O-RU, O-DU, O-CU) as a Markov Decision Process: actions are sent over the E2 interface, while state and reward updates return via the O1 interface. In DQN implementations, a prediction network is updated iteratively while a separate target network generates stable target values to minimize the loss. Multi-agent RL (MARL) is considered necessary when several decision-makers act simultaneously, as in 6G resource management, though ensuring that distributed multi-agent RL converges quickly at scale is an open challenge, with bootstrapping techniques suggested for stabilization. Practical designs therefore tie the learning agents to standardized architecture (such as O-RAN RIC components) and choose algorithms to fit the action space: value-based methods with discrete actions for slice selection and methods suited to continuous actions for resource block allocation. Implementations are commonly built with Python, PyTorch, and OpenAI Gym environments. Adoption of DRL in the RAN is hindered by five challenges: the difficulty of collecting large-scale datasets that capture real-world randomness, the lack of scale for testing robustness without risking outages, unreliable or inconsistent telemetry from production systems, limited generalization to unseen configurations, and high input dimensionality given hundreds of KPMs. Large-scale experiments with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) actor-critic xApps on a 49-node network, evaluated on both an emulator and an indoor testbed, illustrate several design effects. Joint control of slicing (PRB allocation) and scheduling outperformed fixed slicing with adaptive scheduling, particularly for users in the lower throughput percentiles. Constraining the action space (a reduced-action variant) biased the learned policy, and removing certain configurations significantly degraded URLLC performance. Agents that encoded their state with autoencoders outperformed those without, because the latent representation gave a more stable mapping from network state to actions when telemetry was inconsistent. Three xApp variants were studied: sched-slicing, which jointly controls PRBs and scheduling with a reward combining eMBB rate, MTC packets, and URLLC buffer size; sched, which selects a per-slice scheduling policy with slice-specific rewards; and online-training, which fine-tunes pre-trained weights through live exploration.

### Value-Based and Policy-Gradient DRL Algorithm Families (DQN, DDPG, TD3, SAC, A3C)
<!-- sources: resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: 6499413eb5a1 -->
DRL algorithms applied to resource allocation in 5G C-RAN fall into value-based and policy-based families, and the choice is largely driven by whether the action space is discrete or continuous. Deep Q-network (DQN) uses a neural network to approximate the Q-function, estimating long-term reward for state-action pairs, and relies on experience replay and Temporal Difference (TD) learning based on the Bellman equation. It suits discrete actions and has been applied to autonomous cellular function activation in Heterogeneous C-RAN (H-CRAN), to optimizing IRS-enhanced OFDM systems, and to resource orchestration for IIoT devices; its computational complexity is approximately O(n·m·t), with n parameters, m iterations, and t time per iteration. Policy gradient methods instead optimize the mapping from states to actions directly, which makes them more effective for continuous action spaces and for managing intricate environments by optimizing long-term benefit from past experience, with complexity O(N·T·d). Actor-critic methods are hybrids in which an actor selects actions and a critic evaluates them; they have been used for computation offloading in MEC systems and for user scheduling in HetNets with hybrid energy sources, with complexity generally O(k·n). Asynchronous advantage actor-critic (A3C) runs multiple independent agents learning in parallel and asynchronously, using an advantage function to measure how much better an action is than average; it has been applied to energy-efficient RAN slicing with SBiLSTM networks and to improving Quality of Experience for video streaming over software-defined mobile networks integrated with MEC. Deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) is designed for continuous control with a deterministic actor; applications include dynamic computation offloading in Fog Access Points (F-APs) using federated learning to protect privacy, and the DeepRAT framework for joint RAT assignment and power allocation in HetNets, with complexity O(n^2). Twin delayed DDPG (TD3) improves on DDPG by using two critics to reduce Q-value overestimation and by adding noise to the target policy to encourage exploration. It has outperformed model-based methods in IoRT power allocation, LEO satellite MsgA channel allocation, and massive MTC service scheduling (via the MPMA-TD3 algorithm), also with complexity O(n^2). Soft actor-critic (SAC) maximizes both expected reward and policy entropy to promote exploration, and has been used for cooperative computation offloading across edge-cloud and edge-edge servers, dynamic resource scheduling in BAC-NOMA IoT networks, and mixed discrete-continuous action spaces for energy harvesting. Proximal policy optimization (PPO), the remaining major family member, is treated separately. In summary, DQN-style value methods handle discrete decisions such as slice or RAT selection, while DDPG, TD3, SAC, and policy-gradient variants handle continuous decisions such as power and resource block allocation.

### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
<!-- sources: colo-ran_developing_machine_learning-based_xapps_for_open_ran_closed-loop_control_on_programmable_experimental_platforms, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: 67357f6441b4 -->
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a policy-gradient reinforcement learning algorithm that uses an actor-critic architecture: an actor network outputs the policy and a critic network estimates the value used to compute advantages. Its clipped, constrained policy updates restrict the size of each step so that the new policy stays close to the previous one, preventing drastic deviations and supporting stable and unbiased policy learning. Its computational complexity is reported as O(n). In C-RAN resource allocation it has been used for multi-objective RA across various service types, for coordinating swarm robotics in automated warehouses via Coordinated Multipoint (CoMP) clustering, and for UAV-based QoS management during disasters. These properties also motivated its use as the DRL algorithm for O-RAN xApps performing RAN slicing and scheduling control. In the reported implementation, both actor and critic use five fully connected layers of 30 neurons with tanh activations, and they take as input the latent state produced by an autoencoder (a size-3 vector). Agents are trained offline on data from multiple base stations to promote generalization, and can later be fine-tuned online. The xApps built on PPO include variants that jointly control PRB allocation and scheduling policy, with rewards that combine eMBB rate, MTC packet counts, and URLLC buffer size, and variants that select only per-slice scheduling policies.

### Normalized Advantage Function DQL (NAF)
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: 573a2c2d2ade -->
Normalized Advantage Function deep Q-learning (NAF DQL) extends Q-learning to continuous action spaces by decomposing the Q-function into a state-value term and an advantage term that is quadratic in the action, so the maximizing action is available in closed form without a separate actor network. It retains the stability of Q-learning with experience replay while handling continuous actions. It is used for the low-level stage, where resource block allocation requires a continuous action space. One single agent is trained per service type (five in total), each tasked with realizing the QoS of its assigned slice type, and the reward is granted when all predefined KPIs for that service type fall within their required ranges. Training uses a decaying epsilon strategy, exploring first and moving to exploitation using the replay memory. In reported simulations all five agents showed loss convergence after about 6,000 epochs. NAF is one of several ways to handle continuous actions: surveys of DRL in C-RAN instead rely on actor-based methods such as DDPG, TD3 (which uses twin critics to curb Q-value overestimation), and SAC (which adds an entropy term), whereas NAF keeps a single value-based network and obtains the continuous action analytically.

### Multi-Agent RL with QMIX
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks | gen: d784bce2f6e4 -->
QMIX is a cooperative MARL algorithm based on centralized training with decentralized execution: each agent keeps its own policy acting on local observations, while a mixing network combines the agents' individual Q-values into a joint value during training. This avoids the exponential growth of the joint action space as agents are added, which supports scalability. It is used for the high-level slice-assignment problem, where agents choose a discrete slice type (0 to 4, one per service type) for incoming requests, with a state including available service types and KPIs. The reward combines individual and team terms: R_tot,i = (1/3) R_agent,i + (2/3) R_team. The individual reward is binary and depends on resource availability, while the team reward is the ratio of resources in use to total available resources, so agents are pushed to maximize overall utilization (network capacity) cooperatively. In reported simulations, loss converged at about 5,000 episodes and rewards followed an ascending linear trend.

### Hierarchical Two-Level RL for Slicing and Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: a_multi-level_deep_rl-based_network_slicing_and_resource_management_for_o-ran-based_6g_cell-free_networks | gen: 27aab12411ec -->
A hierarchical scheme decomposes the slicing and resource management problem into two levels to reduce complexity, with the goal of maximizing total network capacity while guaranteeing QoS across slice types. The high level is a cooperative MARL stage located in the non-RT RIC (within the SMO). It observes global network status and assigns slice types to requests on a long time slot, T_L > 1 s, keeping assignments consistent and synchronized. The low level consists of single-agent RL located in the near-RT RIC; it receives the assignments and allocates resource blocks on a short time slot, T_S between 10 ms and 1 s. Low-level agents operate locally without needing the global network state. Because the action space of the high-level learner does not grow exponentially with the number of agents and communication occurs mainly over the A1 interface, the design limits overhead and scales. Proposed extensions include clustering of communication links in the low-level logic, agents that handle all service types instead of one, and testing other DRL algorithms and network environments.

### Online Training and Fine-Tuning of DRL xApps
<!-- sources: colo-ran_developing_machine_learning-based_xapps_for_open_ran_closed-loop_control_on_programmable_experimental_platforms, deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial | gen: 0c152ef02596 -->
Online training fine-tunes DRL agents that were pre-trained offline by letting them explore on the live RAN, rather than relying only on a fixed dataset. In the general ML literature the approach is described as offline pre-training followed by continuous real-time updates during deployment; it is space and time efficient but requires robust mechanisms to detect and adapt to concept drift. It addresses a gap in the usual O-RAN workflow, where training is performed offline at the non-RT RIC and only inference runs in the near-RT RIC; the proposed direction is real-time learning within near-RT RIC xApps. In evaluations on the Colosseum emulator and the Arena indoor testbed (with commercial smartphones), agents adapted relatively quickly to environment changes, such as a switch from slice-based to uniform traffic. When traffic profiles shift or the environment differs from the original dataset, online-trained agents significantly outperform offline-only models. The approach has costs. Exploration is sequential on the real system, so it takes more wall-clock time than offline training, which can be parallelized across collected data. The exploration phase also causes temporary throughput degradation and high performance variability, a risk for production networks. Convergence and scalability of distributed multi-agent RL as the network grows are further open concerns. The reported conclusion is that online training enables environment-specific adaptation but should be run behind a safety buffer, such as an emulator or testbed, so that service is not degraded during exploration. The fine-tuned agents are deployed as xApps in the near-RT RIC, where the closed control loop (roughly 114 ms roundtrip in the reported setup) fits near-RT timescales.

### Autoencoders and KPM Feature Selection for RAN Telemetry
<!-- sources: colo-ran_developing_machine_learning-based_xapps_for_open_ran_closed-loop_control_on_programmable_experimental_platforms | gen: 4b9a5cafc8cd -->
O-RAN base stations expose more than 400 key performance measurements (KPMs), and feeding all of them to a learning agent invites the curse of dimensionality. Correlation analysis on a large collected dataset showed that many KPMs are redundant; for example, downlink symbols and transport blocks (TBs) are highly correlated. To reduce dimensionality without losing information, the selected inputs for the DRL agents were buffer occupancy (or the ratio of PRBs granted to PRBs requested), number of TBs, and downlink rate. Autoencoders add a second layer of reduction. The encoder, with four fully connected layers using ReLU activations, compresses the input KPM matrix into a latent vector of size 3, which serves as the agent's state. This mitigates outliers and noise and gives a compact representation of high-dimensional RAN data. Autoencoders are trained with random zero-padding of inputs so that they remain robust when telemetry entries are missing, a common situation with inconsistent reporting from production systems. Experiments found that agents using the autoencoder outperformed agents without it, because the latent state provided a more stable mapping between network conditions and actions, especially when telemetry was incomplete. The general lesson drawn is that data-driven correlation analysis is needed for feature selection, and that autoencoders handle missing or noisy data while shrinking the input.

## ML for Optical and PON Network Management

### LSTM Traffic Forecasting for Proactive Resource Allocation
<!-- sources: deep_recurrent_neural_network_for_optical_fronthaul_dimensioning_and_proactive_vbbu_placement_in_cf-ran | gen: b8c41cdf8ecb -->
Deep Recurrent Neural Networks, in particular Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, are used to forecast traffic so that resources can be allocated proactively rather than reactively, decoupling the operational timescale from incoming traffic fluctuations. LSTM memory cells and gating mechanisms can capture non-stationary patterns in time series. In CF-RAN, a multivariate LSTM trained with Mean Absolute Error (MAE) as the loss function and ReLU activation predicts the network load for the next hour, reaching an R² score of 0.95. The predictions drive a polynomial-time resizing heuristic: predicted traffic is first allocated to the cloud node, the remainder is placed on fog nodes if cloud capacity is exceeded, and any demand that still cannot be accommodated is blocked. Evaluated with the 5GPy simulator, one-hour-ahead prediction gives an MAE of 1.19 Gbps, compatible with ILP optimality. Error grows significantly with the length of the prediction window, leading to outdated data, suboptimal allocation, and increased blocking, so multi-step forecasting trades accuracy against how far in advance decisions are made. The heuristic has linear time complexity and is more than 99.99% faster than ILP, keeps memory usage stable at about 44%, and under business-area traffic patterns showed energy consumption and node activation rates statistically equivalent to the ILP optimum with much lower CPU intensity.

### Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA) in PON with RL and Transfer Learning
<!-- sources: machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial, optical_front-mid-haul_with_open_access-edge_server_deployment_framework_for_sliced_o-ran, toward_6g_optical_fronthaul_a_survey_on_enabling_technologies_and_research_perspectives | gen: a3f9e8425687 -->
In a PON used as mobile fronthaul (MFH), the uplink bandwidth is shared among ONUs attached to radio units, and the dynamic bandwidth allocation (DBA) scheme decides the grant size (T_grant) each unit receives per cycle. In TDM-PON, the processing delay of DBA is the main obstacle to meeting microsecond-level fronthaul latency, which motivates AI-driven DBA that can predict traffic spikes and adjust bandwidth in real time. The same predictive approach is used for energy saving, where AI-driven DBA lets ONUs enter sleep states during low traffic. The Fast Self-Adaptive DBA (FSA-DBA) scheme aims to optimize T_grant without prior knowledge of traffic patterns, to support immersive human-to-machine communications in next-generation fronthaul. Its self-adaptive mechanism uses reinforcement learning in which the reward R is the negative latency at the ONU-RU, and a Q-value function is updated iteratively to associate specific grants with rewards, optimizing the decision for each unit. Because standard RL converges slowly, fast exploration is added through transfer learning: the system identifies the pre-existing source knowledge set (Q_source) most similar to the current target environment and restricts the search space for T_grant to a neighborhood around that known optimum. In simulations of a 10 km 10G-EPON with exponential, generalized Pareto, and gamma traffic distributions, FSA-DBA achieved the lowest uplink latency, about 200 μs, outperforming both baseline and cooperative DBA schemes. RL alone can eventually find the optimal decision, but adding transfer learning reduces the required number of iterations from hundreds to a few tens. A non-learning alternative is the cooperative mobile-DBA (M-DBA), which exploits the coupling between the radio scheduler and the PON. The DU predicts the future uplink traffic load from the scheduling requests of the UEs and informs the OLT in advance, so the OLT can generate bandwidth maps that minimize the waiting time between RU and DU instead of reacting to buffer reports. This reduces PON upstream latency to meet front-haul requirements and is combined with 5G NR mini-slots and grant-free uplink transmission to support uRLLC in sliced O-RAN. In planning studies using this scheme, uplink latencies remain slightly higher than downlink ones because of PON waiting times, yet all front-haul and mid-haul latency bounds are met.

### Transfer Learning for Network Management
<!-- sources: machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: 4dd8dba0848f -->
Transfer learning re-uses parameters from a source model trained on one task to optimize a target model on a different but related task, reducing the data and training time needed on the target. It is considered one mitigation for the lack of representative data in deployed networks, alongside GAN-based augmentation, active learning, and online learning. In DRL for C-RAN resource allocation, it is proposed as a remedy for scalability problems, since training requires large amounts of data and grows costlier as networks scale: pre-trained models can be reused across network scenarios to accelerate training. It is typically listed together with regularization to avoid overfitting and hierarchical learning to split complex problems. In optical networks it is particularly useful for failure detection, where failure data are scarce, and for reducing the training time of equalizers in short-reach optical links. It has also been applied to speed up reinforcement learning: in PON dynamic bandwidth allocation for mobile fronthaul, the system selects the stored source Q-knowledge set most similar to the current environment and restricts the search for the uplink grant size to a neighborhood around that optimum. This cuts the iterations needed for convergence from hundreds to a few tens, enabling fast self-adaptation to changing traffic.

## Distributed and Federated Learning

### Federated Learning in O-RAN
<!-- sources: deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial, resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review, vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: aa224b44941c -->
Federated Learning (FL) is presented as a natural fit for the distributed nature of O-RAN because raw data need not be centralized. In the described supervised-learning deployment, local models are trained at the O-RU level, their weights are aggregated at the non-RT RIC via the O1 interface, and the resulting global model is deployed to near-RT RIC xApps. Decisions are then pushed to the O-DU and O-CU over the E2 interface. FL is also considered important for privacy in multi-operator environments, where operators cannot share raw data. More generally, FL is proposed as a defense for data security and privacy because raw data stay local and only model parameters are shared, which mitigates risks such as manipulation of training data or extraction attacks. It is listed among the privacy-preserving techniques for DRL in C-RAN, together with differential privacy and secure multi-party computation. An example is DDPG-based dynamic computation offloading in Fog Access Points (F-APs), where federated learning protects privacy by avoiding the sharing of raw data. In optical access networks, FL is supported by bandwidth slicing in the PON, which reserves capacity for transmitting model parameters between the participating nodes and the aggregator. Reported results show that leveraging knowledge shared across the federation reduces convergence time by up to 80% in some resource allocation scenarios. FL has also been proposed to cope with changing environments, since comparing model parameter updates across the federation can reveal concept drift. For network slicing, the RIC databases must be partitioned to keep slices isolated, and distributed FL is suggested as a way to do this while still learning across slices. A related open direction is shifting from offline training at the non-RT RIC toward online learning within near-RT RIC xApps while preserving privacy. FL has also been extended to the vertical setting over Cloud-RAN, where devices hold feature-partitioned data rather than disjoint samples. In this vertical variant each device owns a unique sub-model of the global model, so all devices must participate in every round, and the volume of intermediate outputs exchanged grows in proportion to the number of training samples, creating a communication bottleneck. Cloud-RAN with over-the-air computation is proposed to address it: distributed RRHs/edge servers shorten the distance to devices with poor channels (the stragglers), and a central BBU pool coordinates aggregation over capacity-limited fronthaul.

### Vertical Federated Learning
<!-- sources: vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: 688323d1f461 -->
Vertical Federated Learning (VFL) lets devices collaboratively train a model on feature-partitioned datasets, in which the devices share the same samples but each holds different features. This contrasts with horizontal FL, where devices hold different samples with the same features. Because each participant owns a unique sub-model of the global model, VFL requires full device participation, and the volume of intermediate outputs exchanged is proportional to the number of training samples, which makes communication the main bottleneck. The goal is to minimize an empirical risk F(w) made of a sample-wise loss and regularization terms, with the global model w split into local sub-models w_k. Training is cyclic. First, each device computes a local prediction g_k from its own features. Second, the central server aggregates these predictions into a final prediction through a non-linear transformation σ(·). Third, the server computes an auxiliary function G_i and broadcasts it back. Fourth, each device combines G_i with the local gradient of g_k to update its sub-model by gradient descent. When this exchange runs over a wireless Cloud-RAN, uplink and downlink noise corrupt the aggregated prediction and the auxiliary function respectively. Under α-strong convexity and β-smoothness of the objective, a convergence theorem bounds the expected optimality gap to the optimum by a term B(T) that depends on the contraction rate ρ and a weighted sum of the uplink and downlink noise variances. Communication noise therefore leaves a non-zero optimality gap, which motivates minimizing B(T) through joint design of transceivers and fronthaul quantization under power and capacity constraints. In experiments with a regularized logistic regression model on Fashion-MNIST across 49 devices and N=8 edge servers within a 500 m radius, the optimality gap converged linearly, and higher downlink power constraints gave smaller gaps through better SNR.

### Over-the-Air Computation (AirComp)
<!-- sources: vertical_federated_learning_over_cloud-ran_convergence_analysis_and_system_optimization | gen: 6372c7c89abf -->
Over-the-Air Computation (AirComp) aggregates data by exploiting waveform superposition in the wireless medium: devices transmit their values simultaneously over the same resources, and the multiple-access channel itself computes the sum. This reduces aggregation latency and radio resource consumption compared with orthogonal transmission, whose cost grows with the number of devices. In vertical federated learning over Cloud-RAN, K single-antenna devices send their local predictions concurrently by AirComp to N edge servers with M antennas each. The edge servers quantize the superposed signals to fit the fronthaul capacity and forward them to a central server, which applies a receive beamforming vector m and a power control factor η to estimate the aggregated signal. In the downlink, the central server computes the auxiliary result G_i, beamforms it with vectors u_n to the edge servers over quantized fronthaul links, and the edge servers broadcast it, after which each device scales the received signal by a receive scalar b_DL,k to estimate G_i. To remove channel fading distortion, transmit and receive scalars are designed by zero-forcing precoding to invert the channel. This yields unbiased estimates of the aggregated signals under the assumption of small noise amplitude, but effective uplink noise n_UL and downlink noise n_DL,k still persist. These noises enter the convergence bound as weighted variances σ²_UL and σ²_DL,k and leave a non-zero optimality gap in learning. AirComp aggregation error and fronthaul quantization error are the two error sources addressed by joint optimization of beamforming and quantization, with a min-max formulation that minimizes the worst-case noise across devices.

## ML Lifecycle, Trust and Sustainability Challenges

### MLOps for O-RAN Model Lifecycle Automation
<!-- sources: deep_learning_for_b5g_open_radio_access_network_evolution_survey_case_studies_and_challenges, machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial | gen: 4f90218b288d -->
RAN data profiles evolve constantly (data drift), so statically trained models degrade over time, and MLOps is proposed to unify the development and operation of ML systems in O-RAN. This reflects a broader obstacle to moving ML from research to production, particularly in access networks: adaptability to dynamic environments. Concept drift occurs when the distribution of deployment data differs from the training data, rendering models inaccurate. Proposed remedies include online learning, federated learning, and computationally efficient models that allow retraining with high responsivity. Two maturity levels are described for MLOps. At Level 1, the manual process, data preparation, training, and deployment are performed by hand; this is considered insufficient for B5G networks because it cannot react quickly to changes in the environment. At Level 2, the automated process, pipelines provide continuous training (CT) and continuous delivery (CD), triggering retraining when performance degrades or new data becomes available. Data and model validation steps run before an updated model is deployed: schema skews (incorrect or unexpected features) and value skews (changed statistical patterns in the data) are checked. Metadata management records execution history, which supports debugging and rollback to earlier models. In the O-RAN context, this lifecycle complements the split between offline training in the non-RT RIC and deployment of models as xApps in the near-RT RIC.

### Concept Drift and Data Drift Detection
<!-- sources: machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial | gen: 9e387f26aec4 -->
Concept drift arises when the distribution of data seen in deployment differs from that of the training data, making a trained model inaccurate; this is a key adaptability obstacle for ML in dynamic network environments. Two types are distinguished. Virtual (data) drift is a change in the input distribution that does not affect the decision boundary. Real concept drift changes the actual functional relationship between inputs and outputs, shifting the decision boundary, and therefore requires model adaptation. Detection methods include monitoring the error rate, using distance-based distributions of errors, and, in a federated learning setup, comparing model parameter updates across participants. Adaptation strategies include online learning, which combines offline pre-training with continuous real-time updates but needs robust drift detection, federated learning, and computationally efficient models that allow high-responsivity retraining.

### Explainable AI (XAI) for Optical Networks
<!-- sources: machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial | gen: b0a30fef89d0 -->
The black-box nature of ML is problematic for high-reliability networks managed by domain experts, who need to trust model outputs. Explainable AI (XAI) aims to provide interpretable outputs, assessed through metrics such as stability, robustness, and reproducibility. Methods are split into model-agnostic ones, such as SHAP and LIME, which can be applied post hoc to any model, and model-specific ones, such as GRAD-CAM for deep neural networks. In optical networks, XAI has been used to identify the features relevant to Quality-of-Transmission (QoT) estimation and to localize network failures.

### Generative Adversarial Networks and Active Learning for Data Scarcity
<!-- sources: machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial | gen: 3b5cd5ec9d9d -->
Collecting and labeling high-quality data from deployed optical networks is expensive and technically difficult, so representative data are scarce. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) address this with two competing networks, a generator and a discriminator, that synthesize realistic data. In optical networks they are used for network traffic synthesis, for modeling fiber channels with impairments such as chromatic dispersion, and for security, through anomaly detection and defense against adversarial attacks. Active learning reduces the need for large labeled datasets by letting the model proactively query an oracle for labels on the most informative samples. It has been applied to improve channel gain prediction in EDFAs and SNR estimation in flexible grid networks. Online learning and transfer learning are listed as further mitigations.

### Sustainable AI and TinyML
<!-- sources: machine_learning_enhanced_next-generation_optical_access_networkschallenges_and_emerging_solutions_invited_tutorial | gen: 1b65c004a47c -->
The high computational complexity of ML leads to significant energy use and carbon emissions, making environmental impact a recognized challenge for deployment in networks. Sustainable AI addresses this through energy-efficient hardware and computationally efficient models. TinyML, which runs computation on microcontrollers, consumes orders of magnitude less power than GPUs, and by performing inference at the edge it also improves privacy since raw data need not leave the device. This is relevant to access networks, where cost sensitivity and limited computational resources at the edge constrain ML deployment.

### Scalability, Convergence, Fairness and Privacy Challenges of DRL in RAN
<!-- sources: resource_allocation_in_5g_cloudran_using_deep_reinforcement_learning_algorithms_a_review | gen: e08e43126f95 -->
Applying DRL to C-RAN resource allocation faces four groups of challenges, each with proposed remedies. Scalability is hindered by the large volume of training data required, by problem complexity that grows with network size, and by the difficulty of designing reward functions for conflicting objectives; proposed solutions are transfer learning with pre-trained models, regularization to prevent overfitting, and hierarchical learning that decomposes a complex problem into smaller sub-problems. Convergence is complicated by high-dimensional state representations and the large number of hyperparameters, and the non-stationarity of wireless environments (user mobility, fading) makes it hard for policies to stabilize; suggested remedies are ensemble learning and the integration of domain knowledge into the models. Fairness can suffer because DRL may produce unfair outcomes when training data is biased or when the black-box nature of neural networks obscures decision logic; solutions include explicit fairness constraints in the objective function, preprocessing of training data to reduce bias, and Explainable AI (XAI). Security and privacy concerns arise because the large datasets needed expose sensitive user information and network patterns. Mitigations include privacy-preserving algorithms (federated learning, differential privacy, and secure multi-party computation), data anonymization that obfuscates personally identifiable information, secure protocols with encryption and authentication in the C-RAN infrastructure, and regulatory frameworks governing data ownership and consent. Open research directions tied to these challenges include more realistic modeling of network dynamics, reward design that trades spectral efficiency against energy, new exploration-exploitation strategies, real-time feasibility studies on actual hardware, and Multi-Agent RL for collaborative optimization.
